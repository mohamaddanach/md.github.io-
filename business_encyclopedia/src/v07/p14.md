# Organisations: Structures, Departments, Roles and Reporting Lines

:::obj In this chapter
Organisational structures, how departments and roles fit together, and the trade-offs between centralisation and decentralisation.
:::

## Structures

| Structure | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | Organised by function (sales, operations, finance) | Expertise, efficiency | Silos; slow cross-functional decisions | Small and mid-size single-business companies |
| Divisional (product / geographic / customer) | Divisions with own functions | Accountability for results; market focus | Duplication; inconsistent standards | Multi-business or multi-country groups |
| Matrix | Dual reporting (e.g., product and country) | Balances global and local | Confusion, conflict, slow decisions | Multinationals |
| Holding / group | Parent with semi-autonomous subsidiaries | Flexibility, separate legal risk | Weak integration | Conglomerates, family groups |
| Network / flat / team-based | Small hierarchies, project teams | Speed, innovation | Role ambiguity at scale | Startups, consultancies |

## Reporting lines and spans of control

A **solid line** indicates direct authority (hiring, objectives, appraisal); a **dotted line** indicates functional guidance (e.g., a country finance manager reporting directly to the country MD and dotted to the group CFO). Spans of control (direct reports per manager) of roughly 5–10 are common; narrower spans add layers and cost; wider spans require experienced, autonomous teams.

## Centralisation vs decentralisation

| Centralise | Decentralise |
|---|---|
| Treasury, financing, legal, IT platforms, procurement of common categories, brand | Sales, customer service, local operations, local hiring |
| Benefits: scale, control, consistency | Benefits: speed, local knowledge, accountability |
| Risks: slow, out of touch | Risks: duplication, inconsistent controls |

**Shared service centres** centralise transactional processes (accounts payable, payroll, IT support) for efficiency; **centres of excellence** concentrate expertise.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A trading group operating in five countries has each country running its own treasury and bank relationships. What would you centralise, and why?
2. What problems arise when a sales manager reports to both a regional head and a product head with conflicting targets?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Centralise treasury (cash pooling, FX hedging, bank relationships and facility negotiation), key supplier negotiations, IT/ERP, and financial reporting standards — for lower funding costs, better visibility and control; keep sales and local operations decentralised.
2. Conflicting priorities, unclear decision rights, gaming of targets, slow decisions and frustration; resolve through clear decision rights (RACI), aligned targets, and an escalation mechanism.
:::

# Strategy: Mission, Vision, Planning, Analytical Tools and Competitive Advantage

:::obj In this chapter
How organisations define purpose and direction, the strategic planning process, the core analytical tools, sources of competitive advantage, and corporate-level strategy.
:::

## Mission, vision, values and objectives

| Element | Question | Example (fictional logistics company) |
|---|---|---|
| Mission | Why do we exist; what do we do for whom? | “We move our customers’ goods across the Gulf reliably and transparently.” |
| Vision | What do we want to become? | “The most trusted regional logistics partner for industrial importers by 2030.” |
| Values | How do we behave? | Integrity, reliability, safety, customer focus |
| Strategic objectives | What must we achieve? | Reach $150m revenue with 6% EBIT margin; 98% OTIF; enter two new markets |
| Strategic initiatives | How? | Customs digital platform; cold-chain capacity; acquisition in Oman |

## The strategic planning process

1. **Analyse** the external environment (PESTLE, Five Forces, market trends, competitors) and internal capabilities (resources, value chain, financials).
2. **Define** where to play (segments, geographies, products) and how to win (cost, differentiation, focus, ecosystem).
3. **Choose** initiatives and allocate capital; set objectives and KPIs.
4. **Plan** financially (long-range plan, budget) and organisationally.
5. **Execute** with governance (programme office, reviews).
6. **Review** and adapt (quarterly strategy reviews, annual refresh).

## Analytical tools

| Tool | Purpose | Key output | Common misuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| SWOT | Summarise internal strengths/weaknesses and external opportunities/threats | Strategic options (SO, WO, ST, WT) | Lists without choices |
| Porter’s Five Forces | Assess industry attractiveness | Where profits are captured; how to position | Treating as static |
| PESTLE | Scan macro environment | Key external drivers and risks | Generic statements |
| Value chain | Map activities and costs | Sources of cost or differentiation advantage | Ignoring linkages |
| Scenario planning | Prepare for multiple plausible futures | Robust strategies, signposts | Picking a “most likely” scenario only |
| BCG matrix (growth-share) | Portfolio of businesses | Invest, hold, harvest, divest | Over-simplification |
| Ansoff matrix | Growth options | Penetration, product/market development, diversification | Ignoring capabilities |
| Business Model Canvas | Describe how the business creates and captures value | Model coherence | Static description |

:::example Scenario planning for a Gulf logistics company (fictional)
Two key uncertainties: **regional trade growth** (high/low) and **digitalisation of customs and logistics** (fast/slow).

| | Fast digitalisation | Slow digitalisation |
|---|---|---|
| **High trade growth** | “Digital hubs”: platforms win; scale and technology matter | “Physical boom”: capacity (warehouses, trucks) is the bottleneck |
| **Low trade growth** | “Efficiency squeeze”: low-cost digital players take share | “Status quo struggle”: price competition among incumbents |

Robust moves across scenarios: invest in digital customs capability (pays off in two scenarios and protects in others), keep capacity expansion modular (leased warehouses), strengthen industrial key accounts. Signposts to monitor: customs single-window adoption, trade volumes through major ports, entry of platform competitors.
:::

## Competitive advantage

Porter’s **generic strategies**: cost leadership, differentiation, and focus (cost or differentiation in a niche). Sustained advantage comes from resources that are **valuable, rare, hard to imitate and organised to capture value** (VRIO): brands, licences, networks, proprietary data and technology, cost position from scale, relationships, culture. Part XXII explores moats, network effects and switching costs.

## Corporate strategy

At group level, strategy decides **which businesses to be in** and how to add value as a parent: portfolio choices (invest, acquire, divest), synergies across businesses (shared customers, procurement, technology), capital allocation, and governance of subsidiaries. A parent adds value only if businesses are worth more within the group than independently (the “better-off” test).

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Build a SWOT for a mid-sized pharmaceutical distributor in the Levant and derive two strategic options.
2. Apply the Five Forces to the cement industry in a country with five producers and high import tariffs.
3. Distinguish a vision from a strategy with an example.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Strengths: exclusive agencies with two multinationals, cold-chain capability; Weaknesses: dependence on two principals, receivables from public hospitals; Opportunities: growing private healthcare, regional expansion, generics; Threats: principals going direct, price controls, currency instability. Options: (SO) use cold-chain to win new biologics agencies; (WT) diversify principals and secure credit insurance or factoring for hospital receivables.
2. Rivalry: moderate (oligopoly, price discipline or price wars depending on demand); threat of entry: low (capital intensity, licences); substitutes: limited; buyer power: moderate (large contractors, government projects); supplier power: energy and limestone (possibly state-controlled energy prices); tariffs protect from imports → industry attractive while tariffs and demand last.
3. Vision: “Be the region’s most trusted logistics partner for industrial importers.” Strategy: “Win industrial importers in Saudi Arabia and the UAE by offering guaranteed 48-hour customs clearance through our digital platform and licensed brokers, while exiting low-margin general trucking.”
:::

# Performance Management: KPIs, OKRs, Budget Ownership, Management Reports and Decision-Making

:::obj In this chapter
How managers measure and manage performance with KPIs and OKRs, own budgets, read management reports, and make decisions with financial and operational information — including an executive-meeting scenario.
:::

## Designing KPIs

Good KPIs are **few**, **aligned** with strategy, **owned** by someone, **measurable** from reliable data, **comparable** over time, **actionable**, and balanced between **leading** (predictive: pipeline, OTIF, customer complaints) and **lagging** (outcome: revenue, margin) indicators. A **balanced scorecard** covers financial, customer, internal process, and learning/growth perspectives.

:::example KPI tree for return on capital employed (distributor)
ROCE = EBIT ÷ capital employed
- EBIT ← revenue (customers × volume × price) − COGS (purchase price, freight, duty) − opex (warehouse cost per unit, sales cost)
- Capital employed ← receivables (DSO), inventory (DIO), payables (DPO), fixed assets
Each branch has an owner: sales (volume, price, DSO with credit control), procurement (purchase price, DPO), supply chain (DIO, freight, warehouse cost), finance (capital structure).
:::

## OKRs

Objectives and key results set ambitious, time-bound goals (often quarterly). They complement KPIs: KPIs keep the business healthy; OKRs drive change. Good practice: 3–5 objectives, 2–4 key results each, measurable, mostly set bottom-up and aligned upward, reviewed regularly, not tied mechanically to bonuses.

## Budget ownership

A budget owner is accountable for a cost centre or profit centre: preparing the budget, approving spending within the delegation of authority, explaining variances, forecasting, and taking corrective actions. Good budget owners understand which costs they control, separate timing differences from real variances, and raise issues early.

## The management report

:::doc Monthly management report — Gulf Industrial Supplies (fictional), May
**1. Headline:** Revenue $8.4m (−4% vs budget, +9% y/y). Gross margin 21.8% (budget 23.0%). EBITDA $0.71m (budget $0.88m). Operating cash flow −$0.35m; net debt $14.2m (covenant headroom 18%).

**2. Performance vs budget (YTD, $m)**

| | Actual | Budget | Variance | Prior year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 41.2 | 42.5 | (1.3) | 37.6 |
| Gross profit | 9.12 | 9.78 | (0.66) | 8.46 |
| Gross margin | 22.1% | 23.0% | (0.9 pt) | 22.5% |
| Opex | (5.40) | (5.50) | 0.10 | (5.05) |
| EBITDA | 3.72 | 4.28 | (0.56) | 3.41 |
| DSO (days) | 82 | 70 | (12) | 74 |
| Inventory days | 96 | 85 | (11) | 90 |

**3. Explanations**
- *Revenue:* project segment −$1.8m (two contractor projects delayed to Q3); MRO segment +$0.5m.
- *Gross margin:* steel pipe purchase prices rose 6% in March; price increases to customers implemented only from May (timing, ~0.6 pt); higher freight (0.3 pt).
- *DSO:* two government-funded contractors paying at 120+ days ($1.6m overdue).
- *Inventory:* stock for delayed projects ($0.9m) held in warehouse.

**4. Forecast:** full-year EBITDA $9.6m (budget $10.4m). Risks: further contractor payment delays; opportunity: new framework agreement with a utility (+$2m revenue).

**5. Actions and decisions requested**
1. Approve credit-insurance extension to the two contractors or require payment security for new orders (decision: CFO/CEO).
2. Negotiate storage charges/partial billing with delayed-project customers (owner: sales director, by 15 June).
3. Implement monthly price-review mechanism for steel-linked products (owner: commercial director).
4. Discuss with bank a temporary covenant reset if DSO does not improve by August (owner: CFO).

*Training simulation.*
:::

## Making decisions with financial and operational information

A practical decision checklist:

1. **Define the decision and options** (including “do nothing”).
2. **Identify the relevant information:** incremental revenues, costs and cash flows; risks; strategic fit; non-financial factors.
3. **Quantify** (NPV, contribution, payback, effect on KPIs and covenants).
4. **Test sensitivity and downside.**
5. **Consider stakeholders and implementation capacity.**
6. **Decide, assign owners, define success metrics and review dates.**

:::case Executive meeting scenario — should we accept the low-margin contract?
**Setting:** Monthly executive committee of Gulf Industrial Supplies (fictional). Attendees: CEO, CFO, commercial director, operations director.

**Commercial director:** “We can win a 2-year framework with a petrochemical company: $9m a year, gross margin 14% versus our average 22%. It fills warehouse capacity and positions us with a strategic customer.”

**CFO:** “At 14% gross margin we earn $1.26m gross profit. Incremental costs: two staff and extra handling about $0.3m. That leaves $0.96m before financing. Payment terms are 90 days — about $2.2m more receivables plus $0.8m stock, so roughly $3m of working capital at 8%: $0.24m. Net about $0.72m a year, a return of ~24% on the extra capital. But our covenant headroom is only 18%: we would need the bank to increase our facility.”

**Operations director:** “Warehouse utilisation is 72%; this takes us to 86%. Beyond 90% service levels fall for everyone.”

**CEO:** “What is the downside?”

**CFO:** “If the customer pays at 150 days instead of 90, working capital rises by another $1.5m and headroom disappears. Customer credit quality is strong — rated BBB — so the risk is timing, not default.”

**Decision:** Approve, conditional on (1) bank agreement to a $3m facility increase before signing; (2) price-adjustment clause for steel-linked items; (3) payment terms capped at 90 days with late-payment interest, and receivables financing as a backup; (4) operations plan to keep utilisation below 90%. Owners and dates recorded in minutes.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. From the management report, separate timing issues from structural issues.
2. Propose three KPIs for the operations director of a 3PL warehouse and one leading indicator for each.
3. In the executive scenario, what single additional piece of information would most improve the decision?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Timing: delayed contractor projects (revenue moves to Q3), price increases implemented with a lag, inventory held for delayed projects. Structural (potentially): exposure to government-funded contractors paying slowly (DSO), steel price volatility without automatic pass-through, freight cost increases.
2. OTIF (leading: % orders released to picking on time); cost per order (leading: labour productivity, lines per hour); inventory accuracy (leading: cycle-count completion rate); safety incidents (leading: near-miss reports).
3. Examples: the customer’s actual payment history with other suppliers; the bank’s willingness to increase facilities; or the opportunity cost of warehouse capacity (alternative higher-margin uses).
:::

# Leading People and Projects: Hiring, Delegation, Stakeholders, Conflict, Meetings, Project and Change Management, Operational Excellence

:::obj In this chapter
The people and execution side of management: hiring, delegating, managing stakeholders and conflict, running meetings, managing projects and change, and improving operations.
:::

## Hiring

Define the role (outcomes expected in 12 months, competencies), source widely, use structured interviews with consistent questions and scoring, test skills with realistic tasks (e.g., a case for a financial analyst), check references, and onboard deliberately. Avoid hiring for “culture fit” as a cover for similarity; hire for values and complementary strengths. Comply with labour law and localisation requirements.

## Delegation

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Do exactly what I say |
| 2 | Research and report back; I decide |
| 3 | Recommend; I approve |
| 4 | Decide and inform me |
| 5 | Decide and act; no need to report unless issues |

Match the level to the person’s competence and the risk. Delegate outcomes, not just tasks; agree deadlines, resources and checkpoints; avoid taking the work back (“reverse delegation”).

## Stakeholder management

Map stakeholders by **power** and **interest**; understand their objectives and concerns; engage early, especially those who can block; tailor communication; build coalitions; keep promises. For a CFO proposal, key stakeholders typically include the CEO, board members, business unit heads, the bank, and auditors.

## Conflict resolution

Approaches (Thomas-Kilmann model): competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating. In business, aim to collaborate on important issues — separate people from problems, focus on interests, use data, and agree criteria for decisions. Escalate when needed, with facts and options rather than complaints.

## Effective meetings

- Purpose and decision needed stated in advance; agenda with time allocations; pre-reads circulated 48 hours before.
- Right participants (decision-makers present; no passive audiences).
- Start with the decision or conclusion; discuss options and risks; decide.
- Minutes with decisions, action items, owners and deadlines; follow up.

## Executive reporting

Senior executives need the answer first (the “pyramid principle”): the conclusion, supported by 3–5 key arguments with data, followed by details in appendices. One page for the summary; charts with clear titles stating the message (“Margins fell 2 points due to freight”), not just the topic (“Margin analysis”).

## Project management

| Phase | Key activities | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| Initiation | Business case, objectives, sponsor, scope | Project charter |
| Planning | Work breakdown, schedule, budget, resources, risks, RACI | Gantt chart, critical path, risk log |
| Execution | Deliver work, manage team and suppliers | Status meetings |
| Monitoring and control | Track progress, cost, scope changes, quality | Earned value, change control, RAG status |
| Closure | Acceptance, handover, lessons learned | Closure report |

Methods: **waterfall** (sequential, suits well-defined projects such as construction or ERP rollouts with fixed scope) and **agile** (iterative sprints, suits software and uncertain requirements). The **triple constraint**: scope, time and cost (with quality) trade off against each other.

:::formula Earned value basics
Planned value (PV) = budgeted cost of work scheduled; Earned value (EV) = budgeted cost of work performed; Actual cost (AC).
Cost variance CV = EV − AC; Schedule variance SV = EV − PV; CPI = EV ÷ AC; SPI = EV ÷ PV
Estimate at completion (EAC) ≈ Budget at completion ÷ CPI
:::

Example: budget $1.0m; at month 4, PV $400k, EV $320k, AC $380k → CPI 0.84, SPI 0.80 → EAC ≈ $1.19m; the project is over budget and behind schedule.

## Change management

Organisational change fails more often from people issues than technical ones. Kotter’s steps (urgency, guiding coalition, vision, communication, removing obstacles, short-term wins, consolidating, anchoring in culture) and the ADKAR model (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement) provide structure. Practical tips: explain why, involve the people affected, train, measure adoption, celebrate early wins, and align incentives.

## Operational excellence

Lean (eliminate waste: overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects, unused talent), Six Sigma (reduce variation with DMAIC: define, measure, analyse, improve, control), Kaizen (continuous small improvements), standard work, visual management, root-cause analysis (5 Whys, fishbone diagrams), and automation of repetitive processes.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A project has budget $600k; PV $300k; EV $270k; AC $330k. Compute CPI, SPI and EAC and comment.
2. Use “5 Whys” to explore why invoices are issued late in a distribution company.
3. Write the first three lines of an executive summary recommending a new warehouse management system.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. CPI = 270 ÷ 330 = **0.82**; SPI = 270 ÷ 300 = **0.90**; EAC ≈ 600 ÷ 0.82 = **$733k** — over budget and behind schedule; review scope and resources now.
2. Invoices are late → because delivery confirmations reach finance late → because drivers return paper proofs of delivery weekly → because there is no electronic POD → because the TMS was never configured for mobile → root cause: missing electronic proof-of-delivery process; fix with mobile POD integrated with invoicing.
3. “We recommend implementing a warehouse management system at a cost of $420k, delivering $310k annual savings and a 1.4-year payback. It will raise picking accuracy from 97.5% to 99.5% and free 12 days of inventory ($1.1m cash). Implementation takes 6 months with a pilot at the Dammam warehouse.”
:::

# Business Transformation, M&A and Post-Merger Integration

:::obj In this chapter
How companies plan and execute transformations, and how acquisitions are integrated after closing — with a structured integration plan and decision exercises.
:::

## Business transformation

A transformation is a fundamental change in how a company operates — digital transformation, cost transformation, business-model change, turnaround. Success factors: compelling case for change, committed leadership, clear target state and roadmap, strong programme management office (PMO), quantified value tracking, capability building, and communication. Typical pitfalls: too many initiatives, underestimating IT and data work, benefits not tracked, change fatigue.

## Post-merger integration (PMI)

| Phase | Activities |
|---|---|
| Pre-signing | Integration hypotheses during due diligence; synergy estimates; key risks (people, customers, systems) |
| Signing to closing | Integration management office (IMO); day-1 plan; leadership appointments; communication plan; clean team for sensitive data (respecting competition law — no gun-jumping) |
| Day 1 | Legal and operational continuity: payments, payroll, customer service, signage, communications |
| First 100 days | Organisation design, quick wins, synergy capture plans, retention of key people and customers, culture assessment |
| 6–24 months | Systems integration, process harmonisation, facility consolidation, full synergy delivery, value tracking |

:::warn Why integrations fail
- Overestimated synergies and underestimated integration costs.
- Loss of key customers and staff due to uncertainty.
- Culture clash (decision-making styles, incentives).
- Distraction from the core business.
- Systems integration delays.
- No clear accountability for synergy targets.
:::

:::case Integration decision exercise
**Situation (fictional):** Gulf Industrial Supplies acquires a competitor in Oman for $24m (EV 8× EBITDA of $3m). Promised synergies: procurement savings $0.8m, closing one warehouse $0.4m, cross-selling $0.5m gross profit by year 3. Integration costs: $1.2m. Three months after closing: two key Omani salespeople have resigned; a top customer (12% of the target’s revenue) has paused orders; ERP migration is planned for month 6.

**Questions for the executive team:**
1. What are the immediate priorities?
2. Should ERP migration be postponed?
3. How should synergies be reported to the board?

**Model answers:**
1. Customer retention (CEO-level visit to the paused customer, service guarantees), people retention (retention bonuses for remaining key sales staff, clear roles), stabilise operations before further change.
2. Probably yes — delay to after stabilisation (e.g., month 9–12) unless the legacy system is unsupported; a failed migration during customer-retention efforts would compound risks.
3. Track each synergy separately with owners, baseline and realised amounts; report net of integration costs and dis-synergies (lost customers, departures); present revised expectations honestly with recovery actions.
:::

:::summary Part XIV summary
- Structure follows strategy; decide what to centralise and what to keep close to customers.
- Strategy chooses where to play and how to win; tools help only if they lead to choices.
- KPIs and OKRs focus attention; management reports explain variances and request decisions.
- Decisions should use incremental cash flows, downside analysis and implementation capacity.
- People leadership — hiring, delegation, stakeholders, conflict, meetings — determines execution.
- Projects, change and integrations succeed through governance, ownership, communication and value tracking.
:::
