Law defines the rules within which business operates: how companies are formed and governed, how promises become enforceable contracts, how risks are allocated between parties, how disputes are resolved, what happens when a business cannot pay its debts, and which conduct is prohibited. A business professional does not need to be a lawyer, but must recognise legal issues early, read contracts intelligently, and know when to involve a qualified lawyer.

:::framework A note on jurisdiction
Law is national (and sometimes sub-national). Common-law systems (e.g., England and Wales, the US, Singapore, and the common-law courts of financial centres such as the DIFC and ADGM in the UAE) and civil-law systems (e.g., France, Egypt, Lebanon, and the onshore law of most Middle Eastern countries, often influenced by Islamic law principles) can treat the same clause differently. This part explains **general concepts** and common practice in international business. It does not state the law of any jurisdiction. Before signing or enforcing contracts, forming companies, terminating agents, or dealing with insolvency, obtain advice from a lawyer qualified in the relevant jurisdiction.
:::

# Business Entities and Corporate Governance

:::obj In this chapter
Forms of business organisation, the roles of shareholders, directors and officers, governance principles and shareholder agreements.
:::

## Business entities

| Entity | Separate legal person? | Owners’ liability | Typical use | Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship (sole establishment) | No | Unlimited | Small businesses, professionals | Simple; owner bears all risk |
| General partnership | Varies by jurisdiction | Unlimited, joint and several | Professional firms, family ventures | Partners bound by each other’s acts |
| Limited partnership | Varies | General partner unlimited; limited partners limited | Investment funds | LPs must not manage |
| Limited liability partnership (LLP) | Yes (in many jurisdictions) | Limited | Professional services | Availability varies |
| Limited liability company (LLC) | Yes | Limited to capital contributed | SMEs, joint ventures, subsidiaries | Most common form in the Middle East for private businesses |
| Joint-stock / public company (corporation, PJSC/PLC) | Yes | Limited | Large companies, listed companies | Stricter governance and disclosure |
| Branch of a foreign company | No (part of parent) | Parent liable | Market presence | Licensing; parent exposure |
| Free-zone entity | Yes | Limited | Trade, services in free zones | Rules of each free zone |
| Holding company / SPV | Yes | Limited | Group structures, project finance | Purpose restrictions |
| Non-profit / foundation | Yes | — | Charities, family wealth | Specific regimes |

Choice criteria: liability protection, ownership restrictions (some jurisdictions historically required local partners for certain activities — many have liberalised; verify), capital requirements, tax, governance burden, ability to raise capital, exit flexibility, and licensing.

## Shareholders, directors and officers

| Role | Rights and powers | Duties |
|---|---|---|
| Shareholders (members) | Vote on major matters (appointing directors, amendments, capital changes, mergers, dissolution, dividends where required); receive dividends; information rights | Generally none toward the company (except under agreements); controlling shareholders may owe duties not to oppress minorities in some systems |
| Directors (board) | Manage or supervise the company’s business; appoint officers; approve strategy, budgets, major transactions | Fiduciary duties (loyalty, acting in the company’s interest, avoiding conflicts), care and skill, compliance with law and constitution; duties toward creditors near insolvency |
| Officers / executive management (CEO, CFO…) | Day-to-day management under delegated authority | Duties to the company; act within authority |
| Company secretary | Statutory compliance, board administration | — |
| Auditors | Independent audit of statements | Professional standards |

## Corporate governance

The **G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance** (revised 2023) cover shareholder rights and equitable treatment, the role of institutional investors and stock markets, disclosure and transparency, responsibilities of the board, and sustainability and resilience. Many countries have national governance codes (often “comply or explain” for listed companies).

Key governance mechanisms:

- Board composition: independent directors, separation of chair and CEO roles, relevant skills.
- Committees: audit, risk, nomination, remuneration.
- Delegation of authority matrix and reserved matters.
- Related-party transaction approval (conflicted directors excluded).
- Internal audit and external audit independence.
- Disclosure and investor relations.
- Remuneration linked to long-term performance.
- Family governance in family businesses: family constitution, family council, rules on family employment and share transfers.

## Shareholder agreements

A shareholder agreement (SHA) is a private contract among shareholders (and often the company) supplementing the constitutional documents.

| Clause | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Board composition and appointment rights | Each party’s representation |
| Reserved matters (veto rights) | Decisions requiring investor or supermajority approval (budget, new debt, M&A, issuing shares, related-party deals, changing business) |
| Pre-emption rights | Existing shareholders’ right to subscribe to new shares pro rata |
| Transfer restrictions; right of first refusal (ROFR) | Control who can become a shareholder |
| Tag-along | Minority can sell alongside a majority seller on the same terms |
| Drag-along | Majority can force minority to sell to a buyer of 100% |
| Deadlock resolution | Escalation, mediation, buy-sell (“Russian roulette”, “Texas shoot-out”) mechanisms |
| Non-compete and non-solicit | Protect the business (enforceability varies) |
| Information rights | Regular financial reporting |
| Dividend policy | Expectations on distributions |
| Exit provisions | IPO, sale, put/call options, valuation mechanisms |
| Dispute resolution | Arbitration |

:::warn Constitution vs shareholder agreement
In many jurisdictions, if the shareholder agreement conflicts with the company’s articles, third parties and registries follow the articles. Important rights (vetoes, transfer restrictions) should be reflected in the articles where the law allows, or enforcement may be difficult.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Two friends start a trading business. One contributes capital, the other works full-time. Which entity and which three shareholder-agreement clauses would you recommend?
2. A minority investor with 25% wants protection against the majority selling assets cheaply to a related company. Which governance mechanisms help?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. An LLC (limited liability, simple governance). Clauses: vesting/leaver provisions for the working partner’s shares (good/bad leaver), reserved matters and deadlock mechanism, transfer restrictions with ROFR and tag/drag rights; also remuneration and dividend policy.
2. Reserved matter requiring investor consent for asset disposals and related-party transactions; independent director or board seat; information and audit rights; related-party approval procedure excluding conflicted directors; arbitration clause; put option if breached.
:::

# Contract Fundamentals and Key Clauses

:::obj In this chapter
How contracts are formed, how they are structured, and how key clauses allocate business risk — with fictional clause extracts analysed from both sides.
:::

## Formation

General elements (expressed differently across legal systems): **offer** and **acceptance** (consent), **capacity** and **authority** of signatories, a lawful object and cause, **certainty** of essential terms, and (in common law) **consideration** and intention to create legal relations. Formalities (writing, notarisation, registration, language — e.g., some jurisdictions require Arabic versions for certain contracts or court proceedings) depend on law and contract type.

:::warn Battle of the forms
A buyer sends a PO with its terms; the seller confirms with its own terms; goods are shipped. Whose terms govern? Rules differ by jurisdiction (e.g., “last shot” vs “knock-out” approaches). Agree terms explicitly in a signed contract or framework agreement for important relationships.
:::

## Structure of a commercial contract

1. Parties and recitals (background).
2. Definitions and interpretation.
3. Main obligations (supply, services, payment).
4. Conditions precedent; term and commencement.
5. Price, payment, taxes.
6. Delivery, acceptance, title and risk.
7. Warranties and representations.
8. Indemnities; limitation of liability; insurance.
9. Intellectual property and confidentiality.
10. Force majeure; hardship.
11. Term and termination; consequences of termination.
12. Compliance (anti-bribery, sanctions, data protection).
13. Assignment and subcontracting.
14. Notices.
15. Governing law and dispute resolution.
16. Boilerplate: entire agreement, amendments, waiver, severability, counterparts.

## Key clauses with fictional extracts

### Representations and warranties

:::doc Clause extract A (training)
“The Seller warrants that the Goods shall (a) conform to the Specifications in Schedule 1, (b) be free from defects in materials and workmanship, and (c) be fit for the purpose of [use in cold-storage facilities in ambient temperatures up to 50°C], for a period of 18 months from delivery (the ‘Warranty Period’). The Buyer’s sole remedy for breach of this warranty shall be repair or replacement of the defective Goods at the Seller’s option.”
:::

**Analysis:** Fitness for purpose (c) is a strong warranty for the buyer — the seller must ensure suitability for 50°C conditions. But the “sole remedy” sentence limits the buyer to repair/replacement: no damages for downtime or spoiled stock. A buyer would negotiate: refund if repair fails within a set time, plus liability for direct losses up to a cap; a seller would keep the sole-remedy language and exclude consequential loss.

### Indemnities

:::doc Clause extract B (training)
“The Supplier shall indemnify and hold harmless the Customer from and against all losses, liabilities, costs and expenses (including legal fees) arising out of or in connection with any claim that the Software infringes the intellectual property rights of any third party.”
:::

**Analysis:** Common and reasonable for IP infringement. Supplier would add: conditions (prompt notice, supplier controls defence, customer cooperation), exclusions (modifications by the customer, combinations with third-party products), remedies (modify, replace, or refund), and possibly a cap. “Arising out of or in connection with” is broad; “arising from” is narrower.

### Limitation of liability

:::doc Clause extract C (training)
“Neither party shall be liable for any indirect, special or consequential loss, or for any loss of profit, revenue or business, howsoever arising. Each party’s total liability under this Agreement shall not exceed the total fees paid in the 12 months preceding the claim. Nothing in this Agreement limits liability for fraud, death or personal injury caused by negligence, or any liability that cannot be limited by law.”
:::

**Analysis:** Standard structure: exclusion of loss types + cap + carve-outs. Business questions: Is 12 months of fees proportionate to the potential harm (e.g., a data breach)? Should confidentiality, data-protection and IP-indemnity obligations be carved out or subject to a higher “super-cap”? Note: excluding “loss of profit” may exclude the customer’s main loss. Enforceability of exclusions depends on governing law (some laws restrict limitation for gross negligence or wilful misconduct).

### Conditions precedent and conditions subsequent

:::doc Clause extract D (training)
“The obligations of the Buyer to purchase the Shares are conditional upon: (i) the approval of the competition authority; (ii) the delivery of audited accounts for FY2025 showing EBITDA of not less than USD 12m; (iii) no Material Adverse Change having occurred. If the Conditions are not satisfied or waived by the Long-Stop Date (30 September), either party may terminate.”
:::

**Analysis:** Conditions precedent protect the buyer between signing and closing. (ii) gives the buyer a walk-away right linked to a financial threshold; (iii) MAC definitions are heavily negotiated (what counts as material; carve-outs for general market conditions). The long-stop date limits uncertainty for the seller. A **condition subsequent** would instead end or modify an obligation after it has begun (e.g., a licence that terminates if a permit is revoked).

### Force majeure

:::doc Clause extract E (training)
“Neither party shall be liable for delay or failure to perform caused by events beyond its reasonable control, including acts of God, war, terrorism, epidemics, governmental action, strikes (other than of its own employees), and failures of suppliers or subcontractors. The affected party shall notify the other within 7 days and use reasonable endeavours to mitigate. If the event continues for more than 90 days, either party may terminate on written notice.”
:::

**Analysis:** “Failures of suppliers” is very favourable to the seller (it can shift ordinary supply-chain risk onto the buyer); a buyer would delete it or limit it to suppliers themselves affected by force majeure. Note: payment obligations are often excluded from force majeure. Notice and mitigation requirements are conditions to relief — missing the 7-day notice can lose protection.

### Termination

:::doc Clause extract F (training)
“Either party may terminate this Agreement immediately by written notice if the other party (a) commits a material breach which, if remediable, is not remedied within 30 days of written notice, or (b) becomes insolvent or enters any insolvency proceeding. The Supplier may terminate for convenience on 60 days’ notice.”
:::

**Analysis:** Termination for convenience only for the supplier creates asymmetry — the customer may lose a critical service with short notice. Customer would seek mutual convenience rights or a longer notice period, transition assistance and data return. Insolvency termination clauses may be restricted by some insolvency laws (“ipso facto” restrictions).

### Dispute resolution

:::doc Clause extract G (training)
“This Agreement shall be governed by the laws of England and Wales. Any dispute arising out of or in connection with this Agreement shall be referred to and finally resolved by arbitration under the Rules of the [named arbitral institution], which Rules are deemed incorporated by reference. The seat of arbitration shall be London. The tribunal shall consist of one arbitrator. The language of the arbitration shall be English.”
:::

**Analysis:** Clear and enforceable model structure: governing law, institution, seat, number of arbitrators, language. Check that the counterparty’s assets are in countries party to the New York Convention, and whether local mandatory laws (e.g., for agency or real estate) give local courts exclusive jurisdiction.

## Reading a contract like a business professional

| Question | Clauses to check |
|---|---|
| What exactly must each party do? | Scope, specifications, service levels, deliverables |
| When and how do we get paid / must we pay? | Price, payment, invoicing, set-off, late interest |
| What happens if they fail? | Remedies, LDs, service credits, termination |
| What happens if we fail? | Same, from our side; caps; exclusions |
| What if something outside our control happens? | Force majeure, hardship, change in law |
| How long are we bound? | Term, renewal, termination for convenience |
| What do we owe after it ends? | Survival, non-compete, transition, confidentiality |
| Who decides disputes and where? | Governing law, jurisdiction/arbitration |
| What hidden obligations exist? | Exclusivity, MFN, minimum volumes, audit rights, insurance requirements |

لا يحتاج المهني إلى أن يكون محامياً، لكن يجب أن يقرأ العقد ليجيب عن أسئلة تجارية: ما التزاماتنا بدقة؟ متى نقبض ومتى ندفع؟ ماذا يحدث إذا أخلّ الطرف الآخر أو أخللنا نحن؟ ما حدود المسؤولية والتعويضات؟ كيف ينتهي العقد؟ وأين تُحل النزاعات؟ كما يجب التنبه إلى أن البنود نفسها قد تُفسَّر بشكل مختلف في الأنظمة القانونية المختلفة، ولذلك تلزم استشارة محامٍ مختص قبل التوقيع على العقود المهمة.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Rewrite Clause E from the buyer’s perspective (two changes).
2. Your company provides IT services; a client asks for unlimited liability for data breaches. Propose a balanced position.
3. Identify the risk in this clause: “Prices are subject to change without notice.”
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Remove “failures of suppliers or subcontractors” (or limit to cases where the supplier is itself prevented by a force majeure event and no alternative source is reasonably available); require notice within 3 days with evidence; allow the buyer to source elsewhere during the event without liability; termination after 30 days for time-critical supply.
2. A separate “super-cap” for data-protection breaches (e.g., 2–3× annual fees or a fixed amount aligned with cyber insurance limits), carve-out of fines that are uninsurable by law, obligations to maintain security standards and insurance, and unlimited liability only for wilful misconduct/fraud.
3. The buyer has no price certainty; it may be unenforceable or lead to disputes; the buyer should require fixed prices for confirmed orders and a notice period with a defined adjustment mechanism (indices) for future orders.
:::

# Commercial Agreements and Intellectual Property

:::obj In this chapter
The main commercial agreements — purchase and sale, distribution, agency, NDA, services, licensing, employment and shareholder agreements — their key issues, and the basics of intellectual property.
:::

## Agreement guide

| Agreement | Purpose | Key clauses and issues |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase / sale agreement (goods) | Sale of goods, often recurring | Specifications, quantity, price and adjustment, Incoterms, delivery, inspection and acceptance, title and risk, warranties, LDs, payment security, FM, law |
| Sale and purchase agreement (SPA, shares or business) | Acquisition of a company or business | Price and adjustments, CPs, warranties and indemnities, disclosure, limitations, restrictive covenants, completion mechanics |
| Distribution agreement | Distributor buys and resells in a territory | Territory and exclusivity, products, minimum targets, pricing, marketing, IP use, stock and returns, competition-law compliance, termination and compensation (local law) |
| Agency agreement | Agent promotes/negotiates for principal | Authority, commission, territory, exclusivity, expenses, compliance, termination indemnity (some laws), registration requirements in some countries |
| Non-disclosure agreement (NDA) | Protect confidential information | Definition, permitted use, exceptions, duration, return/destruction, remedies; mutual vs one-way; non-circumvention (brokerage) |
| Service agreement / MSA + SOW | Services over time | Scope and service levels, change control, acceptance, fees, IP in deliverables, data protection, liability, exit assistance |
| Licensing agreement | Use of IP (technology, brand, content) | Scope (field, territory, exclusivity), royalties and audits, quality control (trademarks), improvements, sub-licensing, term |
| Employment contract | Employment relationship | Role, pay and benefits, working hours, probation, confidentiality, IP assignment, restrictive covenants, termination and end-of-service benefits (heavily regulated by labour law) |
| Shareholder agreement | Relations among shareholders | See previous chapter |
| Joint venture agreement | Joint business | Contributions, governance, deadlock, exit, non-compete |
| Loan / facility agreement | Debt financing | Facilities, conditions, representations, covenants, events of default (Part VI) |
| Franchise agreement | Use of a business system | Fees, standards, territory, training, supply obligations, termination (Part XIII) |

:::doc NDA essentials (training checklist)
- **Parties and direction:** one-way (only one party discloses) or mutual.
- **Purpose:** information may be used only to evaluate [the potential transaction].
- **Definition of confidential information:** written/oral, marked or reasonably understood as confidential.
- **Exclusions:** already public, already known, independently developed, lawfully received from a third party, legally required disclosure (with notice).
- **Permitted disclosures:** employees and advisers who need to know, bound by confidentiality.
- **Duration:** e.g., 2–5 years; trade secrets possibly longer.
- **Return/destruction** on request.
- **No licence, no obligation to proceed.**
- **Non-solicitation / non-circumvention** where relevant (enforceability varies).
- **Remedies:** injunctive relief; governing law and jurisdiction.
:::

## Intellectual property basics

| IP right | Protects | How obtained | Typical duration | Business points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Patent | Inventions (new, inventive, industrially applicable) | Application and examination, per country/region | Usually 20 years from filing | Territorial; disclose to obtain; enforcement costly |
| Trademark | Brands (names, logos, sometimes colours/shapes) | Registration per country/class (also some rights from use) | Renewable (often 10-year terms) | Register in every key market before entry; watch for squatters |
| Copyright | Original works (software code, texts, designs) | Arises automatically (registration optional in some countries) | Long (often life + 50–70 years) | Software ownership must be assigned in contracts with developers/contractors |
| Industrial design | Appearance of products | Registration | Limited terms (e.g., 10–25 years) | Product designs, packaging |
| Trade secrets / know-how | Confidential valuable information | Reasonable secrecy measures | As long as secret | NDAs, access controls, employee agreements |
| Domain names | Internet addresses | Registration | Renewable | Part of brand protection |

:::warn Common IP mistakes in business
- Not registering trademarks in export markets before appointing distributors (who may register them first).
- Software developed by contractors without written IP assignment — the contractor may own it.
- Sharing trade secrets without NDAs or need-to-know controls.
- Assuming a licence to use includes the right to modify or sub-license.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A distributor asks to register your brand in its own name “to protect it locally”. How do you respond?
2. List four key differences between an agency and a distribution agreement from the principal’s perspective.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Decline; register the trademark in your company’s name; grant the distributor a limited, non-transferable licence to use it during the agreement; include obligations to assist in enforcement and to cease use on termination.
2. Agency: principal owns the goods and sets prices, bears credit risk on customers, pays commission, may face termination indemnities and agency-registration rules. Distribution: distributor buys and owns stock, sets resale prices (competition law limits resale price control), bears customer credit risk, earns margin; principal has less control over pricing and customer data.
:::

# Disputes, Insolvency and Compliance

:::obj In this chapter
Dispute resolution options, insolvency and bankruptcy concepts, and key compliance areas: regulatory compliance, conflicts of interest, anti-bribery and corruption, sanctions, and data protection.
:::

## Dispute resolution options

| Method | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negotiation | Parties resolve directly | Fast, cheap, preserves relationship | Depends on goodwill |
| Escalation clause | Managers, then executives, attempt resolution within set periods | Structured | Can delay |
| Mediation / conciliation | Neutral facilitator helps parties agree | Confidential, flexible | Non-binding unless settled |
| Expert determination | Expert decides technical/valuation issues | Fast, specialist | Limited review |
| Adjudication / dispute boards | Common in construction; interim binding decisions | Keeps projects moving | Temporary finality |
| Arbitration | Private tribunal renders binding award | Neutral, confidential, enforceable internationally (New York Convention) | Costly; limited appeal |
| Litigation | National courts | Public, appeals, interim measures | Foreign judgments may be hard to enforce; slower in some systems |

Practical steps when a dispute arises: preserve documents, follow contractual notice procedures and deadlines, assess the legal and commercial merits and the counterparty’s ability to pay, consider interim measures (asset freezes), calculate costs and time, and keep negotiating in parallel.

## Insolvency and bankruptcy

Insolvency laws differ widely; common features:

- **Tests:** cash-flow insolvency (cannot pay debts as due) and balance-sheet insolvency (liabilities exceed assets).
- **Procedures:** pre-insolvency restructuring/composition, reorganisation under court protection (with a moratorium on creditor actions), administration/receivership, and liquidation.
- **Priority of claims:** secured creditors (on their collateral), costs of the procedure, preferential claims (often some employee and tax claims), unsecured creditors, subordinated creditors, shareholders.
- **Avoidance (claw-back):** transactions shortly before insolvency at undervalue or preferring certain creditors may be reversed.
- **Directors’ liability:** for wrongful/fraudulent trading or failure to file in time, depending on law.
- **Cross-border:** recognition of foreign proceedings (e.g., under laws based on the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency in some jurisdictions).

Several Middle Eastern countries have reformed their bankruptcy laws in recent years (for example, Saudi Arabia’s Bankruptcy Law of 2018 and the UAE’s Federal Decree-Law No. 51 of 2023 on financial restructuring and bankruptcy); consult local counsel.

## Regulatory compliance

Compliance covers licensing, sector regulation (financial services, healthcare, food, telecoms), consumer protection, competition law (cartels, abuse of dominance, merger control), labour law, tax, customs and export controls, environmental law, sanctions and AML. A compliance programme includes: risk assessment, policies, training, due diligence, monitoring, reporting channels (whistleblowing), investigations and remediation.

## Conflicts of interest

A conflict arises when personal interests could influence professional judgement (e.g., a purchasing manager related to a supplier’s owner). Controls: declarations of interest, recusal from decisions, approval of related-party transactions by independent persons, gifts and hospitality policies, and audit.

## Anti-bribery and corruption

| Law / standard | Key features |
|---|---|
| US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA, 1977) | Prohibits bribery of foreign officials by US issuers, domestic concerns and persons acting in US territory; books-and-records and internal-controls provisions for issuers |
| UK Bribery Act 2010 | Covers public and private bribery; corporate offence of failing to prevent bribery (defence: adequate procedures) |
| National anti-corruption laws | Exist across the region (e.g., penal-code provisions and anti-corruption authorities) |
| ISO 37001 | Anti-bribery management system standard |
| OECD Anti-Bribery Convention; UN Convention against Corruption (UNCAC) | International frameworks |

Risk areas: agents and intermediaries (especially in government sales), customs and licensing facilitation, gifts and hospitality, charitable donations and sponsorships, joint-venture partners, and “facilitation payments” (prohibited under the UK Act and many laws). Controls: third-party due diligence, contractual anti-bribery clauses, reasonable commission levels tied to documented services, payment only to the contracting party’s account in its country, approvals and audit rights.

## Data protection

Data-protection laws regulate how personal data (about identifiable individuals) are collected, used, stored and transferred: e.g., the EU **GDPR** (applies to organisations established in the EU and, in some cases, outside the EU when offering goods/services to or monitoring people in the EU), the UAE’s Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on personal data protection, Saudi Arabia’s Personal Data Protection Law (in force since 2023), and free-zone regimes (e.g., DIFC, ADGM). Common principles: lawful basis, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, security, individual rights, breach notification, and restrictions on cross-border transfers. Business implications: privacy notices, consent management, vendor contracts (data processing agreements), security controls, data-transfer assessments, and records of processing.

يشمل الامتثال الالتزام بالقوانين المنظمة للنشاط، ومكافحة الرشوة والفساد، والعقوبات الدولية، ومكافحة غسل الأموال، وقوانين المنافسة، وحماية البيانات الشخصية. وأخطر المناطق في مكافحة الرشوة هي الوكلاء والوسطاء في التعامل مع الجهات الحكومية، والهدايا والضيافة، و«مدفوعات التسهيل». لذلك يجب إجراء العناية الواجبة على الوكلاء، وتضمين العقود بنوداً لمكافحة الرشوة، وربط العمولات بخدمات موثقة ومعقولة. وتختلف القوانين بين الدول، فيلزم الرجوع إلى مستشار قانوني محلي.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. An agent for a government tender asks for a 12% commission, payable to a company in a third country, “to cover expenses”. Identify the red flags and appropriate actions.
2. A supplier is in a court-supervised reorganisation. You have $400,000 of unpaid invoices. What should you do?
3. Your SaaS company stores customer data of EU residents on servers in the Gulf. Which data-protection questions arise?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Red flags: high commission, vague services, payment to a third-country entity, government tender context. Actions: due diligence on the agent (ownership, links to officials, qualifications), documented services and market-rate commission, payment only to the agent’s own account in its country, anti-bribery clauses and audit rights, compliance approval — or decline.
2. File your claim within the procedure’s deadlines, check any security or retention-of-title rights, stop or secure further supplies (e.g., cash in advance or under the procedure’s rules for new supplies), review set-off possibilities, follow creditor meetings and votes, and take local legal advice.
3. Whether GDPR applies (offering services to people in the EU), lawful basis for processing, cross-border transfer mechanism (adequacy, standard contractual clauses, transfer impact assessment), data-processing agreements with clients, security measures, breach notification procedures, and local data-protection law requirements where servers and the company are located.
:::

:::summary Part XII summary
- Entity choice determines liability, governance and flexibility; shareholder agreements protect investors and founders.
- Contracts allocate risk through warranties, indemnities, liability caps, conditions, force majeure, termination and dispute clauses — read them for business consequences.
- Commercial agreements have recurring issues; IP must be registered and assigned deliberately.
- Arbitration with a clear clause is the norm for international disputes; insolvency rules differ by country.
- Compliance — anti-bribery, sanctions, competition and data protection — is a core business risk, not a legal afterthought.
- When in doubt, involve a lawyer qualified in the relevant jurisdiction early.
:::
