Entrepreneurship applies everything in this collection under extreme uncertainty and limited resources. A founder must find a real problem, design a business model that can make money, prove that customers will pay, finance the journey, build a team and systems, and eventually create value for owners through profits or an exit. This part gives a practical framework for evaluating business ideas, explains startup business models and unit economics, covers financing from bootstrapping to venture capital and exits, and works through two complete fictional startup cases with financial projections.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part XIII
- Evaluate whether a business idea is commercially viable using a structured framework.
- Design validation experiments and MVPs and recognise product-market fit.
- Analyse SaaS, subscription, marketplace, franchise and licensing models through unit economics.
- Understand startup financing instruments, valuation, dilution, term sheets, pitch decks and due diligence.
- Build startup projections: revenue, costs, cash flow, CAC, funding requirements.
- Understand scaling and exit options.
:::

# Opportunity, Validation and Product-Market Fit

:::obj In this chapter
A framework to assess commercial viability, methods for validating demand cheaply, MVPs, and how to recognise product-market fit.
:::

## Is the idea commercially viable? A ten-question framework

| # | Question | Evidence that answers it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is the problem real, frequent and painful (or the desire strong)? | Customer interviews, observed behaviour, current spending on workarounds |
| 2 | Who exactly is the customer, and who pays? | Defined segment/ICP, buyer vs user distinction |
| 3 | How large is the reachable market? | Bottom-up SAM/SOM |
| 4 | Why will customers choose us over alternatives (including doing nothing)? | Quantified value proposition, switching triggers |
| 5 | Will they pay enough? | Pre-sales, pilots with paid fees, pricing tests |
| 6 | Can we reach customers at an acceptable cost? | Channel tests, CAC estimates |
| 7 | Are the unit economics positive at scale? | Contribution per unit/customer, LTV/CAC |
| 8 | Can we deliver reliably and legally? | Operations plan, licences, regulation, partners |
| 9 | What capital is needed until the business is self-funding, and can we raise it? | Cash flow forecast, investor appetite |
| 10 | Why us, why now? | Team’s unfair advantages, timing (regulation, technology, market shift) |

:::tip Validation beats planning
A 40-page business plan with untested assumptions is weaker than ten paying pilot customers. Founders should identify the **riskiest assumption** (usually “customers will pay X for this”) and test it first, as cheaply as possible.
:::

## Validation methods

| Method | Tests | Cost/time |
|---|---|---|
| Problem interviews (15–30) | Problem existence and importance | Low |
| Landing page with sign-up or pre-order | Interest and messaging | Low |
| Concierge MVP (deliver the service manually) | Value and willingness to pay | Low–medium |
| Wizard-of-Oz MVP (looks automated, done manually behind the scenes) | User experience and demand | Medium |
| Pilot with letter of intent or paid trial | B2B commitment | Medium |
| Pre-sales / crowdfunding | Real purchase intent | Medium |
| Prototype tests | Usability | Medium |

## Product-market fit (PMF)

Signals: customers use the product repeatedly and complain when it is unavailable; retention curves flatten (a stable share of each cohort stays); growth increasingly comes from referrals; sales cycles shorten; customers accept price increases. Before PMF, focus on learning and iteration; after PMF, on scaling acquisition, operations and the team.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Identify the riskiest assumption and a cheap test for: (a) a B2B platform matching small importers with freight forwarders; (b) a subscription service delivering healthy lunches to offices.
2. A founder reports 5,000 app downloads in a month. Is that evidence of PMF? What would you ask?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (a) That small importers will book through a platform rather than call their usual forwarder (and that forwarders will pay commissions) → manually broker 20 shipments via WhatsApp/email for a fee and measure repeat usage. (b) That offices will pay a recurring subscription at a price covering costs → sign three offices to a paid 4-week pilot and measure retention and willingness to renew.
2. No — downloads measure interest, not value. Ask about weekly active users, retention by cohort (week 1, 4, 8), conversion to paid, frequency of use, referral share, and CAC of those downloads.
:::

# Startup Business Models and Unit Economics

:::obj In this chapter
How common startup models make money and the metrics that determine whether they can become profitable: SaaS and subscriptions, marketplaces, e-commerce and D2C, franchising, licensing and partnerships.
:::

## SaaS and subscriptions

| Metric | Definition | Benchmark heuristics (vary by segment) |
|---|---|---|
| MRR / ARR | Monthly/annual recurring revenue | Growth rate is the headline metric |
| Gross margin | (Revenue − hosting, support, third-party costs) ÷ revenue | Often 70–85% |
| Logo churn / revenue churn | Customers / revenue lost per period | SMB monthly churn of 1–3% is common; enterprise far lower |
| Net revenue retention | Includes expansion | > 100% means existing customers grow |
| CAC payback | Months of gross profit to recover CAC | < 12–18 months often targeted |
| LTV/CAC | Lifetime gross profit ÷ CAC | ≥ 3 often targeted |
| Burn multiple | Net burn ÷ net new ARR | Lower is more efficient |
| Rule of 40 | Growth % + profit margin % | ≥ 40 for mature SaaS (heuristic) |

## Marketplaces

A marketplace connects buyers and sellers and earns a **take rate** (commission) on **gross merchandise value (GMV)** or a subscription/listing fee. Key challenges: the chicken-and-egg problem (attracting both sides), liquidity (probability that a search results in a transaction), disintermediation (parties bypass the platform after meeting), trust (reviews, payments, escrow), and regulatory questions (who is responsible for goods/services).

:::formula Marketplace economics
Net revenue = GMV × take rate
Contribution = Net revenue − payment costs − customer support − incentives/subsidies − variable marketing
:::

## E-commerce and direct-to-consumer (D2C)

Contribution per order = average order value − product cost − packaging − fulfilment − delivery − payment fees − returns − variable marketing. Many D2C businesses discover that **delivery, returns and paid acquisition** consume their gross margin.

## Franchising

The franchisor licenses its brand and operating system to franchisees who invest and operate units, paying an **initial fee**, ongoing **royalties** (e.g., 4–8% of sales) and **marketing fund contributions**. Franchising allows fast expansion with others’ capital but requires a proven, documented, profitable unit model, training and quality control. Some countries regulate franchise disclosure and registration.

## Licensing and partnerships

**Licensing** monetises IP (technology, brands, content) through royalties with low capital needs; success depends on IP protection and licensee performance. **Partnerships** (distribution, technology integration, co-marketing, joint ventures) extend reach; they need clear economics, responsibilities and exit terms.

## Recurring revenue: why investors pay more

Recurring revenue is predictable, compounds through retention and expansion, and lowers the cost of future growth. Investors therefore value high-quality recurring revenue at higher multiples than one-off project revenue — provided churn is low and CAC is recoverable.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A marketplace has GMV of $40m, take rate 12%, payment costs 2% of GMV, support $0.6m, incentives $1.2m. Compute net revenue and contribution.
2. A D2C brand: AOV $55; product cost $18; packaging $2; fulfilment $4; delivery $6; payment 3%; returns cost 5% of AOV; paid marketing $15 per order on average. Compute contribution per order.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Net revenue = **$4.8m**; contribution = 4.8 − 0.8 − 0.6 − 1.2 = **$2.2m**.
2. 55 − 18 − 2 − 4 − 6 − 1.65 − 2.75 − 15 = **$5.60** per order (10.2% of AOV) — thin; improving repeat purchases (lower marketing per order) is critical.
:::

# Financing a Startup: From Bootstrapping to Exit

:::obj In this chapter
Sources of startup capital, funding stages, instruments, valuation methods, dilution and cap tables, term sheets, pitch decks and business plans, investor due diligence, scaling, and exits.
:::

## Sources and stages

| Stage | Typical sources | Purpose | Typical instruments |
|---|---|---|---|
| Idea / pre-seed | Founders, friends and family, grants, accelerators | Validation, MVP | Equity, convertible notes, SAFEs |
| Seed | Angels, seed funds, accelerators | Product-market fit, first team | SAFEs/convertibles, priced equity |
| Series A | Venture capital | Scale go-to-market | Preferred shares |
| Series B, C… | VC, growth funds, corporate VC | Expansion, new markets | Preferred shares |
| Growth / pre-IPO | Growth equity, private equity, sovereign funds | Scale, acquisitions | Preferred/ordinary shares |
| Debt alternatives | Bank loans (often with guarantees), venture debt, revenue-based financing | Extend runway, working capital | Loans, warrants |

**Bootstrapping** (funding from revenue and founders’ resources) preserves control and discipline; it suits businesses that reach profitability quickly. Venture capital suits businesses with very large potential markets where speed matters more than early profits.

## Instruments

| Instrument | Description | Key terms |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary (common) shares | Basic equity | Voting, dividends |
| Preferred shares | Equity with preferences | Liquidation preference, anti-dilution, conversion, voting rights, board seats, protective provisions |
| Convertible note | Loan that converts to equity at a later round | Discount (e.g., 20%), valuation cap, interest, maturity |
| SAFE | Simple agreement for future equity — converts at next round | Valuation cap, discount; no interest/maturity |
| Venture debt | Loans to VC-backed startups | Interest, warrants, covenants |
| Revenue-based financing | Repaid as % of revenue | Cap on total repayment |

## Valuation of early-stage companies

Traditional DCF is unreliable before revenue stabilises. Practitioners use:

- **Comparable rounds** (what similar startups raised at).
- **VC method:** Post-money today = expected exit value ÷ target multiple (or ÷ (1 + target IRR)^years), adjusted for expected future dilution.
- **Scorecard/qualitative methods** at pre-seed.
- **Revenue multiples** once recurring revenue exists.

:::example VC method (fictional)
A startup expects revenue of $30m in year 6; comparable companies sell at 4× revenue → exit value $120m. The VC targets 10× its money (reflecting high failure risk) → post-money today = 120 ÷ 10 = $12m. Expected dilution from future rounds: founders and current investors retain 60% → adjusted post-money = 12 × 0.6 = **$7.2m**. For a $2m investment → pre-money **$5.2m**; investor ownership 2 ÷ 7.2 ≈ **27.8%** today (diluting to ~16.7% at exit).
:::

## Dilution and the cap table

:::example Cap table through two rounds (fictional)
**Founding:** Founder A 6,000,000 shares; Founder B 4,000,000 shares.

**Seed:** $750,000 at $3.0m pre-money, with a 10% employee option pool (post-money basis) created **before** the round (included in pre-money). Post-money = $3.75m → investor 20%, pool 10%, founders 70%.
Total shares = 10,000,000 ÷ 0.70 = 14,285,714; seed investor 2,857,143 shares; pool 1,428,571; price per share = 750,000 ÷ 2,857,143 = **$0.2625**.

**Series A:** $4.0m at $12.0m pre-money (no pool top-up) → post-money $16.0m → new investor 25%. Price per share = 12,000,000 ÷ 14,285,714 = **$0.84**; new shares 4,761,905.

| Holder | After founding | After seed | After Series A |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder A | 60.0% | 42.0% | 31.5% |
| Founder B | 40.0% | 28.0% | 21.0% |
| Option pool | — | 10.0% | 7.5% |
| Seed investor | — | 20.0% | 15.0% |
| Series A investor | — | — | 25.0% |

Founders’ combined stake fell from 100% to 52.5%, while its value rose from nominal to 52.5% × $16m = **$8.4m** (on paper).
:::

## Liquidation preferences: why terms matter more than headline valuation

With 1× non-participating preferences (seed and Series A), consider exits:

- **$80m exit:** preferences (seed $0.75m, Series A $4m) are worth less than converting; everyone converts → Series A 25% = $20m; seed $12m; founders $42m; pool $6m.
- **$10m exit:** Series A takes its $4m preference (better than 25% × $10m = $2.5m); the remaining $6m is shared by common shareholders and the seed investor converting (seed’s share 15/75 = 20% → $1.2m > its $0.75m preference). Founders receive 52.5/75 × $6m = **$4.2m** (42% of proceeds vs 52.5% ownership).

Participating preferences, multiples above 1×, or full-ratchet anti-dilution shift much more value to investors in modest outcomes.

## Term sheet key terms

| Term | Meaning | Founder-friendly | Investor-friendly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valuation (pre-money) | Price | Higher | Lower |
| Option pool | Pre- or post-money | Post-money or small | Large, pre-money |
| Liquidation preference | Priority on exit | 1× non-participating | >1×, participating |
| Anti-dilution | Protection in down rounds | Broad-based weighted average | Full ratchet |
| Board composition | Control | Founder majority | Investor seats/independent chosen by investors |
| Protective provisions | Investor vetoes | Narrow list | Broad list |
| Vesting of founder shares | Retain founders | Credit for time served | 4-year vesting, 1-year cliff |
| Drag-along | Forced sale | High thresholds | Low thresholds |
| Information rights | Reporting | Quarterly | Monthly, detailed |
| Exclusivity / no-shop | Period of exclusive negotiation | Short (30 days) | Longer |

## Pitch decks and business plans

**Pitch deck** (10–15 slides): problem; solution; why now; market size; product (demo); business model and pricing; traction (metrics, customers); go-to-market; competition and differentiation; team; financials and key assumptions; funding ask and use of funds; vision.

**Business plan** (for banks, partners, grant bodies, or internal planning): executive summary; company and team; market analysis; products; marketing and sales plan; operations; organisation; risk analysis; financial plan (assumptions, three statements, cash flow, funding); appendices. A template is in the Document Library (Volume 9).

## Investor due diligence on startups

Legal (incorporation, cap table, IP ownership and assignments, contracts, employment, licences), financial (accounts, burn, runway, unit economics, revenue recognition), commercial (customer interviews, pipeline, churn data), technical (architecture, security, code quality, scalability), team (references, background checks), regulatory and compliance.

## Scaling

Scaling requires repeatable sales processes, hiring managers, systems (finance, CRM, HR), controls, culture building, and capital efficiency. Common failure: premature scaling — hiring sales teams and spending on marketing before product-market fit and unit economics are proven.

## Exits

| Exit route | Description | Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Trade sale (acquisition) | Sale to a strategic buyer | Most common; earn-outs, retention packages |
| Secondary sale | Sale of shares to other investors | Partial liquidity |
| Private equity buyout | Sale to PE (for profitable companies) | Leverage, management rollover |
| IPO | Listing on an exchange | Scale and governance requirements; lock-ups |
| Acqui-hire | Acquisition mainly for the team | Low value for investors |
| Wind-down | Closure | Orderly liquidation; protect reputation |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A startup raises $1m on a SAFE with a $6m post-money valuation cap. At the Series A, the price per share implies a $15m post-money valuation. Approximately what ownership does the SAFE holder get (ignoring other dilution)?
2. Using the cap table example, what does Founder A receive in a $30m exit (all convert)?
3. Name four items you would include in a startup’s data room for due diligence.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Under a post-money cap SAFE, ownership ≈ investment ÷ cap = 1 ÷ 6 ≈ **16.7%** (before the Series A dilution of new money); the cap applies because it gives a lower price than the Series A.
2. At $30m all preferred holders convert (25% × 30 = 7.5 > 4; 15% × 30 = 4.5 > 0.75) → Founder A 31.5% × $30m = **$9.45m** (assuming the pool is fully allocated).
3. Certificate of incorporation and constitutional documents; cap table and all share/SAFE agreements; IP assignment agreements from founders, employees and contractors; customer contracts and revenue data; financial statements and management accounts; key employment contracts; licences and regulatory approvals; product and security documentation.
:::

# Startup Case Studies

:::obj In this chapter
Two complete fictional startup cases — a B2B SaaS company and a food delivery brand — with revenue projections, cost structures, unit economics, cash flows and financing requirements, and an assessment of commercial viability.
:::

## Case 1 — PortDocs: SaaS for customs brokers in the GCC (fictional)

**Concept:** software that automates preparation of customs declarations from commercial documents (OCR + machine learning + integration with customs systems), sold to customs brokers and forwarders.

**Key assumptions:**

| Assumption | Value |
|---|---|
| Price | $600 per customer per month (average; tiered by volume) |
| Gross margin | 78% (cloud hosting, OCR/AI API costs, support) |
| Monthly logo churn | 1.5% (≈ 18% per year) |
| CAC (blended, field + partner sales) | $5,400 |
| Customers at year-end | Y1 40 · Y2 150 · Y3 380 · Y4 700 · Y5 1,100 |
| R&D costs | $0.6m rising to $1.8m |
| G&A | $0.25m rising to $0.8m |

**Unit economics:** monthly gross profit per customer = 600 × 78% = $468; LTV = 468 ÷ 0.015 = **$31,200**; LTV/CAC = **5.8**; CAC payback = 5,400 ÷ 468 = **11.5 months**. Strong unit economics.

**Five-year projection ($m, simplified annual model):**

| | Y1 | Y2 | Y3 | Y4 | Y5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Average customers | 20 | 95 | 265 | 540 | 900 |
| Revenue | 0.14 | 0.68 | 1.91 | 3.89 | 6.48 |
| Gross profit (78%) | 0.11 | 0.53 | 1.49 | 3.03 | 5.05 |
| Gross new customers (net adds + churn) | 44 | 127 | 278 | 417 | 562 |
| Sales and marketing (× CAC) | (0.24) | (0.69) | (1.50) | (2.25) | (3.03) |
| R&D | (0.60) | (0.90) | (1.20) | (1.50) | (1.80) |
| G&A | (0.25) | (0.35) | (0.50) | (0.65) | (0.80) |
| **EBITDA** | **(0.98)** | **(1.40)** | **(1.71)** | **(1.37)** | **(0.58)** |
| ARR at year-end | 0.29 | 1.08 | 2.74 | 5.04 | 7.92 |

**Cash and funding:** cumulative EBITDA losses ≈ $6.0m over five years; annual prepayments by some customers improve cash slightly. Funding plan: pre-seed $0.3m (founders/angels), seed $1.5m (end of Y1, at ~$6m post-money), Series A $6m (Y3, at ARR ~$2.5m, valuation driven by growth and retention). Break-even expected in Y6 as R&D and G&A grow slower than gross profit.

**Viability assessment:**

- *Strengths:* real, quantifiable pain (time and demurrage); strong LTV/CAC; switching costs once integrated; regulatory digitalisation trends.
- *Risks:* dependence on customs-system integrations (each country different); total market size — ~1,100 customers at $600 is ~$8m ARR, which may cap growth unless the product expands (more countries, more products such as duty payments, trade-compliance screening, financing referrals); competition from customs-software incumbents and from governments’ own portals; data security.
- *Verdict:* viable as a capital-efficient B2B business; to attract VC at scale it needs a credible expansion path to a larger market (e.g., all MENA and South Asia, adjacent products).

## Case 2 — Sahtein Kitchens: healthy meals by delivery (fictional, Beirut)

**Concept:** a delivery-only kitchen preparing healthy Lebanese-inspired meals, selling through delivery aggregators and its own app with subscriptions.

**Order economics:**

| Per order (USD) | Via aggregator | Own app |
|---|---|---|
| Average order value | 14.00 | 14.00 |
| Food cost (32%) | (4.48) | (4.48) |
| Packaging (6%) | (0.84) | (0.84) |
| Aggregator commission (25%) | (3.50) | — |
| Delivery (own riders/3PL) | — (included) | (2.50) |
| Payment fees | — (aggregator) | (0.35) |
| CAC allocated (own app: $12 per new customer ÷ 6 orders lifetime) | — | (2.00) |
| **Contribution per order** | **5.18** | **3.83** |

At this stage, aggregator orders are more profitable per order because own-app customers are new and expensive to acquire; once own-app customers order more often (e.g., 15 orders lifetime via subscriptions), allocated CAC falls to $0.80 → contribution **$5.03**, and the brand owns the customer relationship and data.

**Fixed costs:** rent and utilities $4,500/month; kitchen staff $9,500; management and admin $3,000; technology and marketing base $1,000 → **$18,000/month**.

**Break-even:** 18,000 ÷ ~$5.10 average contribution ≈ **3,530 orders/month** (~118 per day).

**Investment and funding need:**

| Item | $ |
|---|---|
| Kitchen fit-out and equipment | 85,000 |
| Licences, deposits, initial inventory | 15,000 |
| Brand, app (white-label), launch marketing | 20,000 |
| Operating losses until break-even (ramp from 900 to 3,600 orders/month over 9 months; cumulative fixed costs 162,000 − cumulative contribution ~105,000) | ~57,000 |
| Contingency (20%) | ~35,000 |
| **Total funding need** | **~212,000** |

**Financing:** founders $70,000; angel investors $150,000 for 25% (post-money $600,000). Alternative: equipment leasing for part of the fit-out to reduce equity needed.

**Viability assessment:** contribution margins are positive but thin; break-even requires ~118 orders a day; risks include intense competition, aggregator dependence and commission changes, food-cost inflation, and economic conditions affecting consumer spending. Paths to stronger economics: subscriptions (higher frequency, lower CAC per order), corporate catering contracts (larger orders, lower delivery cost per meal), and a second brand from the same kitchen (economies of scope).

:::tip What these cases teach
- **Unit economics first:** both businesses are only as good as contribution per customer or per order.
- **Fixed costs determine break-even and funding:** SaaS has high upfront R&D; food delivery has kitchen fixed costs.
- **Funding must cover the path to break-even plus a buffer,** and the type of funding should fit the business (VC for large scalable markets; angels, founders, leasing and bank finance for smaller, steadier businesses).
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. In Case 1, if churn rises to 2.5% per month, recompute LTV, LTV/CAC and comment.
2. In Case 2, the aggregator raises commission to 30%. What is the new contribution per aggregator order and the break-even if half of orders come from aggregators and half from the own app (mature CAC)?
3. Write a three-sentence investment recommendation for an angel considering Case 2.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. LTV = 468 ÷ 0.025 = **$18,720**; LTV/CAC = **3.5**; still acceptable but materially weaker — growth would require more capital, and the value of each customer falls 40%; retention becomes the priority (onboarding, integrations).
2. Commission 4.20 → contribution 14 − 4.48 − 0.84 − 4.20 = **$4.48**; own app mature $5.03 → average **$4.76** → break-even = 18,000 ÷ 4.76 ≈ **3,780 orders/month** (~126/day).
3. Example: “Sahtein has positive unit economics and a credible path to break-even at ~120 orders per day, but margins are thin and dependent on aggregator commissions. The investment is justified only if the team can shift at least 40% of orders to its own subscription channel within 12 months and secure corporate catering contracts. I would invest in tranches tied to those milestones.”
:::

:::summary Part XIII summary
- Test the riskiest assumption first; paying customers beat business plans.
- Product-market fit shows in retention, referrals and willingness to pay.
- Each model has defining unit economics: LTV/CAC and churn for SaaS, take rate and liquidity for marketplaces, contribution per order for D2C.
- Financing must match the business; terms (preferences, pools, anti-dilution) matter as much as valuation.
- Projections should connect unit economics, fixed costs, cash burn and funding milestones.
:::
