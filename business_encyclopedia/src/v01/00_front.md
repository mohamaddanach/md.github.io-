# How to Use This Encyclopedia

## Why this collection exists

A finance or computer-science degree teaches you models: discounted cash flows, regression, data structures, the capital asset pricing model, algorithms. Business practice also runs on a second body of knowledge that universities rarely teach in full. That second layer is made of *conventions* (how a letter of credit is actually presented to a bank), *vocabulary* (what a credit officer means by “tenor” or a trader by “contango”), *documents* (what a bill of lading proves and what it does not), *institutions* (who a correspondent bank is and why it matters), and *judgement* (why an experienced manager worries more about cash than about profit in a growing company).

This collection is designed to close that gap. It is written for a reader who is numerate, comfortable with technical ideas, and already familiar with parts of finance and technology, but who wants to understand the whole commercial world well enough to read any contract, report or email, participate credibly in a meeting, and eventually run or build a business.

:::obj What you should be able to do after working through the collection
- Read a business document — financial statement, term sheet, bill of lading, contract, analyst report — and explain what every important field means and why it is there.
- Follow a commercial transaction end to end: negotiation, contract, shipment, documents, payment, financing, accounting entries, risks and controls.
- Explain how a company makes money, where it loses money, and why profitable companies can still run out of cash.
- Explain what a bank does for a corporate client and how it decides whether to lend.
- Calculate margins, landed cost, break-even, working-capital needs, loan schedules, NPV, IRR, valuation multiples and FX outcomes without hesitation.
- Identify material risks in a deal and propose concrete controls.
- Communicate professionally in English, and connect English business terminology to correct Modern Standard Arabic.
- Combine finance, operations and technology to create business value.
:::

### Learning sequence

The order of volumes is the recommended learning order with two exceptions. First, **Part XX (Business Mathematics)** sits in Volume 3 next to corporate finance, but you should use it as a workbook from the first week: whenever a chapter uses a formula, the full worked treatment is in Part XX. Second, **Part XVII (Communication)** is in Volume 8 but its phrasebook ({{ch:Phrasebook}}) is worth reading early and revisiting weekly. The six-month roadmap in Volume 9 schedules both.

# Terminology and Translation System

## Principles

1. **English is the primary language** of the text because English is the working language of international finance, trade and contracts. Arabic is provided for every important term and for complex explanations.
2. **Modern Standard Arabic** (الفصحى المعاصرة) is used for definitions and explanations. Regional colloquial forms are mentioned only when they are widely used in business.
3. **No mechanical translation.** Where a literal translation would mislead, the text uses the term that Arabic-speaking professionals and regulators actually use, and explains the nuance.
4. **Acronyms are preserved** in their English form (EBITDA, L/C, KYC, IRR) and explained. Arab bankers, accountants and traders overwhelmingly say these acronyms in English.
5. **One preferred translation per term.** Where several Arabic equivalents exist, the preferred one comes first and alternatives are noted.

:::ar لماذا نحتفظ بالمصطلحات الإنجليزية؟
في المصارف وشركات التجارة والاستشارات في العالم العربي تُستخدم كثير من المصطلحات بلغتها الإنجليزية مباشرة، مثل «EBITDA» و«KYC» و«L/C» و«Due Diligence». معرفة المقابل العربي الدقيق ضرورية لقراءة الأنظمة والتعاميم الرسمية والعقود العربية، ومعرفة المصطلح الإنجليزي ضرورية للتواصل المهني اليومي. لذلك يقدّم هذا الكتاب الاثنين معاً.
:::

## Terms Arabic-speaking professionals usually keep in English

The following are normally spoken in English in Arab banks, trading houses, consulting firms and technology companies, even in Arabic conversation: *EBITDA, CAPEX, OPEX, KPI, OKR, ROI, IRR, NPV, CRM, ERP, KYC, AML, L/C, SBLC, B/L, FOB, CIF, DDP, SWIFT, IBAN, LIBOR/SOFR, IPO, SPV, NDA, MoU, LOI, due diligence, pipeline, forecast, deal, closing, onboarding, compliance, cash flow, working capital, pitch deck, term sheet*.

:::tip Practical advice
In a meeting in Riyadh, Dubai, Beirut or Cairo you might hear: «نحتاج نشوف الـ cash flow قبل ما نوقّع الـ term sheet». In a regulator’s circular or a court document the same idea would be written: «يجب دراسة التدفقات النقدية قبل توقيع ورقة الشروط». Learn both registers.
:::

# How Knowledge Is Classified in This Book

Business rules come from very different sources with very different authority. Beginners often treat a market convention as if it were law, or a single bank’s policy as if it were a global standard. This collection labels the source of a rule wherever it matters.

| Category | What it is | Examples | How binding? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Global principle** | An economic or accounting logic true everywhere | Double entry; time value of money; supply and demand | Universal logic, not law |
| **International standard** | Rules published by a standard-setter, binding only when adopted by law or incorporated by contract | IFRS (IFRS Foundation/IASB); UCP 600, URC 522, URDG 758, Incoterms® 2020 (ICC); Basel III (BCBS) | Binding when a law adopts it (IFRS in many countries) or a contract incorporates it (UCP 600 in an L/C) |
| **Country-specific law** | Statutes, regulations and court decisions of a jurisdiction | Company law, insolvency law, customs tariffs, tax law, labour law | Binding within that jurisdiction |
| **Regulatory requirement** | Rules of a supervisor in a jurisdiction | Central bank capital and liquidity rules, AML regulations | Binding on regulated entities |
| **Industry practice** | How most participants actually behave | Payment terms of 30–90 days in B2B; 2-business-day spot FX settlement | Not binding unless contracted; departures need negotiation |
| **Contractual convention** | Standard clauses and forms widely used | Standard commodity contracts, master agreements (e.g., ISDA for derivatives) | Binding only if signed/incorporated |
| **Institution-specific requirement** | A bank’s or company’s internal policy | A bank’s minimum DSCR of 1.25×; a company’s approval matrix | Binding inside that institution only |
| **Transaction-specific term** | Whatever the parties agree in this deal | Price, quantity, tolerance, deadlines | Binding on the parties |

:::warn Common mistakes
- Saying “the law requires a letter of credit to be paid in 5 days”. UCP 600 gives banks a maximum of five banking days *to examine documents*; it is a set of ICC rules that applies because the credit incorporates it, not a statute.
- Assuming IFRS and US GAAP give the same answer. They often do not (inventory methods, development costs, impairment reversals).
- Treating a bank’s internal credit criteria as “the rules”. A different bank may accept the same client on different terms.
:::

## What “thinking like a professional” means

Throughout the collection you will see contrasts between how a **graduate** and an **experienced professional** approach the same problem. The recurring differences are:

1. **Cash before profit.** Professionals ask “when do we get paid, and what do we need to fund until then?” before “what is the margin?”.
2. **Downside before upside.** They ask what happens if the counterparty does not pay, the ship is late, the currency moves or the key customer leaves.
3. **Documents and evidence.** They rely on what can be proven: signed contracts, presented documents, audited numbers, confirmed balances.
4. **Incentives.** They ask who benefits from each clause, number and recommendation.
5. **Second-order effects.** A price cut increases volume, which increases inventory, which consumes cash, which requires borrowing, which increases interest cost and covenant pressure.
6. **Precise language.** They distinguish revenue from cash, a guarantee from collateral, a quotation from an offer, an estimate from a fact.

Keep these six habits in mind as you read. {{ch:Professional Mindset}} returns to them in depth.
