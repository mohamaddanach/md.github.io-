# Commonly Confused Concepts: Comparison Guide

:::obj In this chapter
Side-by-side comparisons of terms that beginners — and sometimes professionals — use interchangeably. Each table is a quick test: if you can explain every row, you can use the terms precisely in a meeting or a contract.
:::

## Profit, cash and value

| Pair | First term | Second term | The practical difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue vs cash receipts | Earned when control passes | Received when customer pays | Credit terms create the gap; receivables bridge them. |
| Profit vs cash flow | Accrual measure of performance | Actual movement of money | Non-cash items, working capital and capex separate them. |
| EBITDA vs operating cash flow | Earnings before D&A, interest, tax | Cash from operations after working-capital changes and tax | EBITDA ignores working capital and tax paid. |
| Gross margin vs contribution margin | Revenue − COGS | Revenue − all variable costs | Contribution includes variable selling/delivery costs; used for break-even. |
| Margin vs markup | Profit ÷ price | Profit ÷ cost | 25% markup = 20% margin. |
| Book value vs market value | Accounting equity | What buyers would pay | Market value reflects future expectations; book value reflects history. |
| Enterprise value vs equity value | Whole business (debt + equity) | Shareholders’ portion | EV − net debt = equity value. |
| ROI vs IRR | Simple gain ÷ cost | Annualised, time-weighted return | ROI ignores timing; IRR does not. |
| NPV vs IRR | Value created in money | Rate of return in % | For mutually exclusive projects, prefer NPV. |
| ROE vs ROA vs ROIC | Return on equity | Return on total assets | ROIC: return on capital invested (debt + equity) in operations; least distorted by leverage. |

## Assets, liabilities and accounting

| Pair | First term | Second term | The practical difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capex vs opex | Capitalised, depreciated over life | Expensed immediately | Changes timing of profit, not of cash. |
| Depreciation vs amortisation | Tangible assets | Intangible assets (or loan repayment) | Same idea, different asset types; two meanings of “amortisation”. |
| Depreciation vs impairment | Planned allocation | Unplanned write-down | Impairment signals lost value. |
| Provision vs reserve | Liability for probable obligation | Part of equity | Provisions reduce profit; reserves are appropriations of equity. |
| Provision vs contingent liability | Probable, estimable — recognised | Possible — disclosed only | Recognition threshold. |
| Accrued expense vs account payable | Owed, not yet invoiced | Invoiced, not yet paid | Both liabilities; source document differs. |
| Deferred revenue vs accrued revenue | Paid, not earned (liability) | Earned, not billed (asset) | Opposite timing gaps. |
| Audit vs review | Reasonable assurance | Limited assurance | A review gives far less comfort. |
| Internal audit vs external audit | Serves management/board on controls and risk | Independent opinion on financial statements to shareholders | Different mandates and reporting lines. |
| Cash basis vs accrual basis | Records cash movements | Records economic events | IFRS/GAAP require accrual. |

## Finance and banking

| Pair | First term | Second term | The practical difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquidity vs solvency | Ability to pay now | Ability to pay over time; assets > liabilities | Can be solvent but illiquid. |
| Funded vs unfunded facility | Bank lends cash | Bank issues an undertaking (L/C, guarantee) | Unfunded becomes funded if called/paid. |
| Overdraft vs RCF | Usually on demand | Committed for a period | Certainty of funding. |
| Term loan vs RCF | Draw once, repay on schedule | Draw, repay, redraw | Purpose: long-term assets vs working capital. |
| Collateral vs guarantee | Asset pledged | Third-party promise to pay | Asset value vs guarantor credit. |
| Guarantee vs standby L/C | Demand guarantee (often URDG 758) | Standby L/C (often ISP98 or UCP 600) | Functionally similar; different rules and market habits (SBLC common in US). |
| Commercial L/C vs standby L/C | Payment mechanism, expected to be used | Back-up, used only on default | Normal vs exceptional drawing. |
| Confirmed vs unconfirmed L/C | Second bank also undertakes to pay | Only issuing bank undertakes | Confirmation removes issuing bank/country risk for the seller. |
| Sight vs usance L/C | Paid on compliant presentation | Paid at a future date (e.g., 90 days after B/L) | Usance gives the buyer credit. |
| Documentary collection vs L/C | Banks handle documents, no payment undertaking | Bank undertakes to pay | Collections are cheaper but riskier for seller. |
| Interest rate vs APR / all-in cost | Nominal rate | Includes fees, compounding | Compare all-in costs. |
| Margin (lending) vs margin (trading) | Credit spread over reference rate | Collateral deposited with broker/exchange | Same word, unrelated meanings. |
| Secured vs senior | Has collateral | Ranks first in priority | Debt can be senior unsecured or secured subordinated. |
| Nostro vs vostro | Our account at another bank | Another bank’s account with us | Point of view. |

## Investment and markets

| Pair | First term | Second term | The practical difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investing vs trading | Long-term ownership for returns | Short-term buying and selling for price moves | Horizon and source of return. |
| Hedging vs speculation | Reduces existing risk | Takes new risk for profit | Same instrument, different purpose. |
| Arbitrage vs speculation | Exploits price differences with little or no net risk | Bets on direction | True arbitrage is rare and small. |
| Forward vs futures | Private (OTC), customised, counterparty risk | Exchange-traded, standardised, cleared, margined | Flexibility vs liquidity and lower counterparty risk. |
| Option vs forward | Right, not obligation; premium paid | Obligation for both parties | Option buyer’s loss limited to premium. |
| Coupon vs yield | Fixed contractual rate on face value | Return based on price paid | Yield moves with market price. |
| Primary vs secondary market | Issuer sells new securities | Investors trade existing securities | Issuer receives cash only in primary. |
| Mutual fund vs ETF | Priced once a day, bought from the fund | Traded on exchange throughout the day | Liquidity, cost, tax treatment vary. |
| Private equity vs venture capital | Mature companies, often leveraged buyouts | Early-stage high-growth companies | Risk profile and use of debt. |
| Volatility vs risk | Statistical fluctuation | Probability of permanent loss / failing objectives | Volatility is one measure of risk. |
| Systematic vs idiosyncratic risk | Market-wide, not diversifiable | Company-specific, diversifiable | Only systematic risk is rewarded in CAPM. |

## Trade, logistics and contracts

| Pair | First term | Second term | The practical difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| FOB vs CIF | Seller delivers on board at origin; buyer arranges freight and insurance | Seller pays freight and minimum insurance to destination; risk still passes at loading | Risk transfer is the same point (on board at origin); cost allocation differs. |
| CIF vs CIP (2020) | Sea only; minimum insurance ICC (C) | Any mode; minimum insurance ICC (A) under 2020 rules | Insurance level and mode. |
| FCA vs FOB | Delivery to carrier (any mode, e.g., container terminal) | Delivery on board vessel | FCA is usually correct for containers. |
| DAP vs DDP | Seller delivers to destination, buyer clears import and pays duties | Seller also pays import duties and taxes | DDP exposes seller to foreign tax and customs. |
| Bill of lading vs sea waybill | Negotiable document of title (if “to order”) | Non-negotiable receipt and contract evidence | Waybill not suitable when banks need control of goods. |
| House B/L vs master B/L | Issued by forwarder/NVOCC to shipper | Issued by carrier to forwarder | Who you can claim against. |
| Commercial invoice vs pro forma invoice | Final invoice for payment and customs | Preliminary for quotation, prepayment, licences | Pro forma is not a demand for payment of a completed sale. |
| Quotation vs offer | Often invitation to negotiate | Binding if accepted within validity | Wording and law decide. |
| Purchase order vs contract | Buyer’s order (becomes contract when accepted) | Negotiated agreement | Battle of the forms: whose terms apply? |
| Agent vs distributor | Acts on principal’s behalf; does not own goods; earns commission | Buys and resells on own account; earns margin | Risk, control and legal protections differ. |
| Broker vs trader | Intermediary matching parties for commission | Principal taking title and price risk | Balance-sheet risk. |
| Demurrage vs detention | Container held inside terminal (typically) | Container held outside terminal (typically) | Carrier definitions vary. |
| Freight forwarder vs carrier vs customs broker | Organises | Transports | Clears customs |
| Warranty vs indemnity | Promise that facts are true; claim for breach | Promise to compensate specific losses | Indemnity usually stronger and more certain. |
| Force majeure vs hardship | Performance prevented | Performance onerous | Suspension vs renegotiation. |
| Arbitration vs litigation | Private, binding, internationally enforceable awards | Public courts | Enforceability and confidentiality. |
| MoU vs contract | Usually non-binding intentions | Binding obligations | Wording and law decide. |
| Condition precedent vs covenant | Must happen before obligation starts | Ongoing promise during the contract | Timing and consequence. |

## Organisation and management

| Pair | First term | Second term | The practical difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shareholder vs stakeholder | Owner | Anyone with an interest | All shareholders are stakeholders, not vice versa. |
| Board vs management | Oversight and strategy | Execution | Separation is the core of governance. |
| Director vs officer | Board member | Appointed executive (CEO, CFO) | One person may be both. |
| KPI vs OKR | Run-the-business metric | Change goal with key results | Health vs ambition. |
| Responsible vs accountable | Does the work | Owns the outcome | Only one accountable person. |
| Strategy vs tactics | Where to play, how to win | Specific actions | Time horizon and level. |
| Efficiency vs effectiveness | Doing things right (less input per output) | Doing the right things | Both needed. |
| Budget vs forecast | Approved plan/target | Latest expectation | Budgets are fixed; forecasts update. |
| Risk vs issue | May happen | Has happened | Different logs and responses. |
| Compliance vs risk management | Follows rules | Manages all risks within appetite | Compliance is one risk area. |

:::ar كيف تستخدم هذا الفصل؟
اقرأ كل صف وحاول أن تشرح الفرق بصوت مرتفع وكأنك في اجتماع عمل. إذا لم تستطع شرح الفرق بمثال عملي، فارجع إلى بطاقة المصطلح في الفصول السابقة أو إلى الفصل المتخصص في الأجزاء اللاحقة. الدقة في التمييز بين هذه المصطلحات هي ما يميّز المهني المتمرّس عن المبتدئ، لأن الخلط بينها قد يؤدي إلى أخطاء في التسعير أو العقود أو تقييم المخاطر.
:::

:::exercise Terminology quiz (Part I review)
Answer each in one or two sentences.
1. A buyer under FOB terms wants the seller to insure the goods. Is the seller obliged to?
2. Your bank offers an overdraft instead of the committed RCF you asked for. What risk are you taking?
3. A supplier offers a “40% margin” deal. Your sales team computes price as cost × 1.40. What is the actual margin?
4. A company reports EBITDA of $5m and negative operating cash flow. Give two possible explanations.
5. Your contract says “Seller shall deliver CIF Aqaba”. A storm damages the goods at sea. Who bears the loss and who claims?
6. Is a provision for a legal claim cash?
7. Explain the difference between an agent and a distributor to a new colleague.
8. “The transaction is subject to due diligence.” What does that mean for the buyer?
9. A guarantee is “payable on first written demand”. Can the bank refuse to pay because the applicant says it was not in breach?
10. Name three things the word “margin” can mean.
:::

:::answer Terminology quiz answers
1. No. Under FOB the seller has no obligation to insure; the buyer insures from loading. The seller may agree to arrange it at the buyer’s cost if contracted.
2. An on-demand overdraft can be withdrawn or reduced by the bank, possibly when you most need it; there is no committed availability.
3. Markup 40% → margin = 0.40 ÷ 1.40 = **28.6%**, not 40%.
4. Large increases in receivables or inventory; tax payments; EBITDA “adjustments” that are cash costs; prepayments to suppliers.
5. Risk passed to the buyer when the goods were loaded on board at the port of shipment; the buyer bears the loss and claims under the insurance policy the seller procured for the buyer’s benefit (and possibly against the carrier).
6. No. It is a non-cash charge recognising an expected outflow; cash leaves when the claim is paid.
7. An agent finds customers and negotiates on the principal’s behalf, earns commission and does not buy the goods; a distributor buys the goods, owns the stock and credit risk, and resells at its own price for a margin.
8. The buyer may withdraw or renegotiate if investigation reveals problems; the offer is not yet final.
9. Generally no: a demand guarantee is independent of the underlying contract; the bank pays against a compliant demand. Exceptions are narrow (e.g., fraud as recognised by the governing law, or court injunctions).
10. Profit ratio (gross/net margin); lending spread over a reference rate; collateral posted with a broker/exchange (initial/variation margin).
:::

:::summary Part I summary
- Business language is precise; similar-sounding terms often have different economic meanings.
- Most confusion comes from mixing accounting measures with cash, contractual concepts with legal effects, and different users’ definitions of the same word.
- Keep the master glossary (Volume 10) at hand and practise using terms in sentences; the phrasebook in Part XVII shows how they sound in professional conversation.
:::
