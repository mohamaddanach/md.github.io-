Treasury is the function that makes sure a company always has the cash it needs, in the right currency, at the right place and time, at the lowest reasonable cost and risk. In international businesses, treasury also manages currency and interest-rate exposures. This part explains the foreign-exchange market from a corporate user’s point of view, the tools and policies of corporate treasury, and — with detailed numerical cases — how importers and exporters manage currency risk.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part XVIII
- Read FX quotes, compute conversions, spreads, cross rates and forward rates.
- Explain spot, forward, NDF, FX swap, cross-currency swap and option instruments and their uses.
- Describe the functions and tools of corporate treasury: liquidity forecasting, cash pooling, netting, funding, bank relationship and fee management.
- Manage interest-rate exposure with swaps and caps.
- Identify transaction, translation and economic FX exposures and design a hedging policy.
- Distinguish corporate treasury from speculative trading and from investment management.
:::

# Foreign Exchange Markets: Quotes, Spot, Forwards and Swaps

:::obj In this chapter
How the FX market is organised, how currencies are quoted, and the instruments corporates use.
:::

## Market structure

The FX market is the world’s largest financial market, traded mostly over the counter (OTC) between banks, corporates, asset managers, central banks and electronic platforms. The BIS Triennial Survey measured average daily turnover of about **$7.5 trillion in April 2022**; a new survey round took place in April 2025 — consult the BIS for the latest figures. The US dollar is on one side of the large majority of trades; major centres include London, New York, Singapore and Hong Kong. Corporates usually deal with their relationship banks by phone, bank portals or multi-bank platforms.

## Quotation conventions

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Currency pair BASE/QUOTE | Price of 1 unit of BASE in QUOTE | EUR/USD 1.0850 → €1 = $1.0850 |
| Bid / ask (offer) | Price at which the dealer buys / sells the base currency | 1.0848 / 1.0852 |
| Spread | Ask − bid; the dealer’s margin and liquidity indicator | 4 pips |
| Pip | Usually the 4th decimal (2nd for JPY pairs) | 0.0001 |
| Direct quote (from a country’s view) | Foreign currency in units of domestic currency | In the UAE: USD 1 = AED 3.6725 |
| Indirect quote | Domestic currency in units of foreign | |
| Cross rate | Rate between two non-USD currencies via USD | EUR/AED = EUR/USD × USD/AED |
| Mid-rate | Average of bid and ask | Used in accounting and benchmarks |

:::warn Read the pair, not just the number
“Rate 3.75” means nothing without the pair. USD/SAR 3.75 = 1 dollar costs 3.75 riyals; SAR/USD = 0.2667. Many costly errors come from multiplying instead of dividing. Always write the conversion with units: SAR 1,000,000 ÷ 3.75 SAR/USD = USD 266,667.
:::

## Spot

A spot transaction exchanges currencies for settlement normally **two business days** after the trade date (T+2) for most pairs — T+1 for some pairs such as USD/CAD. Settlement risk (paying one currency without receiving the other) is mitigated by systems such as **CLS** for eligible currencies.

## Forwards

An **outright forward** fixes today the rate for an exchange at a future date. The forward rate reflects the **interest-rate differential** (covered interest parity), not a forecast.

:::formula Forward rate
F = S × (1 + i_quote × d/basis) ÷ (1 + i_base × d/basis)
The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount.
:::

:::example Forward for an importer
EUR/USD spot 1.0850; 90-day USD rate 4.40%; EUR rate 2.40% (act/360). Forward = 1.0850 × 1.011 ÷ 1.006 = **1.0904**. An importer buying EUR forward pays 1.0904 rather than 1.0850 — not a “cost” in the sense of a fee, but the interest differential: if it bought EUR today and deposited them, it would earn 2.40% instead of 4.40% on its USD.
:::

**Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs)** are cash-settled forwards used for currencies with convertibility restrictions; the difference between the contracted rate and a fixing rate is paid in a convertible currency (usually USD). **Window (flexible) forwards** allow drawdown at any date within a window — useful when payment timing is uncertain, at a slightly worse rate.

## FX swaps and cross-currency swaps

- **FX swap:** a spot (or near-date) exchange combined with a reverse forward exchange. Used to move liquidity between currencies temporarily or to roll a hedge. Example: a company holding surplus USD but needing EUR for one month sells USD/buys EUR spot and buys USD/sells EUR one month forward.
- **Cross-currency (interest-rate) swap:** exchange of principal and interest flows in two currencies over years, e.g., converting a USD bond into synthetic EUR debt to match EUR assets.

## Options

An **FX option** gives the right, not the obligation, to exchange at a strike rate on (or by) an expiry date, for a premium paid upfront.

| Option | Right | Typical user |
|---|---|---|
| Call on currency X | Buy X at strike | Importer paying in X |
| Put on currency X | Sell X at strike | Exporter receiving X |
| Collar (buy one option, sell another) | Range of outcomes, often zero premium | Corporates wanting protection with some participation |
| Participating forward / structured products | Combinations | Use with caution — complexity and leverage |

:::warn Structured FX products
Some banks offer complex products (knock-outs, target redemption forwards, leveraged forwards) with zero upfront premium. They often contain sold options that create large losses when markets move sharply — many companies suffered heavy losses on such products. A corporate hedging policy should list permitted instruments and prohibit leveraged or speculative structures.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. USD/JPY bid 151.20, ask 151.25. A Japanese supplier must be paid JPY 30,000,000. How many USD does the importer need?
2. EUR/USD 1.0820, GBP/USD 1.2650. Compute EUR/GBP.
3. GBP/USD spot 1.2650; 180-day USD rate 4.3% (act/360), GBP rate 4.6% (act/365). Compute the forward.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. The bank sells JPY = buys USD at the bid: USD = 30,000,000 ÷ 151.20 = **$198,413**. (Bank buys base USD at bid; the importer sells USD, so it gets 151.20 JPY per USD.)
2. EUR/GBP = EUR/USD ÷ GBP/USD = 1.0820 ÷ 1.2650 = **0.8553**.
3. F = 1.2650 × (1 + 0.043 × 180/360) ÷ (1 + 0.046 × 180/365) = 1.2650 × 1.0215 ÷ 1.022685 = **1.2635** (GBP at a slight forward discount).
:::

# Corporate Treasury Management

:::obj In this chapter
What treasury does, liquidity forecasting, cash concentration and pooling, netting, funding and interest-rate risk management, bank relationship and fee management, treasury policy, and the difference between treasury, speculation and investment management.
:::

## Treasury functions

| Function | Activities | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Cash and liquidity management | Daily positioning, forecasting, pooling, investing surplus cash | Cash position, 13-week and 12-month forecasts |
| Funding | Bank facilities, bonds, leasing, intra-group loans | Funding plan, maturity profile |
| Financial risk management | FX, interest rate, commodity, counterparty risk | Hedging programme, exposure reports |
| Bank relationship management | Bank selection, wallet allocation, KYC, fee review | Bank scorecard |
| Payments and operations | Payment factory, bank connectivity, controls | Secure, efficient payments |
| Corporate finance support | M&A funding, dividends, capital structure | Analysis for CFO/board |
| Guarantees and trade finance | L/C and guarantee issuance, limits | Instrument register |

## Liquidity forecasting

| Horizon | Method | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Daily / weekly (0–13 weeks) | Direct (receipts and payments) | Liquidity, short-term investment and borrowing |
| Monthly (to 12–18 months) | Indirect from budget/forecast + working-capital drivers | Facility sizing, covenant forecasts |
| Long term | Strategic plan | Refinancing, capital structure |

Forecast accuracy is measured by comparing forecast vs actual by category; persistent bias (e.g., receipts always over-estimated) is corrected at source with sales and credit teams.

## Cash concentration, pooling and netting

**Physical cash pooling (zero-balancing):** balances of subsidiary accounts are swept daily into a header account; deficits funded from it. Creates real intercompany loans (with tax and legal implications such as transfer pricing and thin-capitalisation rules).

**Notional pooling:** balances remain in separate accounts but the bank calculates interest on the net position. Availability is restricted in some jurisdictions and affected by bank capital rules.

**Multilateral netting:** intercompany payables and receivables are offset, so each subsidiary pays or receives one net amount per period.

:::example Netting four subsidiaries (USD m, monthly intercompany invoices)
| Payer → payee | Amount |
|---|---|
| A → B | 4.0 |
| B → C | 3.0 |
| C → A | 2.5 |
| D → A | 1.0 |
| B → D | 1.5 |
| C → D | 0.5 |
Gross transfers: 6 payments, $12.5m. Net positions: A: receives 2.5 + 1.0 − pays 4.0 = **−0.5**; B: +4.0 − 3.0 − 1.5 = **−0.5**; C: +3.0 − 2.5 − 0.5 = **0.0**; D: +1.5 + 0.5 − 1.0 = **+1.0**. After netting: A and B each pay $0.5m to the netting centre, which pays D $1.0m — **3 payments, $2.0m**. FX conversions and bank fees fall accordingly.
:::

**In-house bank:** a central treasury entity that holds subsidiaries’ cash, lends to them, executes FX for them and makes external payments on their behalf (“payments on behalf of”).

## Funding and interest-rate risk

Treasury maintains a funding plan: sources (bank facilities, bonds, leases, supplier finance), maturity profile (avoid concentrations), covenant headroom, and liquidity buffer (cash + undrawn committed facilities, often targeted at a multiple of monthly outflows).

:::example Hedging floating-rate debt with a swap
A company has a $30m 5-year term loan at 3M reference rate + 2.00%. It enters a 5-year **pay-fixed interest-rate swap** at 3.90%: it pays 3.90% fixed and receives the 3M reference rate on $30m.
- Net cost = (reference + 2.00%) − reference + 3.90% = **5.90% fixed**, regardless of rate moves.
- If the reference rate rises to 5.5%, unhedged cost would be 7.5%; savings ≈ 1.6% × $30m = $480,000 a year.
- If rates fall to 2.5%, the company still pays 5.90% (vs 4.5% unhedged) — the swap has negative value, and early termination would cost money.
Alternatives: an **interest-rate cap** (pay a premium; protected above the cap strike; benefits if rates fall), or a mix (hedge 50–75% of debt).
:::

## Bank relationship and fee management

Treasurers allocate ancillary business to banks that provide credit, monitor **bank fees** (account maintenance, payment fees, FX margins, L/C and guarantee commissions, cash handling), use **bank fee analysis statements** (e.g., in standard electronic formats) and periodically run **RFPs** for cash management. FX execution costs are often hidden in spreads — measuring them against market mid-rates at execution time can reveal significant savings.

:::example Hidden FX margin
A company converts $40m a year into EUR at an average of 30 pips above the market mid-rate (≈ 0.28% at 1.08). Cost ≈ **$110,000 a year**. A multi-bank platform with competitive quotes reduced the average margin to 6 pips, saving about **$88,000**.
:::

## Payment timing

Treasury optimises timing within contractual terms: pay on the due date (not earlier) unless a discount justifies it; batch payment runs; align large payments with receipts; manage value dates and cut-offs; consider holiday calendars for both currencies.

## Treasury policy

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Objectives | E.g., ensure liquidity, protect margins from financial risk, minimise cost of funds — **not** to generate speculative profits |
| Governance | Board/committee approval, roles, delegation limits, reporting |
| Liquidity | Minimum liquidity buffer, forecasting requirements |
| Funding | Approved sources, maturity limits, covenant headroom targets |
| FX risk | Exposures to hedge, hedge ratios by horizon, permitted instruments, budget rates |
| Interest-rate risk | Target fixed/floating mix |
| Counterparty risk | Minimum bank ratings, limits per bank for deposits and derivatives |
| Investments | Permitted instruments (deposits, money-market funds, T-bills), tenors, concentration |
| Controls | Segregation of dealing, confirmation and settlement; authorised signatories; systems |
| Reporting | Daily positions, monthly exposure and hedge reports, policy breaches |

## Treasury vs speculation vs investment management

| | Corporate treasury | Speculative trading | Investment management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Objective | Reduce risk and cost for the operating business | Profit from market movements | Achieve returns on capital within a mandate |
| Starting point | An existing commercial exposure | No exposure needed | Investor capital |
| Typical instruments | Forwards, swaps, plain options, deposits | Any, including leveraged | Securities, funds, derivatives within mandate |
| Success measure | Predictability of margins and cash; cost; compliance with policy | Trading P&L | Risk-adjusted return vs benchmark |
| Danger sign | Hedges larger than exposures; “view-taking” | — | Style drift, excessive risk |

:::ar الخزينة ليست مضاربة
هدف إدارة الخزينة في الشركة هو حماية الهوامش والتدفقات النقدية من تقلبات العملات وأسعار الفائدة وضمان السيولة بأقل تكلفة، وليس تحقيق أرباح من التوقعات. لذلك يجب أن يرتبط كل تحوّط بتعرّض تجاري قائم، وأن تحدد سياسة الخزينة الأدوات المسموح بها ونسب التحوط والحدود والصلاحيات. أما المضاربة فهي اتخاذ مراكز جديدة بهدف الربح، وإدارة الاستثمارات تسعى إلى عائد على رأس المال ضمن تفويض استثماري محدد.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Subsidiaries owe each other: X→Y $5m; Y→Z $2m; Z→X $4m; Y→X $1m. Compute net positions and the number/value of payments after netting.
2. A $50m floating loan at reference + 1.75% is 60% hedged with a pay-fixed swap at 4.10%. Reference rate is 5.0%. Compute the all-in average cost.
3. Which of these are inconsistent with a hedging policy: (a) buying a forward to cover a firm EUR purchase order; (b) selling a GBP forward because the treasurer expects GBP to fall, with no GBP exposure; (c) buying a cap on 50% of floating debt?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. X: +4 + 1 − 5 = **0**; Y: +5 − 2 − 1 = **+2**; Z: +2 − 4 = **−2**. After netting: Z pays $2m to Y (via the centre): **1 payment, $2m** (instead of 4 payments, $12m).
2. Hedged 30m: 4.10% + 1.75% = 5.85%; unhedged 20m: 5.0% + 1.75% = 6.75%. Average = (30 × 5.85 + 20 × 6.75) ÷ 50 = **6.21%**.
3. (b) is speculation (no exposure). (a) and (c) are consistent.
:::

# Managing Currency Risk in International Trade

:::obj In this chapter
Types of FX exposure, measuring exposure, hedging strategies for importers and exporters with complete numerical cases, budget rates and layered hedging, natural hedges and contract clauses, and an introduction to hedge accounting.
:::

## Three kinds of exposure

| Exposure | Definition | Example | Typical management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transaction | Known future cash flows in foreign currency | EUR invoice due in 90 days | Forwards, options, natural hedges |
| Translation (accounting) | Effect of retranslating foreign subsidiaries’ statements | EUR subsidiary’s equity reported in USD | Often not hedged; net investment hedges |
| Economic (operating) | Long-term effect of currency moves on competitiveness and cash flows | Competitors source in a cheaper currency | Strategic: sourcing, pricing, location |

**Exposure lifecycle:** price list/quotation issued (contingent exposure) → order confirmed (firm commitment) → invoice (receivable/payable) → payment. Hedging policies often hedge a percentage of **forecast** exposures and 100% of **firm** commitments.

## Case A: an importer paying EUR

Gulf Machinery Trading (fictional, Dubai; functional currency AED, pegged to USD) must pay **EUR 2,000,000 in 90 days** for equipment it has already sold to a client at a fixed AED price based on a budget rate of EUR/USD 1.10 (cost budget $2.2m). Market: spot 1.0850; 90-day forward 1.0904; a 90-day EUR call option with strike 1.1000 costs 1.2% of the USD notional at strike (~$26,400); a zero-premium collar (buy EUR call at 1.1100, sell EUR put at 1.0600).

| Strategy | Spot at payment 1.0300 | Spot 1.0900 | Spot 1.1500 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unhedged | $2,060,000 | $2,180,000 | $2,300,000 |
| Forward at 1.0904 | $2,180,800 | $2,180,800 | $2,180,800 |
| Call option strike 1.10 (+ premium $26,400) | $2,086,400 (let option lapse) | $2,206,400 | $2,226,400 (exercise) |
| Collar 1.0600–1.1100 (zero premium) | $2,120,000 (floor binds) | $2,180,000 | $2,220,000 (cap binds) |
| vs budget $2,200,000 | | | |

Analysis:
- **Forward** locks the cost at $2,180,800 — below budget; no upside if the EUR weakens.
- **Option** caps the cost at $2,226,400 (above budget by the premium) but retains the full benefit of a weaker EUR — expensive insurance.
- **Collar** gives a range of $2.12–2.22m with no premium.
- Because the sale price is already fixed, the company’s goal is **certainty of margin** → the forward is the natural choice for this firm commitment. Options suit uncertain exposures (e.g., a tender not yet won).

## Case B: an exporter receiving EUR (pegged-currency exporter)

Levant Pharma (fictional, Jordan; JOD pegged to USD at about 0.709 JOD per USD) sells medicines to a European distributor: **EUR 1,500,000 receivable in 120 days**. Costs are in JOD. Budget rate EUR/USD 1.07. Market: spot 1.0850; USD 120-day rate 4.40%, EUR 2.40% (act/360).

- Forward = 1.0850 × (1 + 0.044 × 120/360) ÷ (1 + 0.024 × 120/360) = 1.0850 × 1.014667 ÷ 1.008 = **1.0922**.
- Selling EUR 1.5m forward → **USD 1,638,300** → at 0.709 JOD/USD ≈ **JOD 1,161,555**.
- At the budget rate: USD 1,605,000 → JOD 1,137,945. The forward secures **JOD 23,610 above budget**.
- If unhedged and EUR/USD fell to 1.02: USD 1,530,000 → JOD 1,084,770 — **JOD 76,785 below the forward outcome**.

The exporter benefits from the interest differential (EUR at a forward premium vs USD in this rate environment). In environments where the exporter’s currency has much higher interest rates than the invoice currency, forward points work against the exporter — e.g., an exporter in a high-interest-rate country selling forward USD receivables receives a much better forward rate than spot in local-currency terms, while an importer there pays a large forward premium. Interest differentials can make hedging appear “expensive” to importers in high-rate currencies.

## Layered hedging and budget rates

Companies with recurring flows hedge in layers to smooth rates:

| Horizon | Hedge ratio of forecast exposure (example policy) |
|---|---|
| 0–3 months | 75–100% |
| 4–6 months | 50–75% |
| 7–12 months | 25–50% |
| > 12 months | 0–25% (only with approval) |

Each month, treasury adds hedges so that coverage reaches the target ratios. The **budget rate** is set from forward rates at budget time; achieved rates are reported against it. This avoids all-or-nothing timing decisions.

## Natural hedges and commercial tools

| Tool | How it reduces exposure |
|---|---|
| Invoice in your own currency | Shifts risk to the counterparty (affects competitiveness and price) |
| Match currency of costs and revenue | E.g., buy inputs in EUR if you sell in EUR |
| Foreign-currency borrowing | Debt in the currency of revenues (also a hedge of net investment) |
| Price-adjustment / currency clauses | Price adjusts if the rate moves beyond a band |
| Shorter quotation validity | Less time for rates to move before acceptance |
| Leading and lagging payments | Timing intra-group payments (subject to tax and regulatory limits) |
| Local sourcing / production | Reduces economic exposure long term |

:::example Currency clause in a supply contract
“Prices are based on an exchange rate of EUR/USD 1.0850. If the ECB reference rate on the invoice date differs by more than 3% from this rate, the price in USD shall be adjusted by the difference exceeding 3%.” Both parties share small movements; large moves are passed through.
:::

## Hedge accounting (introduction)

Derivatives are measured at fair value, which can create profit-and-loss volatility before the hedged transaction occurs. **Hedge accounting** (IFRS 9; ASC 815 under US GAAP) allows companies to align the timing of gains and losses on hedges with the hedged items — e.g., in a **cash flow hedge**, effective fair-value changes go to other comprehensive income and are recycled when the hedged purchase affects profit. It requires formal designation, documentation and an economic relationship between hedge and hedged item. Without it, a perfectly sensible hedge can make quarterly profits look volatile.

:::warn Common FX risk-management mistakes
- Hedging based on a view (“the euro will fall”) rather than on policy.
- Over-hedging forecasts that do not materialise, creating speculative positions.
- Ignoring the timing mismatch between payment dates and hedge maturities.
- Buying complex zero-cost structures with hidden leverage.
- Measuring success by “did we beat spot?” — the objective is reducing uncertainty, not winning bets.
- Forgetting counterparty limits and collateral requirements on derivatives.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. An importer must pay USD 500,000 in 60 days; its home currency is EGP (assume spot USD/EGP 49.00; 60-day forward 50.80). Explain why the forward is higher than spot and what the importer should consider.
2. A Turkish exporter receives EUR monthly (EUR 3m per month). Design a 12-month layered hedging programme using the example ratios above.
3. In Case A, what would the company pay if it hedged 50% with the forward and left 50% unhedged, and the spot at payment is 1.15?
4. Why is translation exposure often left unhedged?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. EGP interest rates are much higher than USD rates, so the forward includes the interest differential (covered interest parity). The importer pays more than spot but locks its cost; alternatives are buying USD now (if available and if it has EGP liquidity) and holding it, negotiating EGP pricing, or reducing exposure. In markets with FX shortages, access to USD at maturity can be a bigger risk than the rate itself.
2. At any time: months 1–3 hedged 75–100% (EUR 6.75–9m), months 4–6 50–75% (EUR 4.5–6.75m), months 7–12 25–50% (EUR 4.5–9m). Each month, add forwards for the newly entering month(s) to restore ratios. Report average achieved rate vs budget monthly.
3. 50% forward: EUR 1m × 1.0904 = $1,090,400; 50% at spot: EUR 1m × 1.15 = $1,150,000 → total **$2,240,400**.
4. It affects reported equity (through OCI), not cash flows in the short term; hedging it uses cash (collateral, settlements) to protect accounting values; and long-term assets abroad are often naturally financed or hedged by local borrowing.
:::

:::summary Part XVIII summary
- FX quotes must be read with the base currency in mind; forwards reflect interest differentials, not forecasts.
- Treasury ensures liquidity, manages funding and financial risks, controls bank relationships and costs.
- Pooling, netting and in-house banking reduce idle cash, transfers and fees.
- Interest-rate swaps and caps convert or limit floating-rate exposure.
- Transaction exposures should be hedged according to a written policy; forwards suit firm commitments, options suit uncertain exposures.
- Corporate treasury reduces risk; it must not become a speculative trading desk.
:::
