Banks sit at the centre of almost every commercial transaction: they hold companies’ cash, move their payments, lend to them, guarantee their obligations, finance their trade and help them raise capital. Understanding how a bank thinks — what it earns, what it fears, what its regulators require — lets you negotiate better, prepare better credit applications, and design transactions that banks can support.

This part builds the banking system from the ground up, then follows a corporate client through credit analysis, facility structuring, payments, compliance and specialised finance. It ends with a classification of all major bank products by commercial purpose. Trade-finance instruments (letters of credit, collections, guarantees in trade) are introduced here and explained in full in Part VII.

# The Financial System and the Types of Banks

:::obj In this chapter
The functions of the financial system, the types of banks and their clients, non-bank financial institutions, and Islamic banking.
:::

## What the financial system does

1. **Payments:** moving money between parties safely and quickly.
2. **Intermediation:** channelling savings to borrowers, transforming maturities (short-term deposits into long-term loans), sizes and risks.
3. **Risk management:** insurance, hedging, diversification, guarantees.
4. **Price discovery:** markets set interest rates, exchange rates, security prices.
5. **Monitoring and information:** lenders and investors assess and monitor borrowers, reducing information problems.

## Types of banks

| Type | Main clients | Core activities | How it earns | Examples of roles in a deal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central bank | Government, banks | Monetary policy, currency issue, lender of last resort, payment-system operator, often supervisor | Seigniorage, reserve returns (not profit-driven) | Sets policy rate; runs the RTGS system |
| Commercial bank | Individuals, SMEs, corporates | Deposits, loans, payments, trade finance, FX | Net interest income, fees, FX margins | Lends to an importer; issues an L/C |
| Retail banking (division) | Individuals, small businesses | Current/savings accounts, cards, mortgages, personal loans | Spread, fees, card interchange | Payroll accounts for staff |
| Corporate / commercial banking (division) | Mid-size and large companies | Credit facilities, cash management, trade finance, FX, hedging | Interest, fees, cross-sell | Relationship manager for a trading company |
| Investment bank | Corporates, governments, institutional investors | M&A advisory, underwriting equity and bonds, sales and trading, research | Advisory and underwriting fees, trading revenue | Advises on acquisition; arranges a bond issue |
| Private bank / wealth management | High-net-worth individuals and families | Investment management, credit secured on portfolios, estate structuring | Asset-based fees, lending spreads | Manages the owner’s personal wealth |
| Correspondent bank (role) | Other banks | Accounts and payment services for banks in other countries/currencies | Fees, balances | Clears USD payments for a regional bank |
| Development bank / DFI | Governments, projects, private sector in developing countries | Long-term loans, guarantees, equity | Policy-driven returns | Co-finances a power plant (e.g., IFC, regional development banks) |
| Export credit agency (ECA) | Exporters, banks financing exports | Insurance, guarantees, direct loans supporting exports | Premiums | Covers a bank loan to a foreign buyer |
| Islamic bank | Individuals, corporates | Sharia-compliant deposits and financing (murabaha, ijara, musharaka, sukuk) | Profit margins on sale/lease contracts, fees | Murabaha financing of inventory |

Universal banks combine commercial and investment banking; some jurisdictions separate or ring-fence them (e.g., ring-fencing of UK retail banks).

## Non-bank financial institutions

Insurance companies, pension funds, asset managers, hedge funds, private-equity and private-credit funds, finance companies and leasing companies, factoring companies, microfinance institutions, payment institutions and electronic-money issuers, exchanges and clearing houses, broker-dealers. **Private credit** funds have grown into major lenders to mid-sized companies, particularly in leveraged buyouts.

## Islamic banking essentials

Islamic finance prohibits riba (interest), excessive uncertainty (gharar) and financing prohibited activities; returns must be linked to trade, leasing or partnership.

| Product | Structure | Conventional analogue |
|---|---|---|
| Murabaha | Bank buys an asset and sells it to the client at cost plus disclosed profit, payable in instalments | Inventory / trade finance, term loan |
| Ijara | Bank buys and leases an asset to the client (possibly with ownership transfer at the end) | Lease / asset finance |
| Musharaka / diminishing musharaka | Partnership; client buys out the bank’s share over time | Equity / home finance |
| Mudaraba | One party provides capital, the other management; profits shared, losses borne by capital provider | Investment account, fund |
| Wakala | Agency arrangement for investment | Deposit / treasury placement |
| Tawarruq (commodity murabaha) | Buying and selling commodities to generate cash financing | Cash loan, liquidity management |
| Sukuk | Certificates representing ownership in assets or ventures, generating returns | Bonds |

Sharia supervisory boards approve products; standards are issued by bodies such as AAOIFI and the IFSB. In credit analysis, Islamic facilities are analysed like conventional ones (cash flow, collateral, structure), with attention to legal enforceability and documentation.

# How Banks Make Money: The Bank Balance Sheet, Deposits, Lending and Relationship Management

:::obj In this chapter
The structure of a bank balance sheet and income statement, net interest margin and fee income, the economics of a lending relationship, and how relationship management works.
:::

## A bank’s balance sheet

| Assets | % (illustrative) | Liabilities and equity | % (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash and central-bank reserves | 8 | Customer deposits (current, savings, term) | 72 |
| Interbank placements | 5 | Interbank borrowings | 6 |
| Investment securities (government bonds etc.) | 20 | Debt securities issued | 7 |
| Loans and advances (net of provisions) | 62 | Other liabilities | 3 |
| Fixed and other assets | 5 | Subordinated debt / AT1 capital | 2 |
| | | Shareholders’ equity | 10 |
| **Total** | **100** | **Total** | **100** |

A bank is highly leveraged: equity may be ~10% of assets. A 1% loss on assets destroys ~10% of equity. That is why banks care so much about credit losses, and why regulators require capital and liquidity buffers.

## A bank’s income statement

| Line | Driver |
|---|---|
| Interest income | Loans, securities, placements × yields |
| Interest expense | Deposits, borrowings × funding costs |
| **Net interest income (NII)** | Volume × net interest margin |
| Fee and commission income | Payments, trade finance, cards, advisory, asset management |
| FX and trading income | Client FX margins, hedging, market-making |
| **Operating income** | |
| Operating expenses | Staff, technology, premises |
| **Pre-provision profit** | |
| Impairment charges (expected credit losses) | Credit quality |
| **Profit before tax** | |

Key ratios: **net interest margin** (NII ÷ average interest-earning assets), **cost-to-income** (operating expenses ÷ operating income), **cost of risk** (impairment charges ÷ average loans), **ROE**, **loan-to-deposit ratio**, **NPL ratio** and **coverage**, capital ratios.

## The economics of a corporate loan

:::example Return on a $10m corporate loan (fictional, simplified)
| Item | Annual | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Interest at margin 2.25% over funding | +225,000 | Bank funds at reference rate (simplification) |
| Upfront fee 0.75% amortised over 3 years | +25,000 | |
| Expected loss: PD 1.0% × LGD 40% × EAD 10m | −40,000 | Cost of risk |
| Operating cost allocation | −30,000 | Relationship, credit, operations |
| **Pre-tax risk-adjusted income** | **180,000** | |
| Tax 20% | −36,000 | |
| **Net income** | **144,000** | |
| Capital required: risk weight 100% × 10m × 12.5% capital ratio | 1,250,000 | Regulatory capital allocated |
| **Return on allocated capital (RAROC-style)** | **11.5%** | vs cost of equity, say 12% |

The loan alone barely covers the bank’s cost of equity. This is why banks seek **cross-sell**: payments, payroll, trade finance, FX and hedging add fee income with little extra capital. A client who borrows from a bank but keeps all its transactional business elsewhere is unattractive to that bank.
:::

## Deposits and funding

Deposits are the cheapest and most stable funding, especially operational current accounts used for payments. Term deposits and wholesale funding cost more and are less stable. **Liquidity regulation** (LCR, NSFR) gives banks incentives to attract stable deposits and match long-term assets with stable funding. For corporates, this explains why banks ask for “operating accounts” and “wallet share” in return for credit.

## Relationship management

The **relationship manager (RM)** is the client’s main contact and the bank’s commercial owner of the relationship. The RM:

- Understands the client’s business, strategy and needs.
- Prepares credit applications and coordinates with credit risk, product specialists (trade, cash management, treasury) and compliance.
- Monitors the client: financials, covenants, account conduct, industry news.
- Prices and negotiates within the bank’s policies.
- Manages the annual review and renewals.

:::tip How to work with your bank as a corporate treasurer or CFO
- Give the RM a clear story: business model, strategy, numbers, risks and mitigants. RMs must “sell” your credit internally to a credit committee; make it easy for them.
- Provide information early and consistently: management accounts, forecasts, ageing reports, covenant certificates.
- Concentrate transactional business with your core lenders in proportion to their credit support.
- Never surprise your bank. If a covenant breach is coming, tell them before the test date with a plan.
- Maintain relationships with at least two or three banks to avoid dependency.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A bank’s NII is $900m on average interest-earning assets of $36bn; operating income $1.3bn; operating expenses $520m; impairments $180m on loans of $24bn. Compute NIM, cost-to-income and cost of risk.
2. Why might a bank offer a lower margin on a loan to a company that moves its payroll and trade finance to it?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. NIM = 900 ÷ 36,000 = **2.5%**; cost-to-income = 520 ÷ 1,300 = **40%**; cost of risk = 180 ÷ 24,000 = **0.75%**.
2. Ancillary business brings fee income and low-cost deposits with little additional capital, raising the return on the capital allocated to the relationship; the bank can accept a thinner lending margin while achieving its target return.
:::

# Corporate Credit Analysis: How a Bank Decides

:::obj In this chapter
The credit process from application to approval; the analytical frameworks banks use; risk ratings; structuring; and a complete fictional credit memorandum.
:::

## The credit process

1. **Origination:** RM identifies the need; preliminary discussion of amount, purpose, structure.
2. **KYC and compliance onboarding** (for new clients) — no credit without completed KYC.
3. **Information gathering:** audited financials (3 years), management accounts, forecasts, business plan, ageing reports, bank statements, list of existing facilities, collateral details, contracts.
4. **Analysis:** business and industry risk, management, financial analysis, cash-flow and repayment capacity, collateral, structure.
5. **Risk rating:** internal rating (obligor rating, e.g., 1–10 or AAA–C equivalents) and facility rating considering security.
6. **Credit memo** prepared by RM and/or credit analyst; recommendation.
7. **Approval:** by authorised credit officers or committee depending on amount and risk (delegated authority).
8. **Documentation:** facility letter or agreement, security documents, legal opinions; **conditions precedent** satisfied.
9. **Disbursement / utilisation.**
10. **Monitoring:** covenant compliance, financial reviews, early-warning indicators, annual review; watch-list and remedial management if needed.

## Frameworks: the Cs of credit

| C | Question | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Are the owners and managers honest, competent and committed? | Track record, references, bank conduct, credit bureau, transparency |
| Capacity | Can the business repay from cash flow? | Historical and projected cash flow, DSCR, leverage, coverage |
| Capital | How much of their own money is at risk? | Equity, tangible net worth, shareholder support |
| Collateral | What secondary source of repayment exists? | Security value, enforceability, liquidity, haircuts |
| Conditions | What external factors affect the borrower and the loan? | Industry, economy, purpose of the loan, terms |

Many banks use additional frameworks such as **CAMPARI** (Character, Ability, Margin, Purpose, Amount, Repayment, Insurance) or **PARSER**. All converge on the same logic: **the primary source of repayment is cash flow from the business; collateral is the secondary source.**

:::warn A bank does not lend on collateral alone
A loan that can only be repaid by selling the collateral is a bad loan, even if fully secured: enforcement is slow, costly, uncertain and damages the relationship and the bank’s reputation. Credit officers first ask “how will this loan be repaid in the normal course of business?”
:::

## Analysing repayment capacity by purpose

| Purpose | Repayment source | Key analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Working capital (overdraft, RCF) | Conversion of inventory and receivables into cash | CCC, borrowing base, seasonality |
| Trade finance (import loan) | Sale of the specific goods financed | Transaction-level: buyer, goods, price risk, documents |
| Capex (term loan) | Future operating cash flows | Project returns, DSCR, debt tenor vs asset life |
| Acquisition | Combined group cash flow | Pro forma leverage, synergies, integration risk |
| Refinancing | Same as original purpose; why refinance? | Is the borrower restructuring problems? |
| Dividend recapitalisation | Future cash flows | Weakens creditor position; strict covenants |

## Risk rating and pricing

Banks map obligors to a **probability of default (PD)** through scoring models (financial ratios, qualitative factors) and expert judgement. Facility-level **loss given default (LGD)** depends on seniority and collateral. Pricing considers expected loss, capital consumption, funding cost, operating cost and relationship value (as in the RAROC example in the previous chapter).

# Credit Facilities, Collateral, Guarantees and Covenants

:::obj In this chapter
Each major credit facility explained with its commercial purpose; secured and unsecured lending; types of collateral and their haircuts; guarantees; covenants and pricing; syndicated lending; and how to negotiate a facility.
:::

## Matching facility to purpose

| Need | Appropriate facility | Inappropriate choice | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily timing differences | Overdraft, RCF | Term loan | Flexibility needed |
| Seasonal inventory build | RCF, borrowing base, import loans | Overdraft only | Peak needs committed funding |
| Import of goods | L/C line + import loans/trust receipts | Term loan | Self-liquidating trade cycle |
| Customer pays at 90 days | Receivables finance, invoice discounting, factoring | Long-term loan | Linked to the receivable |
| Equipment purchase | Term loan, leasing, asset finance | Overdraft | Long-lived asset |
| Acquisition | Acquisition term loan, bridge, bonds, equity | RCF (alone) | Long-term, larger risk |
| Tender or contract performance | Bank guarantee line | Cash deposit | Preserve liquidity |
| Project with long construction | Project finance | Corporate overdraft | Ring-fenced risk and long tenor |

## Facility types in detail

### Overdraft

Flexible, priced at reference rate + margin on daily drawn balances, plus possibly an arrangement fee. Usually **repayable on demand** and reviewed annually. Banks dislike “hard-core” overdrafts that never swing into credit — a sign of long-term funding needs disguised as working capital.

### Revolving credit facility (RCF)

**Committed** for an availability period (1–5 years). Fees: arrangement/upfront fee, **commitment fee** on undrawn amounts (often 30–40% of the margin), margin on drawn amounts, sometimes **utilisation fees** when usage exceeds thresholds. Each drawing (“loan”) has an interest period (1, 3, 6 months) and is repaid and redrawn. Conditions to each drawing: no default, repeating representations true.

### Term loans

Fixed amount, specific purpose. Structure elements: availability period (to draw), grace period, repayment profile (equal instalments, sculpted to cash flow, balloon, bullet), voluntary prepayment rights (and break costs on fixed-rate loans), mandatory prepayments (asset sale proceeds, excess cash flow sweep in leveraged loans).

### Syndicated and club loans

Large facilities shared among lenders under one agreement (often based on Loan Market Association templates in EMEA). Roles: mandated lead arranger(s), bookrunner, facility agent (administers payments and communications), security agent (holds security for all lenders). Lenders act by **majority lenders** decisions (often 66⅔%) except for matters requiring all-lender consent (amount, pricing, maturity, release of key security).

### Asset finance and leasing

The asset itself secures the financing; lessors may own the asset. Useful for vehicles, equipment, aircraft, ships. Under IFRS 16 lessees show most leases on the balance sheet.

## Secured vs unsecured lending

| | Unsecured | Secured |
|---|---|---|
| Lender’s recourse | General claim on all assets, ranking with other unsecured creditors | Priority claim on specific assets |
| Typical borrowers | Strong, rated companies; investment-grade | SMEs, mid-caps, leveraged borrowers |
| Pricing | Depends on credit quality | Lower than equivalent unsecured for weaker borrowers |
| Protections | Negative pledge, pari passu, covenants | Plus security documents, registrations |

## Types of collateral

| Collateral | How taken (varies by law) | Typical advance / haircut | Issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash deposit | Pledge / lien / set-off | ~100% (same currency) | Simple; ties up liquidity |
| Real estate | Mortgage registered at land registry | 50–70% LTV | Valuation, enforcement time, legal title |
| Receivables | Assignment (notified or not) | 70–90% of eligible | Dilution, disputes, concentration, set-off |
| Inventory | Pledge, possessory pledge with collateral manager, warehouse receipts | 40–70% | Perishability, price volatility, control, fraud |
| Equipment / vehicles | Chattel mortgage / registered charge | 40–70% | Depreciation, mobility |
| Listed shares | Pledge over securities account | 30–70% depending on liquidity | Market volatility, margin calls |
| Shares of a private company | Share pledge | Enforcement means controlling the company | Valuation, minority rights |
| Ships / aircraft | Registered mortgage | 50–70% | Specialist valuation and registries |
| Insurance proceeds | Assignment, loss-payee endorsement | — | Coverage adequacy |
| Contract rights | Assignment of contract proceeds | — | Counterparty consent |

**Perfection** (registration, notification, possession) makes security effective against third parties; **priority** determines who is paid first. These depend entirely on the governing law and the location of the assets — banks use local counsel and legal opinions.

## Guarantees and other credit support

- **Personal guarantee** by owners: common for SMEs; aligns incentives; value depends on owners’ assets.
- **Corporate guarantee** by a parent or group companies (upstream, downstream, cross-stream): critical where cash and assets are spread across a group.
- **Bank guarantee / SBLC** from another bank: substitutes the bank’s credit for the borrower’s.
- **Comfort letter / letter of awareness:** usually not legally binding; limited value.
- **Keepwell agreement:** parent undertakes to maintain the subsidiary’s solvency — enforceability varies.
- **Credit insurance / ECA cover:** transfers part of the risk to an insurer.
- **Subordination of shareholder loans:** owners agree their loans rank after the bank.

## Covenants

| Type | Examples | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Financial maintenance | Leverage, interest cover, DSCR, minimum net worth, current ratio, capex limits | Early warning; renegotiation trigger |
| Incurrence (bonds, some loans) | Tested only when taking an action (e.g., new debt) | Control actions, not performance |
| Positive (affirmative) | Provide financial statements, maintain insurance, comply with laws, pay taxes | Information and preservation |
| Negative | No further security (negative pledge), no disposals, no new debt, no change of business, no mergers, dividend restrictions | Protect lender’s position |
| Information | Monthly/quarterly reports, compliance certificates, borrowing-base reports | Monitoring |

**Events of default:** non-payment, covenant breach (after any cure period), misrepresentation, cross-default (default on other debt), insolvency events, material adverse change (debated), change of control, audit qualification, judgments above thresholds. Upon an event of default, lenders may **accelerate** the loan, cancel commitments and enforce security — but in practice usually negotiate a **waiver** or **amendment** (with a fee and tighter terms).

:::tip Negotiating covenants as a borrower
- Set covenant levels with **headroom** of 25–35% versus your downside budget, not your base case.
- Agree **definitions** carefully: EBITDA (adjustments allowed, pro forma for acquisitions, pre- or post-IFRS 16), net debt (which items, cash netting).
- Seek **equity cure** rights (shareholders can inject equity to cure a breach) and **cure periods**.
- Prefer **semi-annual** testing on rolling 12-month figures for seasonal businesses.
- Avoid cross-default to small, unrelated obligations (set a threshold).
:::

## Pricing a facility

All-in cost = reference rate + margin + fees (arrangement, commitment, utilisation, agency) + costs (legal, valuation, registration) + compensating requirements (minimum balances, hedging with the bank) + break costs. Compare offers on an **all-in annualised basis** at expected utilisation.

:::example Comparing two RCF offers ($20m, 3 years, expected average utilisation 40%)
| | Bank A | Bank B |
|---|---|---|
| Margin | 2.10% | 1.90% |
| Commitment fee (on undrawn) | 35% of margin = 0.735% | 40% of margin = 0.76% |
| Upfront fee | 0.50% flat | 1.00% flat |
| Annual cost (excluding reference rate) | Drawn 8m × 2.10% = 168,000; undrawn 12m × 0.735% = 88,200; upfront 100,000 ÷ 3 = 33,333 → **289,533** | 8m × 1.90% = 152,000; 12m × 0.76% = 91,200; 200,000 ÷ 3 = 66,667 → **309,867** |

Bank A is cheaper despite the higher margin; at 80% utilisation, Bank B becomes cheaper (A: 336,000 + 29,400 + 33,333 = 398,733; B: 304,000 + 30,400 + 66,667 = 401,067 — nearly equal). Utilisation expectations drive the choice.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A company wants to finance a 5-year machine with its overdraft because “the rate is the same”. Explain the risks.
2. A borrower with EBITDA $12m proposes a leverage covenant of 3.0× net debt/EBITDA. Net debt is $30m. Its downside budget shows EBITDA of $10m. Is the headroom adequate?
3. Inventory of $5m of steel rebar is offered as collateral. Which risks would you consider, and what advance rate might be reasonable?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. The overdraft is on demand and reviewed annually; the bank can reduce or cancel it, forcing repayment of a long-term asset from short-term funds. It also consumes working-capital headroom and hides the true funding structure; covenants and liquidity may suffer. A term loan or lease matched to the machine’s life is appropriate.
2. Base case leverage 2.5×; downside 3.0× → zero headroom. Adequate headroom would require a covenant around 3.75–4.0× or a plan to reduce net debt.
3. Price volatility, theft/fraud, control of the stock (who holds keys/warehouse receipts), quality and specifications, insurance, storage conditions (rust), legal perfection of the pledge, liquidity on enforcement. Advance rate perhaps 50–60% with collateral management, regular mark-to-market and margin top-up.
:::
