# Payments, Correspondent Banking, SWIFT and Reconciliation

:::obj In this chapter
How money actually moves: domestic payment systems, cross-border payments through correspondent banks, SWIFT messaging and ISO 20022, payment instruments, timing and costs, and bank reconciliation.
:::

## Payment instruments

| Instrument | How it works | Typical use | Key risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | Physical currency | Retail, small payments | Theft, AML restrictions |
| Cheque | Written order to drawer’s bank to pay | Still used in some markets, including post-dated cheques as security in parts of the Middle East | Bounced cheques; legal consequences vary by country |
| Bank transfer (credit transfer) | Payer instructs its bank to credit payee’s account | Supplier payments, payroll, B2B | Wrong beneficiary, fraud (BEC) |
| Direct debit | Payee pulls funds with payer’s mandate | Subscriptions, utilities | Mandate management |
| Card payment | Card networks authorise, clear, settle; merchant pays fees | Retail, e-commerce | Chargebacks, fraud |
| Instant payment | 24/7 real-time transfers | Retail and SME payments | Irrevocability; fraud speed |
| Documentary instruments | Payment linked to documents (L/C, collections) | International trade | Discrepancies |
| Bill of exchange / promissory note | Negotiable written payment promise/order | Trade credit, discounting | Enforceability by jurisdiction |
| Digital wallets / e-money | Stored value with a licensed provider | Consumer and gig payments | Provider risk |

## Domestic payment systems

- **RTGS (real-time gross settlement):** high-value payments settled individually and irrevocably in central-bank money — e.g., Fedwire (US), T2 (euro area, operated by the Eurosystem since 2023), CHAPS (UK), UAEFTS (UAE), SARIE (Saudi Arabia).
- **ACH / batch systems:** lower-value bulk payments (payroll, direct debits) netted and settled in batches.
- **Instant payment systems:** real-time retail payments, e.g., FedNow (US, launched 2023), SEPA Instant (euro area), UPI (India), and regional systems such as the UAE’s Aani and Saudi Arabia’s instant-payment service.
- **Card schemes and processors:** Visa, Mastercard and domestic schemes; acquiring banks and payment service providers.

## Cross-border payments and correspondent banking

A bank cannot hold accounts at every bank in the world. To pay in a foreign currency, it uses **correspondent banks** that hold its **nostro** accounts in that currency.

:::example Path of a USD payment from Beirut to Shenzhen (simplified)
1. An importer instructs its bank in Lebanon to pay USD 120,000 to a supplier at a bank in Shenzhen.
2. The Lebanese bank debits the importer’s account and sends a customer payment message to its USD correspondent in New York (bank A), which holds the Lebanese bank’s nostro account.
3. If the Chinese bank’s USD correspondent is a different US bank (bank B), bank A pays bank B through a US system (e.g., Fedwire or CHIPS), with cover messages identifying the original parties.
4. Bank B credits the Chinese bank’s USD account; the Chinese bank credits the supplier (possibly converting to CNY).
5. Each bank screens the payment for sanctions and AML; any bank may hold or reject it.

Fees can be deducted along the chain unless the charges code is **OUR** (payer pays all charges). Codes: **OUR** (sender pays all), **SHA** (shared: each party pays its own bank), **BEN** (beneficiary pays all, deducted from the amount).
:::

:::warn Why the supplier received less than the invoice amount
Under SHA or BEN, intermediary banks deduct fees, so a supplier invoicing USD 120,000 may receive USD 119,960. Contracts should specify that payments are made in full “free of bank charges outside the payer’s country” or specify OUR — otherwise disputes over small shortfalls delay shipments and document releases.
:::

## SWIFT and ISO 20022

SWIFT is a member-owned cooperative that provides secure financial **messaging** between institutions; it does not hold accounts or move money itself. Each institution has a **BIC** (business identifier code, e.g., 8 or 11 characters). Accounts are identified by account numbers or **IBANs** (international bank account numbers used in Europe, the Middle East and elsewhere).

| Traditional MT message | Use | ISO 20022 (MX) equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| MT103 | Single customer credit transfer | pacs.008 |
| MT202 / MT202 COV | Bank-to-bank transfer / cover payment | pacs.009 (and pacs.009 COV) |
| MT940 / MT950 | Account statements | camt.053 |
| MT900 / MT910 | Debit / credit confirmation | camt.054 |
| MT101 | Request for transfer (corporate to bank) | pain.001 (customer initiation) |
| MT700 | Issue of a documentary credit | Trade messages remain largely MT (category 7) at the time of writing |
| MT760 | Guarantee / standby L/C | MT (revised in 2021 with URDG-aligned fields) |
| MT799 / MT999 | Free-format messages | Free-format equivalents |

SWIFT migrated cross-border payment instructions to **ISO 20022**, with the coexistence period for MT payment messages ending in November 2025; trade-finance messages follow separate timelines. ISO 20022 carries richer, structured data (structured addresses, purpose codes, remittance information), which improves sanctions screening and automated reconciliation. **SWIFT gpi** adds end-to-end tracking through a unique transaction reference (UETR). Verify current SWIFT standards and timelines before implementing.

:::tip Why this matters to a CS-minded finance professional
Structured payment data enables straight-through processing: matching incoming payments to invoices automatically (cash application), screening with fewer false positives, and real-time tracking. Treasury technology projects often consist of mapping ERP payment files to bank formats (pain.001), consuming bank statements (camt.053) through APIs or host-to-host connections, and automating reconciliation.
:::

## Value dates, cut-offs and timing

- **Value date:** the date funds are available and interest starts/stops.
- **Cut-off times:** latest time for same-day processing, varying by currency and bank.
- **Spot FX settlement:** typically T+2 for most currency pairs (T+1 for USD/CAD, among others).
- **Holidays:** a payment needs both currencies’ settlement systems to be open; Friday–Saturday weekends in some Gulf countries historically created mismatches (the UAE moved to a Saturday–Sunday weekend in 2022).

## Bank reconciliation

Bank reconciliation compares the company’s cash ledger with the bank statement and explains differences.

| Typical differences | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Outstanding payments (issued, not yet cleared) | Reconciling item; no entry |
| Deposits in transit | Reconciling item |
| Bank charges and interest not yet recorded | Record in the books |
| Direct debits / incoming transfers not yet recorded | Record in the books |
| Errors (bank or company) | Correct |
| Returned or rejected payments | Reverse and investigate |

:::example Reconciliation (fictional, USD)
| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Balance per bank statement, 31 May | 284,600 |
| + Deposit in transit (cheque received 31 May) | 18,000 |
| − Outstanding payments (two transfers released 31 May, value 1 June) | (42,500) |
| **Adjusted bank balance** | **260,100** |
| Balance per cash ledger, 31 May | 262,450 |
| − Bank charges not recorded | (350) |
| − Customer payment returned (insufficient details) | (2,000) |
| **Adjusted ledger balance** | **260,100** |

Unreconciled differences that persist are red flags for errors or fraud. Daily automated reconciliation is now standard in well-run treasury functions.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A supplier complains it received USD 49,950 instead of USD 50,000. Explain the likely cause and how to prevent it.
2. Map these to ISO 20022: a corporate’s payment file to the bank; the bank’s end-of-day statement; a cross-border customer transfer between banks.
3. Bank statement shows $105,000; ledger shows $112,300; outstanding cheques $9,500; deposit in transit $15,000; bank charges unrecorded $200; interest received unrecorded $1,400. Reconcile.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Intermediary or beneficiary bank charges deducted under SHA/BEN. Use OUR where available, agree in the contract who bears charges, or add an amount to cover expected charges.
2. pain.001 (customer credit transfer initiation); camt.053 (bank-to-customer statement); pacs.008 (FI-to-FI customer credit transfer).
3. Adjusted bank: 105,000 + 15,000 − 9,500 = **110,500**. Adjusted ledger: 112,300 − 200 + 1,400 = 113,500. Difference **3,000** remains unexplained → investigate (unrecorded payment, error, or irregularity).
:::

# Trade, Working-Capital, Project and Structured Finance; Debt and Equity Capital Markets

:::obj In this chapter
Specialised forms of financing: trade finance (overview; Part VII has the detail), supply-chain and working-capital finance, project finance, structured and asset-based finance, and how banks help companies raise debt and equity in capital markets.
:::

## Trade finance — overview

Trade finance solves three problems in trade: **payment risk** (will I get paid / will I get the goods?), **financing** (who funds the time between paying the supplier and receiving cash from the buyer?), and **information/document handling**.

| Instrument | Main purpose | Detail in |
|---|---|---|
| Documentary credit (L/C) | Payment security for the seller; document control for the buyer | {{ch:Payment Methods in International Trade}} |
| Documentary collection | Document control via banks without payment undertaking | {{ch:Payment Methods in International Trade}} |
| Bank guarantee / SBLC | Security for performance or payment | {{ch:Payment Methods in International Trade}} |
| Import loan / trust receipt | Finance the buyer after the bank pays under an L/C | This chapter and Part VII |
| Pre-shipment / packing credit | Finance the exporter to produce goods | This chapter |
| Post-shipment / export bill discounting | Advance to exporter against shipped documents or accepted drafts | This chapter |
| Forfaiting | Without-recourse purchase of medium-term trade receivables (often guaranteed/avalised) | This chapter |
| Receivables purchase / factoring | Sale of receivables | This chapter |

## Supply chain and working-capital finance

| Technique | Who benefits | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Payables finance (reverse factoring) | Large buyer and its suppliers | Buyer approves supplier invoices on a platform; bank pays suppliers early at a discount priced on the buyer’s credit; buyer pays the bank at maturity |
| Receivables finance / invoice discounting | Seller | Bank advances against receivables (recourse or non-recourse) |
| Distributor finance | Manufacturer’s distributors | Bank finances distributors’ purchases, often with manufacturer support |
| Inventory finance | Holder of inventory | Loans against stock, often with collateral manager or warehouse receipts |
| Dynamic discounting | Buyer with surplus cash | Buyer itself pays early for a sliding discount |

:::example Payables finance economics (fictional)
A supplier invoices a large retailer $1,000,000 on 90-day terms. Supplier’s own borrowing cost 11%; retailer is investment-grade, so the bank’s discount rate on its risk is 6.0% p.a.
- Supplier receives early payment on day 10: discount = 1,000,000 × 6.0% × 80/360 = **$13,333** → receives $986,667.
- If the supplier had borrowed at 11% for 80 days instead: cost $24,444. Saving: **$11,111**.
- Retailer keeps 90-day terms (or negotiates 120 days), strengthening its working capital.
Accounting and disclosure: buyers must disclose supplier finance arrangements (IFRS amendments effective 2024; US GAAP disclosures effective 2023); analysts may treat them as debt-like.
:::

## Project finance

Project finance funds a single, ring-fenced project (power plant, toll road, desalination plant, port) through a special purpose vehicle, with debt repaid **only from project cash flows** (non-recourse or limited recourse to sponsors).

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Sponsors | Equity investors who develop the project (typically 20–35% equity) |
| SPV | Project company owning the assets and contracts |
| Offtake agreement | Long-term purchase contract (e.g., power purchase agreement) giving revenue certainty |
| EPC contract | Fixed-price, date-certain engineering, procurement and construction contract, with liquidated damages |
| O&M contract | Operations and maintenance |
| Lenders | Banks, DFIs, ECAs, bond investors |
| Security | All project assets, contracts, accounts, shares of the SPV; step-in rights |
| Key metrics | DSCR (often minimum 1.20–1.40×), loan life coverage ratio, debt-to-equity |
| Accounts | Waterfall of cash: operating costs → senior debt service → reserve accounts (DSRA) → distributions |

:::example DSCR sculpting
A solar project expects CFADS (cash flow available for debt service) of $12m per year for 18 years. Lenders require minimum DSCR 1.30× → maximum annual debt service = 12 ÷ 1.30 = **$9.23m**. At 7% over 18 years, the annuity factor is 10.0591 → maximum debt ≈ **$92.9m**. If the project costs $125m, equity must fund at least $32m (26%).
:::

## Structured and asset-based finance

- **Securitisation:** pooling receivables (mortgages, auto loans, trade receivables) into an SPV that issues securities in tranches; investors are paid from the pool’s cash flows.
- **Asset-based lending (ABL):** borrowing bases over receivables, inventory and equipment.
- **Pre-export finance (PXF):** loans to commodity producers repaid from assigned export contracts.
- **Prepayment finance:** a trader or bank prepays a producer for future deliveries.
- **Structured commodity finance:** financing secured on commodities through storage, shipment and sale, with collateral managers and assignment of proceeds.
- **Acquisition and leveraged finance:** senior, second-lien and mezzanine debt for buyouts.

## Debt capital markets (DCM)

Companies and governments raise debt by issuing **bonds** (or sukuk) to investors rather than borrowing from banks.

| Step | Activity |
|---|---|
| Preparation | Choose arrangers/bookrunners; credit rating(s); legal documentation (prospectus/offering circular, trust deed or fiscal agency agreement); listing |
| Marketing | Investor roadshow or calls; initial price thoughts |
| Bookbuilding | Investors submit orders; pricing tightens if demand is strong |
| Pricing and allocation | Coupon set as benchmark yield + credit spread; allocation to investors |
| Settlement | Funds paid against securities, typically through Euroclear/Clearstream for Eurobonds |
| After issue | Coupon payments, covenant compliance, secondary trading |

Bonds vs loans: bonds offer longer tenors, fixed rates, a diversified investor base and typically fewer maintenance covenants; loans offer flexibility, confidentiality, prepayment and relationship support.

## Equity capital markets (ECM)

Investment banks help companies issue shares: **IPOs** (initial public offerings), **follow-on offerings**, **rights issues**, **private placements**, and **block trades** for existing shareholders. Steps for an IPO: readiness (governance, audited accounts, reporting systems), appointment of advisers, due diligence, prospectus, regulator approval, valuation and price range, bookbuilding, pricing, allocation, listing, stabilisation. Part X covers IPOs from the investor side.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A supplier is offered payables finance at 5.5% p.a. on a $400,000 invoice paid 75 days early (act/360). What does it receive?
2. A project has CFADS of $20m per year for 15 years. Lenders require DSCR 1.35× and the interest rate is 8%. Estimate maximum debt (annuity factor 15 years at 8% = 8.5595).
3. Why might a company choose a bond rather than a bank loan for a 10-year financing?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Discount = 400,000 × 5.5% × 75/360 = **$4,583.33** → receives **$395,416.67**.
2. Max debt service = 20 ÷ 1.35 = 14.815 → max debt ≈ 14.815 × 8.5595 = **$126.8m**.
3. Longer tenor available, fixed rate locking in cost, broader investor base reducing dependence on banks, fewer maintenance covenants; downsides include public disclosure, rating requirements, issuance costs, and less flexibility to renegotiate.
:::

# KYC, AML, Sanctions Screening, Bank Risk and Regulation

:::obj In this chapter
Why banks ask so many questions; the KYC and AML process; sanctions screening; the main risks banks manage; and the Basel framework for capital and liquidity — with what all this means for corporate clients.
:::

## KYC and customer due diligence

Banks must identify and verify their customers and understand their business, under national AML laws based on the **FATF Recommendations**.

| Element | What the bank collects |
|---|---|
| Legal entity | Commercial registration, constitutional documents, licences, registered address |
| Ownership and control | Shareholder register, group structure chart, **ultimate beneficial owners** (commonly natural persons holding 25% or more, or exercising control; thresholds vary by jurisdiction) |
| Management and signatories | IDs, board resolutions, powers of attorney, signature specimens |
| Nature of business | Products, countries, customers, suppliers, expected transaction volumes and currencies |
| Source of funds / wealth | Especially for high-risk clients |
| Screening | Sanctions lists, PEP lists, adverse media |
| Risk rating | Low, medium, high → determines frequency of review and enhanced due diligence (EDD) |

**Periodic reviews** (e.g., every 1–3 years depending on risk) and **trigger events** (change of ownership, unusual transactions) require updates. Failure to update KYC can lead to frozen accounts.

## AML and transaction monitoring

Banks monitor transactions for patterns inconsistent with the customer’s profile and report suspicions to the national **financial intelligence unit** (suspicious transaction/activity reports). Tipping off the customer is prohibited.

:::warn Trade-based money laundering (TBML) red flags
- Over- or under-invoicing relative to market prices.
- Goods descriptions that are vague or inconsistent with the client’s business.
- Multiple invoicing of the same shipment; phantom shipments.
- Payments from or to unrelated third parties or high-risk jurisdictions.
- Unusual shipping routes, transhipment through free zones without commercial reason.
- Frequent amendments to L/Cs changing beneficiaries or ports.
(See guidance from FATF and the Wolfsberg Group on trade finance principles.)
:::

## Sanctions screening

Banks screen names (customers, counterparties, banks, vessels, ports, goods) against lists from the UN, OFAC (US), EU, UK (OFSI) and local authorities, plus ownership rules. **Payments are stopped** while alerts are investigated; true matches are blocked or rejected. For trade, banks also screen **vessels** (IMO numbers, flag, ownership, voyage history) and **dual-use goods**.

For corporates: provide complete, accurate payment details (full names and addresses, purpose), avoid vague descriptions, and expect delays for high-risk countries or goods.

## The main risks a bank manages

| Risk | Description | How managed |
|---|---|---|
| Credit risk | Borrowers or counterparties default | Credit analysis, limits, collateral, diversification, provisions, capital |
| Liquidity risk | Unable to meet outflows (deposit withdrawals, maturing funding) | Liquid asset buffers (LCR), stable funding (NSFR), contingency funding plans |
| Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB) | Mismatch between repricing of assets and liabilities | Gap analysis, duration matching, swaps, limits on NII and economic value sensitivity |
| Market risk | Losses on trading positions (FX, rates, equities, commodities) | VaR and stress limits, hedging |
| Operational risk | Process, people, system failures, fraud, cyber | Controls, three lines model, insurance, resilience |
| Compliance and conduct risk | Breaches of laws and regulations, mis-selling | Compliance function, training, monitoring |
| Concentration risk | Large exposures to single names, sectors, countries | Large exposure limits |
| Strategic and reputational risk | Business model failure, loss of trust | Governance, strategy |

## Basel framework essentials

The **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS)** sets global standards that national regulators implement (with local variations).

| Pillar / standard | Content |
|---|---|
| Pillar 1 — minimum capital | Capital ≥ requirements for credit, market and operational risk, measured as % of risk-weighted assets (RWA) |
| Pillar 2 — supervisory review | Bank’s own capital assessment (ICAAP); supervisor may require more |
| Pillar 3 — market discipline | Public disclosure |
| Basel III capital (post-2008 reforms) | Minimum CET1 4.5%, Tier 1 6%, total capital 8% of RWA, plus capital conservation buffer 2.5%, countercyclical buffer (0–2.5%), systemic buffers for systemically important banks |
| Leverage ratio | Tier 1 ÷ total exposure ≥ 3% (non-risk-based backstop) |
| Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) | High-quality liquid assets ≥ net cash outflows over a 30-day stress (≥ 100%) |
| Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) | Available stable funding ≥ required stable funding (≥ 100%) |
| Large exposures | Limits on exposure to a single counterparty group (generally 25% of Tier 1) |
| Basel III finalisation (sometimes called “Basel IV”) | Revised standardised approaches, limits on internal models, output floor phasing to 72.5% — implementation timelines differ by jurisdiction |

:::example Why risk weights affect what banks offer you
Under the standardised approach, an unrated corporate exposure often carries a 100% risk weight; certain short-term, self-liquidating trade transactions receive favourable credit conversion factors (e.g., 20% for short-term documentary credits in the standardised approach). A $10m unfunded L/C line may therefore consume much less capital than a $10m loan. That partly explains why banks are often more willing to provide trade-finance lines than unsecured working-capital loans.
:::

:::ar لماذا تطلب البنوك كل هذه المستندات؟
تلتزم البنوك بقوانين مكافحة غسل الأموال وتمويل الإرهاب المستندة إلى توصيات مجموعة العمل المالي (FATF)، وبقوانين العقوبات الدولية والمحلية. لذلك تطلب معرفة هوية العميل والمستفيد الحقيقي وطبيعة النشاط ومصدر الأموال، وتفحص كل عملية مقابل قوائم العقوبات. والتقصير في ذلك قد يعرّض البنك لغرامات ضخمة ولفقدان علاقاته مع البنوك المراسلة. كما تخضع البنوك لمعايير بازل لرأس المال والسيولة، مما يؤثر في نوع التمويل الذي تقدمه وتسعيره.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A new client refuses to disclose the owners of a holding company in a secrecy jurisdiction. What will the bank do?
2. A bank has CET1 capital of $1.2bn and RWA of $11bn. Is it above the minimum CET1 plus conservation buffer? By how much?
3. A trading client’s invoices for “electronic equipment” are 40% above market prices and paid by a third-party company in another country. What should the bank consider?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Without identifying UBOs the bank cannot complete KYC; it will decline onboarding (or exit the relationship) and may consider whether a suspicious activity report is warranted.
2. CET1 ratio = 1.2 ÷ 11 = **10.9%**; minimum + conservation buffer = 7.0% → surplus ≈ **3.9 pp** (~$429m), before any countercyclical, systemic or Pillar 2 add-ons.
3. TBML indicators (over-invoicing, vague goods, third-party payments): enhanced due diligence on the transactions and parties, request contracts and shipping documents, possibly decline the transactions, and consider filing a suspicious transaction report — without tipping off the client.
:::

# Classifying Bank Products: Payment, Financing, Guarantee and Risk-Mitigation Instruments

:::obj In this chapter
A single map of bank products by commercial purpose, so you can tell immediately what a product does — and does not — do.
:::

## Four functions

1. **Payment instruments** move money or settle obligations.
2. **Financing instruments** provide funds (credit) for a period.
3. **Guarantee instruments** provide an undertaking to pay if something happens (or fails to happen).
4. **Risk-mitigation instruments** transfer or reduce a risk (credit, FX, interest rate, commodity, political).

Many products combine functions — the key professional skill is to identify which function each party is buying.

## Master classification table

| Product | Payment | Financing | Guarantee / undertaking | Risk mitigation | Who is protected / funded |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bank transfer (SWIFT, RTGS) | ✔ | | | | Payer settles |
| Cheque / promissory note | ✔ (instrument) | Possibly (post-dated) | | | Payee holds a claim |
| Overdraft / RCF | | ✔ | | | Borrower funded |
| Term loan | | ✔ | | | Borrower funded |
| Import loan / trust receipt | | ✔ | | | Importer funded |
| Sight L/C | ✔ (mechanism) | | ✔ (issuing bank undertakes) | ✔ (seller’s credit risk; buyer’s document control) | Seller protected; buyer controls documents |
| Usance L/C | ✔ | ✔ (deferred payment) | ✔ | ✔ | Buyer gets credit; seller protected |
| L/C confirmation | | | ✔ (second bank) | ✔ (issuing bank and country risk) | Seller |
| Documentary collection | ✔ (mechanism) | | | Partial (document control only) | Seller (weakly) |
| Bank guarantee (performance, advance payment, bid) | | | ✔ | ✔ | Beneficiary (buyer/employer) |
| Payment guarantee / SBLC | | | ✔ | ✔ | Seller / lender |
| Factoring (non-recourse) | | ✔ | | ✔ (buyer credit risk) | Seller funded and protected |
| Invoice discounting (recourse) | | ✔ | | | Seller funded |
| Forfaiting | | ✔ | | ✔ (without recourse) | Exporter |
| Payables finance | ✔ (early payment) | ✔ | | | Supplier funded |
| FX forward / swap | ✔ (settlement) | | | ✔ (FX risk) | Hedger |
| Interest rate swap / cap | | | | ✔ (rate risk) | Borrower |
| Commodity swap / futures (via bank/broker) | | | | ✔ (price risk) | Producer/consumer |
| Credit insurance (via insurer, often arranged with bank) | | | | ✔ (buyer default) | Seller / financing bank |
| Cash pooling | ✔ (internal movement) | ✔ (internal funding) | | ✔ (liquidity) | Group treasury |
| Escrow account | ✔ (conditional release) | | Partial | ✔ (performance) | Both parties |

:::warn Distinctions beginners miss
- **An L/C is not a loan.** A sight L/C is a payment undertaking; the bank lends only if it pays and the applicant has not yet reimbursed (e.g., through an import loan).
- **A guarantee is not insurance.** A demand guarantee is paid against a compliant demand; the bank then claims reimbursement from its client (the applicant), who bears the loss. Insurance transfers the loss to the insurer.
- **Factoring with recourse does not transfer credit risk.** If the buyer does not pay, the seller must repay the factor.
- **Confirmation is not advising.** An advising bank authenticates and passes on the credit; a confirming bank adds its own payment undertaking.
- **A collection gives the seller no bank undertaking.** If the buyer refuses documents, the seller’s goods are at a foreign port with no buyer.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Classify each by primary function: (a) an advance-payment guarantee; (b) a 180-day usance L/C; (c) a cross-currency swap; (d) a trust receipt loan; (e) non-recourse receivables purchase.
2. An exporter says, “I have an L/C from my buyer, so I’m financed.” Is that correct?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (a) Guarantee/risk mitigation (protects the buyer’s advance); (b) payment + guarantee + financing (buyer gets 180 days); (c) risk mitigation (FX and interest) and possibly financing restructuring; (d) financing; (e) financing + risk mitigation (credit risk transfer).
2. Not necessarily. The L/C secures payment if compliant documents are presented; it does not fund production. The exporter may obtain financing *based on* the L/C (pre-shipment finance, discounting of a usance L/C after acceptance), but that is a separate facility.
:::

:::summary Part VI summary
- Banks are leveraged intermediaries that earn spreads and fees; capital, liquidity and credit losses drive their behaviour.
- Credit decisions rest on repayment from cash flow; collateral and guarantees are secondary protection.
- Facilities should match the purpose and tenor of the need; covenants are early-warning tools to be negotiated with headroom.
- Payments run through domestic systems and correspondent chains, with SWIFT messages increasingly in ISO 20022 format.
- KYC, AML and sanctions controls are legal obligations that shape what banks can do.
- Always identify whether a product pays, finances, guarantees or mitigates risk — and for whom.
:::
