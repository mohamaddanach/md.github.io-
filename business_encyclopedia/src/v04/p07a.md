International trade is where commercial, logistical, legal and financial knowledge meet. One shipment involves a sales contract, an Incoterms rule, a carrier, an insurer, two customs authorities, at least two banks, a set of documents that must agree with each other, a payment method that allocates risk, and often financing and FX hedging. A mistake in any element can cost more than the margin on the deal.

This part follows a transaction from first enquiry to final payment, explains every document and payment method, the ICC rules that govern them (Incoterms® 2020, UCP 600, URC 522, URDG 758), customs procedures, disputes and claims, and ends with three complete simulated transactions — from a $5,000 air shipment to a $25 million export contract.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part VII
- Describe every participant in an international transaction and what each needs.
- Choose and apply the correct Incoterms® 2020 rule and understand its cost, risk and document consequences.
- Read and prepare the main trade documents and check them for consistency.
- Explain customs classification, valuation, origin, duties and taxes.
- Compare payment methods by risk and cost, and apply UCP 600, URC 522 and URDG 758.
- Handle discrepancies, payment disputes, cargo claims, demurrage and detention.
- Use credit insurance and export credit agencies, and recognise trade-based financial crime.
- Build complete transaction cost and profitability calculations.
:::

:::framework The ICC rules referred to in this part
The International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) publishes voluntary rules that apply when parties incorporate them into their contracts or instruments: **Incoterms® 2020** (delivery terms, effective 1 January 2020), **UCP 600** (Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, 2007 revision), **ISBP 821** (International Standard Banking Practice for the examination of documents under UCP 600, 2023), **eUCP 2.1** (electronic presentations), **URC 522** (Uniform Rules for Collections, 1995 revision), **URDG 758** (Uniform Rules for Demand Guarantees, 2010 revision), and **ISP98** (International Standby Practices, published by the Institute of International Banking Law & Practice and endorsed by ICC). They are not laws: they bind because the contract or instrument says they apply, and mandatory local law prevails over them. Obtain the official texts from the ICC for actual transactions; this book summarises and paraphrases.
:::

# Anatomy of an International Transaction and Its Participants

:::obj In this chapter
The parties, the sequence of steps, and the flows of goods, documents, money and information in an international sale.
:::

## The participants

| Participant | Role | What they want | Key documents they issue or need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seller / exporter | Supplies goods | Payment in full, on time; minimal risk after delivery | Pro forma, commercial invoice, packing list, certificates |
| Buyer / importer | Purchases goods | Correct goods, on time, at agreed cost; pay only for what is shipped | Purchase order, L/C application, import declaration |
| Agent | Finds buyers/sellers for a principal; commission | Commission; protection against being bypassed | Agency agreement |
| Broker | Matches parties for a fee; not a party to the sale | Fee; confidentiality | Brokerage agreement, NCNDA (non-circumvention) |
| Distributor | Buys and resells in a territory | Margin, exclusivity, stable supply | Distribution agreement |
| Trading company | Buys and resells as principal across borders | Margin; financing; controlled risk | Back-to-back contracts |
| Freight forwarder | Organises transport and documents | Margin on freight; repeat business | House B/L or FCR, shipping instructions |
| Carrier (shipping line, airline, trucker) | Transports goods | Freight paid; equipment returned | B/L, AWB, CMR note |
| Customs broker / clearing agent | Files customs declarations | Fees | Declarations, duty payments |
| Inspection company | Verifies quantity/quality | Fees; independence | Inspection certificates |
| Insurer | Covers cargo risks | Premium; good risk | Policy / insurance certificate |
| Chamber of commerce / issuing authority | Certifies origin | Fees | Certificate of origin |
| Banks (issuing, advising, confirming, nominated, collecting, remitting) | Payment, guarantees, finance | Fees; documentary compliance; no fraud or sanctions breach | L/C, collection instruction, guarantees |
| Customs authorities (both countries) | Control, collect duties, enforce restrictions | Correct declarations; duties paid | Release orders |
| Ports and terminals | Handle cargo | Charges paid | Terminal receipts, delivery orders |
| Credit insurer / ECA | Covers non-payment | Premium; underwriting information | Policies, guarantees |

## The sequence of a transaction

| Phase | Steps | Main risks |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Pre-contract | Enquiry, RFQ, quotation, samples, negotiation, KYC on counterparty, sanctions and export-control screening | Fraudulent counterparties; unrealistic prices |
| 2. Contract | PO and acceptance or signed sales contract: goods, specs, quantity and tolerance, price and currency, Incoterms rule, delivery date, payment terms, documents, inspection, warranties, claims, force majeure, law and disputes | Ambiguity; terms not aligned with L/C |
| 3. Payment security | Advance payment, L/C issuance and advising, collection instructions, guarantees, credit insurance | L/C terms impossible to comply with |
| 4. Production and pre-shipment | Manufacturing, quality control, pre-shipment inspection, export licences, packing and marking | Delays, quality failure |
| 5. Transport booking | Forwarder, booking, shipping instructions, container release, VGM (verified gross mass) | Missed vessel, rate changes |
| 6. Export clearance and loading | Export declaration, customs release, loading, B/L issued | Documentary errors |
| 7. Documents and payment | Seller assembles documents, presents to bank or sends to buyer; examination; payment or acceptance | Discrepancies; late presentation |
| 8. Transit | Tracking; insurance in place | Loss, damage, delay, re-routing |
| 9. Arrival and import | Arrival notice, delivery order (against original B/L or release), import declaration, duties, inspection, release | Demurrage, customs disputes |
| 10. Delivery and after-sale | Inland transport, receipt, inspection, claims, final payment (open account), reconciliation | Quality claims, payment disputes |

## The four flows

1. **Goods:** seller → carrier → ports → buyer.
2. **Documents:** seller → (banks) → buyer → carrier/customs.
3. **Money:** buyer → (banks) → seller; plus payments to carriers, insurers, customs, brokers.
4. **Information:** quotations, bookings, tracking, notices, customs data — increasingly digital.

A transaction is safe when the flows are synchronised: the seller does not lose control of the goods (or the title documents) before being paid or secured, and the buyer does not pay before receiving goods or documents that control them.

# Incoterms® 2020 in Depth

:::obj In this chapter
The eleven Incoterms® 2020 rules: obligations, cost split, risk transfer, export and import clearance, insurance and documents, with guidance on choosing the right rule and common errors.
:::

## What Incoterms do and do not cover

**They cover:** delivery point, transfer of risk of loss or damage, allocation of costs (transport, loading/unloading, export/import clearance, security), obligations to obtain documents and provide information, insurance obligations (CIF and CIP only).

**They do not cover:** price and payment, transfer of ownership/title, breach of contract remedies, product quality, force majeure, dispute resolution, sanctions or export-control compliance. These must be in the sales contract.

:::def How to quote an Incoterms rule correctly
Rule + named place (as precise as possible) + version: “FCA Seller’s warehouse, 12 Industrial Road, Izmir, Incoterms® 2020” or “CIF Jebel Ali Port, Incoterms® 2020”. A vague place (e.g., “FOB China”) creates disputes about costs at the port.
:::

## The eleven rules

| Rule | Full name | Mode | Seller delivers / risk passes | Seller pays main carriage | Insurance | Export clearance | Import clearance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| EXW | Ex Works | Any | Goods at buyer’s disposal at seller’s premises, not loaded | No | None | Buyer | Buyer |
| FCA | Free Carrier | Any | Handed to buyer’s carrier at named place (loaded if at seller’s premises) | No | None | Seller | Buyer |
| CPT | Carriage Paid To | Any | Handed to first carrier | Yes, to named destination | None | Seller | Buyer |
| CIP | Carriage and Insurance Paid To | Any | Handed to first carrier | Yes | Seller; minimum ICC (A) / all risks, 110% | Seller | Buyer |
| DAP | Delivered at Place | Any | At named destination, on arriving transport, ready for unloading | Yes | None required (seller bears risk to destination) | Seller | Buyer |
| DPU | Delivered at Place Unloaded | Any | Unloaded at named destination | Yes | None required | Seller | Buyer |
| DDP | Delivered Duty Paid | Any | At destination ready for unloading, import-cleared, duties paid | Yes | None required | Seller | Seller |
| FAS | Free Alongside Ship | Sea/inland waterway | Alongside the vessel at port of shipment | No | None | Seller | Buyer |
| FOB | Free on Board | Sea/inland waterway | On board the vessel at port of shipment | No | None | Seller | Buyer |
| CFR | Cost and Freight | Sea/inland waterway | On board at port of shipment | Yes, to destination port | None | Seller | Buyer |
| CIF | Cost, Insurance and Freight | Sea/inland waterway | On board at port of shipment | Yes | Seller; minimum ICC (C), 110% | Seller | Buyer |

## Two families and two critical points

- **E, F rules (EXW, FCA, FAS, FOB):** the buyer contracts the main carriage. Delivery and risk transfer at origin.
- **C rules (CPT, CIP, CFR, CIF):** the seller contracts and pays the main carriage, but **risk passes at origin** (on handing to the carrier / on board). These are **shipment contracts** with two critical points: risk point (origin) and cost point (destination).
- **D rules (DAP, DPU, DDP):** **arrival contracts**; the seller bears risk until the goods reach the destination.

:::warn The most common Incoterms errors
1. **Using FOB/CFR/CIF for containers.** Containerised goods are handed to the carrier at an inland terminal or container yard days before loading on board. Under FOB the seller keeps risk until the goods are on board, though it has lost control. FCA/CPT/CIP fit container traffic better.
2. **EXW for exports.** The buyer is responsible for export clearance, but often cannot act as exporter in the seller’s country; VAT and export evidence problems follow. FCA at seller’s premises is usually better.
3. **DDP without understanding import taxes.** The seller becomes liable for import duties and VAT, needs local registration or an importer of record, and bears customs risk.
4. **Believing CIF means the seller bears risk during the voyage.** It does not: risk passes on loading; the insurance protects the buyer.
5. **Insufficient insurance under CIF.** ICC (C) is minimal (major casualties only); buyers often need ICC (A) and should specify it.
6. **Named place too vague,** leading to disputes about terminal handling and port charges.
7. **Mismatch between the sales contract, the L/C and the documents** (e.g., L/C asks for an on-board B/L under FCA without using the FCA on-board B/L option).
:::

## Costs: who pays what (typical container shipment)

| Cost element | EXW | FCA (seller’s premises) | FOB | CFR/CPT | CIF/CIP | DAP | DPU | DDP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Packing and marking | S | S | S | S | S | S | S | S |
| Loading at seller’s premises | B | S | S | S | S | S | S | S |
| Inland transport to port/terminal | B | B | S | S | S | S | S | S |
| Export clearance and duties | B | S | S | S | S | S | S | S |
| Origin terminal handling | B | B | S* | S | S | S | S | S |
| Main carriage (freight) | B | B | B | S | S | S | S | S |
| Cargo insurance | (B) | (B) | (B) | (B) | S | (S) | (S) | (S) |
| Destination terminal handling | B | B | B | B** | B** | S** | S | S |
| Unloading at destination | B | B | B | B | B | B | S | B |
| Import clearance, duties, taxes | B | B | B | B | B | B | B | S |
| Delivery to buyer’s premises | B | B | B | B | B | S (if named place) | S | S |

S = seller, B = buyer, (x) = not obliged but bears the risk, so normally insures. *Under FOB, costs until goods are on board are the seller’s; terminal handling practices vary by port. **Destination charges under C and D rules depend on what the carriage contract includes — specify them to avoid double charging.

## Choosing a rule

| Situation | Recommended rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Containerised export, buyer has a strong forwarder | FCA (seller’s premises or terminal) | Risk passes when goods handed over; seller handles export clearance |
| Containerised export, seller controls freight | CPT or CIP | Seller can earn on freight; risk passes early |
| Bulk commodity by sea | FOB, CFR, CIF | Goods loaded directly over the ship’s rail; matches charter-party practice |
| Buyer wants door delivery but will import-clear | DAP | Seller delivers, buyer handles customs |
| Seller has local import capability in buyer’s country | DDP | Full service; seller controls customs |
| L/C transaction requiring on-board B/L with FCA | FCA with the 2020 on-board B/L option (A6/B6) | Allows the buyer to instruct its carrier to issue an on-board B/L to the seller |
| Buyer collects from factory, domestic trade | EXW (domestic) | Simple |

:::example How the rule changes the seller’s price (fictional container shipment)
Ex-works price of goods: $40,000. Costs: loading $150; inland haulage to port $600; export clearance $200; origin terminal $250; sea freight $2,400; insurance ICC (A) 0.15% of 110% CIF ≈ $72; destination terminal $300; import duty 5% of CIF; import clearance $250; delivery to buyer $400.

| Rule | Seller’s quoted price (approx.) |
|---|---|
| EXW | 40,000 |
| FCA seller’s premises | 40,000 + 150 + 200 = **40,350** |
| FOB port of shipment | 40,350 + 600 + 250 = **41,200** |
| CFR destination port | 41,200 + 2,400 = **43,600** |
| CIF destination port | 43,600 + 72 = **43,672** |
| DAP buyer’s warehouse (incl. destination terminal and delivery) | 43,672 − 72 + 300 + 400 = **44,300** (seller would also insure at its own choice) |
| DDP buyer’s warehouse | 44,300 + duty 5% × 43,672 ≈ 2,184 + clearance 250 = **46,734** (plus import VAT if not recoverable by seller) |

The buyer’s total landed cost is roughly the same under any rule if costs are passed through at cost; what changes is **who controls** each service, **who bears the risk**, and who earns or loses on cost changes.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A Chinese supplier quotes “FOB Shanghai” for goods delivered in containers to a terminal 5 days before vessel loading. A fire at the terminal destroys the container on day 3. Who bears the loss under FOB? Under FCA terminal?
2. An Egyptian importer buys CIF Alexandria and the insurer offers only ICC (C). What risks are not covered, and what should the importer do?
3. A UAE seller sells DDP Riyadh. Which obligations does the seller take on that it would not have under DAP?
4. Recommend an Incoterms rule for: (a) 30,000 tonnes of wheat from Ukraine to Egypt, buyer charters the vessel; (b) machine parts by air from Germany to Jordan, seller has a good forwarder; (c) furniture in containers from Malaysia, buyer has a nominated forwarder.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Under FOB the seller bears risk until goods are on board — the **seller** bears the loss (even though the terminal had custody). Under FCA (delivery at the terminal), risk passed to the **buyer** when the goods were handed to the carrier — buyer bears the loss (and claims on its insurance).
2. ICC (C) covers major casualties (fire, explosion, stranding, sinking, collision, jettison, general average) but not, for example, theft, rough handling damage, water damage outside named perils. The importer should require ICC (A) in the contract or buy additional cover itself.
3. Import clearance in Saudi Arabia, payment of customs duties and import VAT (15%, verify current rate), compliance with Saudi product certification and labelling rules, acting as or appointing an importer of record — and bearing the risk of customs delays and reassessments.
4. (a) **FOB** (Black Sea port) — the buyer charters the vessel; (b) **CPT or CIP Amman** (air, seller controls transport); (c) **FCA** (Port Klang terminal or seller’s premises), buyer’s forwarder.
:::

## The packing list

States for each package: marks and numbers, contents, quantity, net and gross weight, dimensions/volume; totals must match the B/L and invoice. Customs use it to select packages for examination; the buyer uses it to receive goods; the forwarder to plan loading. Errors cause customs delays and claims disputes.

## The bill of lading

| Field | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Shipper | Party shipping the goods (usually seller) | Must match L/C requirements |
| Consignee | “To order”, “to order of [bank]” or named party | Determines negotiability and who can claim goods |
| Notify party | Party informed on arrival | Usually the buyer or its agent |
| Vessel and voyage, port of loading, port of discharge | Transport details | Must match contract/L/C |
| Place of receipt / delivery | For combined transport | Determines carrier responsibility |
| Description of goods, marks, packages, weights | As declared by shipper (“said to contain”) | Must not conflict with invoice; carrier usually does not verify container contents |
| Freight | “Freight prepaid” or “freight collect” | Must match Incoterms (prepaid under C/D rules) |
| On-board notation and date | Goods loaded on named vessel on date | Shipment date for L/C; risk transfer under FOB/CFR/CIF |
| Clean | No clause declaring defective condition of goods or packaging | Banks reject “unclean” B/Ls under L/Cs |
| Number of originals | E.g., “3/3” | Full set needed to control goods |
| Signature | Carrier, master, or named agent for them | UCP 600 art. 20 requirements |

:::warn Bill of lading risks
- **Release without original B/L:** carriers sometimes release cargo against a letter of indemnity; if the buyer has not paid, the seller (or its bank) may lose control of the goods — and the carrier may be liable to the holder of the original B/L.
- **Telex release / express release:** the seller surrenders originals at origin; goods are released to the consignee without originals — only safe once payment is secured.
- **Switch B/Ls:** issuing a second set (e.g., changing shipper or port details) is legitimate in some trading chains but also a common tool of fraud and sanctions evasion.
- **Ante-dated or post-dated B/Ls** to meet L/C deadlines are fraudulent.
- **House B/L vs master B/L:** a buyer holding a forwarder’s house B/L must also ensure the forwarder controls the master B/L.
:::

## Air waybill, road and rail documents

The **air waybill (AWB)** is not a document of title; goods are delivered to the named consignee. To keep control in air shipments under L/Cs or collections, the consignee is often the buyer’s bank. Road (CMR convention) and rail (CIM) consignment notes are likewise not documents of title.

## Certificate of origin

Two main types: **non-preferential** (general proof of origin, e.g., for trade statistics, anti-dumping, sanctions, labelling) and **preferential** (enabling reduced duties under a trade agreement, issued in specific forms or by approved exporter declarations, e.g., EUR.1 for some EU agreements, GAFTA/Arab certificate). Origin is determined by **rules of origin** (wholly obtained, change in tariff classification, value-added thresholds, specific processes), not simply by the country of shipment.

## Insurance document

Under CIF/CIP and many L/Cs: an insurance policy or certificate (UCP 600 art. 28) for at least 110% of the CIF/CIP value (unless otherwise required), in the credit’s currency, covering from the place of shipment, dated no later than the shipment date (or showing cover effective from that date), covering the risks required (e.g., Institute Cargo Clauses (A), war and strikes clauses), and endorsed or made out so the holder can claim.

## Inspection certificates

Issued by independent inspection companies for quantity (weight, count), quality (specifications, laboratory analysis), condition, and loading supervision. Contracts must state **who appoints** the inspector, **where** inspection occurs (loading or discharge), and whether the result is **final and binding** — commodity contracts often specify “quality and weight final at loading as per independent inspector’s certificate”.

## Customs and regulatory documents

Export and import declarations (electronic, via single-window systems in many countries), licences for controlled goods, conformity certificates (e.g., national product-safety schemes), health and phytosanitary certificates, halal certificates, dangerous-goods declarations (IMDG for sea, IATA DGR for air), and verified gross mass (VGM) under SOLAS for containers.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Check the consistency of this set: invoice says “CIF Aqaba, 500 cartons, gross 10,500 kg”; packing list totals 500 cartons, gross 10,050 kg; B/L shows “freight collect”, 500 cartons, 10,050 kg; insurance certificate value $55,000 against invoice $52,000. Identify problems.
2. Why do banks want B/Ls “to order of the issuing bank” under L/Cs?
3. Your buyer asks you to telex-release the cargo because originals will arrive late. Payment is “against documents through the bank (D/P)”. What do you do?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Gross weight mismatch (10,500 vs 10,050) between invoice and other documents; “freight collect” is inconsistent with CIF (seller must prepay freight; B/L should say “freight prepaid”); insurance $55,000 is below 110% of $52,000 = $57,200 (under UCP 600 default rule, if the L/C requires CIF insurance). All are potential discrepancies.
2. The bank controls the goods until the applicant reimburses it: the B/L must be endorsed by the bank for the buyer to obtain the goods, giving the bank security.
3. Refuse unless payment is first received or secured (e.g., a bank guarantee); a telex release would let the buyer take the goods without paying, defeating the D/P collection.
:::
