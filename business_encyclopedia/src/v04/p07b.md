# Customs Clearance, Valuation, Duties and Taxes

:::obj In this chapter
How goods cross borders legally: classification, valuation, origin, duties and import taxes, procedures and special regimes, and the role of customs brokers — with calculations.
:::

## The three questions customs asks

1. **What is it?** — tariff classification (HS code), which determines the duty rate and any restrictions.
2. **What is it worth?** — customs value, to which ad valorem duties apply.
3. **Where is it from?** — origin, which determines preferential rates, anti-dumping duties, quotas and sanctions.

Plus: Is it allowed (licences, prohibitions, standards)? Who is responsible (importer of record)?

## Classification: the Harmonized System

The **HS**, maintained by the World Customs Organization (WCO), classifies goods with a 6-digit code used by over 200 economies; countries extend it to 8–12 digits. The nomenclature is revised roughly every five years (HS 2022 is current at the time of writing; HS 2027 has been adopted for future implementation). Classification follows the **General Interpretive Rules (GIRs)**; disputes are common for multifunction products, kits and parts.

:::tip Practical classification steps
1. Get a precise technical description (material, function, use, composition).
2. Identify the section and chapter; read section and chapter notes.
3. Apply the GIRs; check national subheadings and explanatory notes.
4. For significant volumes, request an **advance (binding) tariff ruling** from customs.
5. Keep classification decisions documented and consistent across shipments.
:::

## Customs valuation

Most countries follow the **WTO Customs Valuation Agreement** (Agreement on Implementation of Article VII of GATT 1994), with six methods applied in order:

| Order | Method | When used |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Transaction value — price actually paid or payable, with adjustments | Default; applies in the large majority of imports |
| 2 | Transaction value of identical goods | When method 1 cannot apply (e.g., related-party price influenced) |
| 3 | Transaction value of similar goods | |
| 4 | Deductive value (resale price in importing country minus profit, costs) | |
| 5 | Computed value (cost of production + profit + overheads) | |
| 6 | Fall-back (reasonable means consistent with the agreement) | |

**Adjustments to transaction value** typically add: commissions (other than buying commissions), packing costs, assists (tools, moulds supplied free by the buyer), royalties and licence fees related to the goods and payable as a condition of sale, proceeds of resale accruing to the seller; and — in countries using a CIF basis — freight and insurance to the place of importation. Many countries (including the GCC) use **CIF value**; the US uses an FOB-type basis.

## Duties and import taxes

| Charge | Basis | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Customs duty (ad valorem) | % of customs value | The GCC common external tariff applies 5% to most goods, with exemptions and higher rates for some products (e.g., tobacco); verify current schedules |
| Specific duty | Amount per unit/weight | Common on alcohol, tobacco, some agricultural goods |
| Anti-dumping / countervailing / safeguard duties | Additional duties on specific products/origins | Must be checked by HS code and origin |
| Import VAT / GST | % of (customs value + duty + other charges) | Recoverable for registered businesses in most VAT systems; rates differ by country (e.g., UAE 5%, Saudi Arabia 15%, Bahrain 10% as of the time of writing — verify) |
| Excise taxes | Specific products (tobacco, sugary drinks, energy drinks) | Several GCC states apply excise taxes |
| Fees | Port, inspection, documentation, legalisation | Local |

:::example Import duty and VAT calculation (fictional, Saudi-style VAT at 15% for illustration)
Goods: FOB $80,000; freight $3,200; insurance $120 → CIF **$83,320**. Duty 5% → **$4,166**. VAT 15% on (83,320 + 4,166) = 15% × 87,486 = **$13,122.90**. Customs clearance and port charges $900.

- Cash paid at import: duty 4,166 + VAT 13,122.90 + charges 900 = **$18,188.90**.
- Cost to a VAT-registered importer (VAT recoverable): 4,166 + 900 = **$5,066** plus CIF.
- The VAT is a cash-flow cost until recovered — significant for working capital (two to three months).
:::

## Origin and preferential trade

Preferential duty requires (1) goods meeting the agreement’s **rules of origin**, (2) direct transport or permitted transit, and (3) a valid **proof of origin**. Customs can verify origin retroactively; if certificates are found invalid, the importer pays back-duties and possibly penalties. Non-preferential origin matters for anti-dumping duties, sanctions, country-of-origin labelling and public procurement.

## Customs procedures and special regimes

| Regime | Purpose | Business use |
|---|---|---|
| Release for free circulation (home use) | Normal import with duties paid | Standard |
| Customs warehousing / bonded warehouse | Store goods with duties suspended | Distribution hubs; re-exports |
| Free zones | Goods enter without duties; duties due when entering the domestic market | Regional trading and logistics (e.g., Jebel Ali Free Zone) |
| Temporary admission | Temporary import with duty suspension (exhibitions, equipment); often under ATA Carnet | Trade fairs, project equipment |
| Inward processing | Import inputs duty-suspended for processing and re-export | Manufacturing for export |
| Transit | Movement through a country under customs control | Land transit corridors |
| Re-export | Export of previously imported goods; duty refunds in some cases | Trading hubs |
| Authorised Economic Operator (AEO) | Trusted-trader status (WCO SAFE Framework) | Faster clearance, fewer inspections |

## The customs broker’s role

Customs brokers (clearing agents) prepare and file declarations, classify goods, calculate duties, arrange inspections, and liaise with customs. The **importer of record** remains legally responsible for the declaration in most countries — choose brokers carefully and review their work.

:::warn Customs risks that destroy margins
- Misclassification leading to back-duties and penalties years later.
- Under-valuation (including undeclared royalties or assists) — treated as fraud.
- Invalid preferential origin certificates.
- Missing product-conformity certificates causing goods to be held or re-exported.
- Duty payable on CIF when you priced on FOB.
- Demurrage and storage charges accumulating while documents are corrected.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. CIF value $120,000; duty 12%; VAT 14% on duty-paid value; clearance $1,100. Compute total import cash outflow and the non-recoverable cost for a VAT-registered importer.
2. An importer pays a royalty of 5% of resale value to the foreign brand owner as a condition of sale. Is it part of customs value? Why does it matter?
3. Why do traders use free zones in the Gulf for regional distribution?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Duty = 14,400; VAT = 14% × 134,400 = 18,816; total cash = 14,400 + 18,816 + 1,100 = **$34,316**. Non-recoverable cost = 14,400 + 1,100 = **$15,500** (VAT recovered later).
2. Generally yes — royalties related to the goods and payable as a condition of sale are added to transaction value under the WTO Agreement. Omitting them under-declares value, risking back-duties and penalties.
3. Goods can be stored, repacked and re-exported without paying duties; duty is paid only on goods entering the local market; plus logistics infrastructure, 100% foreign ownership regimes in many free zones, and fast clearance.
:::

# Payment Methods in International Trade: Advance Payment, Open Account, Collections, Letters of Credit and Guarantees

:::obj In this chapter
The five payment methods ranked by risk, the mechanics and rules of documentary collections (URC 522), letters of credit (UCP 600), standby letters of credit (ISP98/UCP 600) and demand guarantees (URDG 758), with timelines, costs and choice criteria.
:::

## The risk ladder

| Method | Seller’s risk | Buyer’s risk | Cost | When used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Advance payment (cash in advance) | Lowest | Highest (pays before goods) | Low | New/risky buyers, small orders, custom goods, strong seller bargaining power |
| Letter of credit (especially confirmed) | Low (if documents comply) | Low-medium (pays against documents, not goods) | Higher (bank fees) | New relationships, large values, risky countries |
| Documentary collection (D/P, D/A) | Medium (buyer may refuse documents) | Low-medium | Moderate | Established relationships, moderate risk |
| Open account | Highest (ships, then waits) | Lowest | Lowest | Trusted relationships, competitive markets; often with credit insurance or receivables finance |
| Consignment | Highest (paid only when goods resold) | Lowest | — | Distribution, new market entry |

Advance payment and open account are often combined with **bank guarantees** (advance-payment guarantee protects the buyer; payment guarantee/SBLC protects the seller) or **credit insurance**.

## Advance payment

The buyer pays all or part before shipment (or before production). The buyer’s protection: an **advance-payment guarantee (APG)** from the seller’s bank, refund guarantees, or escrow. Many contracts use partial advances: e.g., 30% with order, 70% against copy of B/L — the seller should keep the original B/L until the balance is paid.

## Open account

Goods and documents go directly to the buyer; payment is due at an agreed date (e.g., 60 days from invoice). Dominant in trade between affiliated companies and established partners (a majority of world trade by value is on open account). Seller protections: credit limits, credit insurance, receivables finance (with or without recourse), SBLC as payment security, retention of title clauses (effectiveness depends on law), and close monitoring.

## Documentary collections (URC 522)

The seller’s bank (**remitting bank**) sends documents to the buyer’s bank (**collecting/presenting bank**) with instructions to release them only against:

- **D/P — documents against payment** (at sight), or
- **D/A — documents against acceptance** of a bill of exchange payable at a future date (e.g., 90 days after sight); the buyer receives documents (and goods) and is legally bound on the accepted draft, but the seller now has payment risk on the buyer.

Key points of URC 522:

- Banks act on the collection instruction; they have **no obligation to pay** and do not examine documents beyond checking they appear as listed.
- Goods should not be consigned to a bank without its prior agreement.
- If documents are unpaid/unaccepted, the presenting bank informs the remitting bank; **protest** for non-payment or non-acceptance happens only if instructed.
- Charges: specify who pays; if the buyer refuses charges, the bank may release documents without collecting them unless the instruction says charges may not be waived.

:::warn The seller’s main risk in collections
If the buyer refuses to pay or accept, the goods sit at the destination port accumulating demurrage and storage charges, and the seller must find another buyer, ship them back, or abandon them. D/P protects against losing the goods without payment, not against non-acceptance. It suits reliable buyers in stable countries with goods easy to resell.
:::

## Letters of credit (UCP 600)

### Parties and roles

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| Applicant | Buyer requesting the credit |
| Issuing bank | Buyer’s bank; gives the irrevocable undertaking to honour complying presentations |
| Beneficiary | Seller |
| Advising bank | Authenticates the credit and advises it to the beneficiary (no payment obligation unless it confirms) |
| Confirming bank | Adds its own irrevocable undertaking at the issuing bank’s request/authorisation |
| Nominated bank | Bank authorised to honour or negotiate (e.g., “available with any bank by negotiation”) |
| Reimbursing bank | Pays claims from nominated/confirming bank on behalf of the issuing bank |

### Core principles (paraphrasing UCP 600)

- **Irrevocability:** a credit is irrevocable even if it does not say so (art. 3).
- **Independence:** the credit is separate from the sales contract; banks are not concerned with the contract (art. 4).
- **Documents, not goods:** banks deal with documents, not with goods or services (art. 5).
- **Complying presentation:** documents must comply with the credit terms, UCP 600 and international standard banking practice (ISBP) (art. 2, 14).
- **Examination time:** banks have a **maximum of five banking days** following the day of presentation to determine compliance (art. 14(b)).
- **Presentation period:** if the credit requires an original transport document, presentation must be made not later than **21 calendar days after the date of shipment**, and in any case not later than the expiry date (art. 14(c)).
- **Data consistency:** data need not be identical but must not conflict with other documents or the credit (art. 14(d)).
- **Refusal:** a bank refusing must give a **single notice** stating all discrepancies (art. 16); otherwise it is precluded from claiming non-compliance.
- **Tolerances:** “about” or “approximately” allows ±10% on amount, quantity or unit price; ±5% quantity tolerance applies where quantity is not stated in packing units or individual items and the drawing does not exceed the credit amount (art. 30).
- **Partial drawings and shipments** are allowed unless the credit prohibits them (art. 31).
- **Insurance** must be at least 110% of CIF/CIP value if not otherwise stated (art. 28).

### Types of credit

| Type | Feature | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Sight | Paid on complying presentation | Standard |
| Usance / deferred payment / acceptance | Paid at a future date (e.g., 90 days after B/L date) | Buyer credit; seller may discount |
| Confirmed | Second bank’s undertaking | Issuing bank or country risk |
| Silent confirmation | Bank adds protection at the beneficiary’s request without the issuing bank’s authorisation (outside UCP’s confirmation concept) | Seller wants protection when issuing bank won’t request confirmation |
| Transferable (art. 38) | Beneficiary (often a middleman) transfers all or part to a second beneficiary | Trading intermediaries |
| Back-to-back | Second, separate L/C issued on the strength of the first | Traders (bank takes risk on mismatch) |
| Revolving | Reinstates automatically for repeated shipments | Regular supplies |
| Red clause / green clause | Allows advances to beneficiary before shipment | Commodity sourcing (rare today) |
| Standby (SBLC) | Payable on default; guarantee-like | Payment or performance security, often under ISP98 |

### Step by step

1. Sales contract specifies payment by L/C with key terms.
2. Buyer applies to its bank; bank approves (credit line, margin deposit) and issues the credit (typically via SWIFT MT700).
3. Advising bank authenticates and advises the seller; confirming bank adds confirmation if requested.
4. Seller checks the credit **immediately** and requests amendments if terms cannot be met (amendments require consent of the issuing bank, confirming bank and beneficiary).
5. Seller ships and collects documents.
6. Seller presents documents to the nominated/confirming bank within the presentation period.
7. Bank examines (≤ 5 banking days). If compliant, it honours/negotiates and forwards documents to the issuing bank; if discrepant, it notifies refusal (or seeks the applicant’s waiver).
8. Issuing bank examines, reimburses the nominated bank, and releases documents to the buyer against payment or under a financing arrangement (import loan/trust receipt).
9. Buyer uses documents to clear and collect the goods.

### Costs (illustrative — vary widely by bank, country and risk)

| Fee | Typical basis | Paid by (usually) |
|---|---|---|
| Issuance commission | 0.1–0.5% per quarter of L/C amount | Applicant |
| Advising fee | Flat fee | Beneficiary |
| Confirmation fee | 0.1–2%+ per quarter depending on issuing bank/country risk | Beneficiary (or applicant if agreed) |
| Negotiation / payment commission | % or flat | Beneficiary |
| Acceptance / deferred payment commission | % per annum for usance period | Per contract |
| Discrepancy fee | Flat per presentation (e.g., $50–150) | Beneficiary |
| Amendment fee | Flat | Party requesting |
| Courier and SWIFT charges | Flat | Each party |
| Discounting interest | Reference rate + margin for usance period | Beneficiary (or applicant under “usance payable at sight”) |

### Standby letters of credit and demand guarantees

**Standby L/Cs** and **demand guarantees** are paid against a demand (and sometimes a statement of default), not against shipping documents. They are independent of the underlying contract.

- **URDG 758** (demand guarantees): a demand must be supported by a statement indicating in what respect the applicant is in breach (art. 15) unless excluded; the guarantor has **five business days** to examine (art. 20); provisions for expiry, reduction, extend-or-pay demands, force majeure, and counter-guarantees.
- **ISP98** governs many standbys (especially US-style); UCP 600 can also apply to standbys “to the extent applicable”.

| Guarantee | Protects | Typical amount | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bid bond (tender guarantee) | Employer, if bidder withdraws or refuses to sign | 1–5% of bid | Bid validity |
| Performance guarantee | Buyer/employer against non-performance | 5–10% (sometimes more) | Until completion/acceptance |
| Advance-payment guarantee | Buyer’s advance | Equal to advance, reducing as work progresses | Until advance recovered |
| Retention guarantee | Replaces cash retentions | 5–10% | Defects liability period |
| Warranty / maintenance guarantee | Defects after delivery | 5–10% | Warranty period |
| Payment guarantee / SBLC | Seller against buyer non-payment | Credit exposure | Credit period |
| Customs guarantee | Customs authority for duties under suspension regimes | Duties at risk | Regime duration |

:::warn “On-demand” means on demand
Beneficiaries can call demand guarantees with a compliant demand even if the applicant disputes the breach. Courts in many jurisdictions intervene only in narrow cases (e.g., clear fraud). Exporters and contractors must treat performance guarantees as potentially lost cash, negotiate reasonable amounts and expiry dates, and avoid “extend or pay” traps.
:::

## Choosing a payment method

| Factor | Pushes toward more security (advance, confirmed L/C) | Pushes toward less (open account) |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer creditworthiness | Unknown, weak | Strong, rated, long history |
| Country risk | High, FX controls | Low |
| Amount | Large relative to seller’s equity | Small |
| Goods | Customised, perishable, hard to resell | Standard, liquid |
| Competition | Seller’s market | Buyer’s market; competitors offer terms |
| Relationship | New | Long-standing |
| Financing need | Seller needs bank-acceptable security for finance | Seller has strong balance sheet or insurance |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Documents are presented on Monday 3 June (a banking day). The credit expires 30 June; B/L dated 20 May. Is the presentation timely? By when must the bank decide (assume no holidays and Saturday–Sunday weekends)?
2. Credit amount “about USD 500,000” for “about 1,000 MT of sugar”. What maximum quantity and amount are allowed?
3. A buyer insists on D/A 120 days; the seller is unsure of the buyer’s credit. Propose two ways to make this acceptable.
4. Compare a bank guarantee and a commercial L/C in terms of when the bank expects to pay.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. 21 days after 20 May = 10 June; presentation on 3 June is within both the 21-day period and expiry — **timely**. Examination: up to five banking days following the day of presentation → Tue 4, Wed 5, Thu 6, Fri 7, Mon 10 June → decision **by 10 June**.
2. Up to **1,100 MT** and **USD 550,000** (±10% on both under art. 30(a)), subject to unit price consistency.
3. Credit insurance covering the buyer; an SBLC or avalised draft (bank guarantee of the acceptance); or forfaiting/discounting without recourse with a bank aval; or reduce exposure with partial advance payment.
4. A commercial L/C is expected to be paid in the normal course (it is the payment mechanism). A guarantee/standby is expected **not** to be paid — it is called only if the applicant defaults.
:::

# Discrepancies, Disputes, Cargo Claims, Demurrage and Trade Risk Protection

:::obj In this chapter
What happens when things go wrong: documentary discrepancies, payment disputes, cargo loss and damage claims, demurrage and detention, and the protection offered by trade credit insurance and export credit agencies — plus trade-based financial crime.
:::

## Documentary discrepancies

Industry surveys have long found that a large share of first presentations under L/Cs are discrepant. Common discrepancies:

| Discrepancy | Example | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Late shipment | B/L on-board date after latest shipment date | Realistic dates; amend early |
| Late presentation | More than 21 days after shipment or after expiry | Courier planning; present locally to the nominated bank |
| Description of goods | Invoice description differs from credit | Copy credit wording exactly in the invoice |
| Inconsistent data | Weights, marks, quantities differ across documents | Single source data; checklist |
| Missing documents | Inspection certificate not presented | Document checklist from the credit |
| Wrong consignee / endorsement | B/L “to order” not endorsed | Know the endorsement chain |
| Unclean B/L | Notation “packaging torn” | Proper packing; resolve before shipment |
| Insurance | Under-insured, wrong risks, dated after shipment | Insure correctly and early |
| Amount | Drawing exceeds credit or tolerance | Check arithmetic |
| Signatures / issuers | Certificate issued by wrong party | Use specified issuers |
| Port names | Different from credit | Exact names |

**What happens next:** the bank issues a refusal notice listing discrepancies and states whether it holds documents pending the applicant’s waiver, holds until further instructions, returns them, or acts on prior instructions (UCP 600 art. 16). In practice, most discrepant presentations are eventually paid after the applicant waives — **if** the buyer still wants the goods at that price. In a falling market, buyers use discrepancies to renegotiate or reject. A discrepancy converts a bank undertaking into, effectively, a collection.

:::tip The seller’s defence: check the credit on day one
Read the L/C as soon as it is advised and verify that every requirement can be met by whom and when. Request amendments before shipment — after shipment, the seller has lost leverage. Many traders use a document-checking checklist and a pre-check by their bank before presentation.
:::

## Payment disputes and remedies

| Situation | Typical remedies |
|---|---|
| Buyer refuses to pay under open account | Negotiation, demand letter, credit-insurance claim (after waiting period), collection agency, arbitration/litigation under contract |
| Bank refuses L/C documents | Check if refusal valid under UCP/ISBP; challenge the refusal; ICC DOCDEX expert decision; litigation |
| Unfair guarantee call | Negotiate; seek injunction only in exceptional cases (e.g., fraud) per governing law |
| Quality dispute | Joint survey, independent inspection, contract claims procedure, arbitration |
| Fraud (fake documents, phantom shipments) | Criminal complaint, bank notification, fraud exception in L/C law |

## Cargo claims

Loss or damage may be claimed against the **carrier** (within liability regimes and limits) and/or the **cargo insurer** (which then pursues the carrier by subrogation).

| Regime | Mode | Carrier liability limit (summary) | Time bar (summary) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hague-Visby Rules | Sea | Higher of 666.67 SDR per package/unit or 2 SDR per kg gross | 1 year from delivery |
| Hamburg Rules | Sea (fewer countries) | Higher of 835 SDR per package or 2.5 SDR per kg | 2 years |
| Montreal Convention 1999 | Air | Per-kg limit in SDR, periodically revised (raised to 26 SDR/kg from December 2024 — verify) | 2 years; written complaint within 14 days for damage |
| CMR | International road | 8.33 SDR per kg | 1 year (3 for wilful misconduct) |

SDR = Special Drawing Right, the IMF’s reserve unit. Carriers also have defences (e.g., under Hague-Visby: perils of the sea, insufficient packing, inherent vice, nautical fault).

**Claims procedure:** notify carrier in writing promptly (note damage on delivery receipt; for non-apparent damage, within the regime’s period, e.g., 3 days under Hague-Visby); arrange a joint survey; preserve evidence (photos, seals, temperature logs); notify insurer immediately; mitigate further loss; submit claim with invoice, B/L, packing list, survey report, insurance certificate, correspondence.

**General average:** a maritime principle under which all parties to a sea venture share proportionally in extraordinary sacrifices or expenses made to save the voyage (e.g., jettison, salvage). Cargo owners must post security (often a general average guarantee from their insurer) before cargo is released — another reason to insure.

## Demurrage and detention

:::example Container D&D after a document delay (fictional rates)
Two 40ft containers arrive at destination. Free time: 5 days demurrage, 5 days detention (separate clocks, combined free time arrangements vary). Original B/Ls arrive 9 days late because the seller sent them by ordinary mail; customs inspection takes 3 more days; containers are collected on day 14 and returned empty on day 20.

| Charge | Days chargeable | Rate | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demurrage (in terminal) | 14 − 5 = 9 days | $90 for days 6–10, $150 thereafter per container | (5 × 90 + 4 × 150) × 2 = **$2,100** |
| Detention (outside terminal) | 6 days out, 5 free → 1 day | $60/day per container | **$120** |
| Port storage (terminal) | per terminal tariff | | **$640** |
| **Total** | | | **$2,860** |

On goods worth $48,000 with a 12% gross margin ($5,760), this delay costs half the profit. Who pays depends on the sales contract and Incoterms — under CFR the buyer bears destination costs, but may claim against the seller if the delay was caused by the seller’s late documents.
:::

## Trade credit insurance

Covers a seller (or its financing bank) against non-payment by buyers due to **commercial risks** (insolvency, protracted default) and often **political risks** (transfer restrictions, war, cancellation of licences). Features: credit limits approved per buyer, indemnity typically 85–95% of the loss, waiting period before claim (e.g., 3–6 months after due date), obligations to report overdue accounts, premium as % of turnover or insured receivables. Major private insurers include Allianz Trade, Atradius and Coface; many countries also have state or quasi-state insurers.

Benefits beyond loss protection: better financing terms (banks lend more against insured receivables), credit information on buyers, and discipline in credit management.

## Export credit agencies (ECAs)

ECAs are government or government-mandated institutions supporting exports through insurance, guarantees and sometimes direct lending, especially for capital goods and projects with medium/long tenors. Examples include UK Export Finance, the Export-Import Bank of the United States, Euler Hermes acting for the German government, Sinosure (China), Etihad Credit Insurance (UAE), Saudi EXIM Bank and the multilateral ICIEC (Islamic Development Bank group). For OECD members, terms of officially supported export credits (minimum premiums, maximum tenors, down payments — typically at least 15%) follow the **OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits**.

| ECA product | How it works |
|---|---|
| Buyer credit | Bank lends to the foreign buyer to pay the exporter; ECA guarantees the bank (often 95–100%) |
| Supplier credit insurance | Exporter grants credit; ECA insures the receivable |
| Pre-shipment / working-capital guarantees | ECA guarantees bank loans to exporters |
| Bond support | ECA shares risk on performance/APG guarantees issued by banks |
| Investment insurance | Political risk cover for foreign investments |

## Trade-based financial crime

Trade finance is exposed to fraud and money laundering because documents are complex and banks see only paper. Typologies: fictitious shipments, over/under-invoicing, multiple financing of the same goods by different banks, fake warehouse receipts, collusion between buyer and seller, misuse of free zones, dual-use goods diversion, and sanctions evasion through ship-to-ship transfers or falsified origin. Controls: KYC on all parties, pricing checks against market data, vessel tracking and B/L verification, collateral management with reputable firms, independent inspection, limits on related-party trades, and attention to the red flags described in {{ch:KYC, AML}}.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A credit requires shipment “not later than 15 May”; the B/L is dated 17 May. The buyer’s market price has fallen 10%. What is likely to happen, and what could the seller have done?
2. A carton of electronics worth $9,000, weighing 40 kg, is lost at sea. Under Hague-Visby, what is the carrier’s maximum liability (assume 1 SDR = $1.33)?
3. A credit insurer approved a $500,000 limit on a buyer; indemnity 90%; buyer defaults owing $620,000. How much does the seller recover from the insurer?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. The late shipment is a discrepancy; the issuing bank will refuse unless the applicant waives. With prices down, the buyer may refuse or demand a price reduction. The seller should have requested an amendment extending the shipment (and expiry) date before shipping.
2. Package limit: 666.67 SDR ≈ $887; weight limit: 2 × 40 = 80 SDR ≈ $106 → higher = **≈ $887**, far below the $9,000 value — hence the need for cargo insurance (or declaring value on the B/L, which raises the limit and the freight).
3. Insured amount limited to the approved limit: 90% × $500,000 = **$450,000** (the excess $120,000 is uninsured).
:::
