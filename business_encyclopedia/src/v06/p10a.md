Capital markets connect those who have capital with those who need it, and price the risk in between. For a business professional, understanding investment is useful three times over: to raise capital for a company, to evaluate companies as a lender, partner or acquirer, and to manage personal and institutional wealth. This part explains the structure of equity and debt markets, the main investment vehicles and managers, portfolio construction, derivatives, and how professional investors research and decide.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part X
- Explain primary and secondary markets, IPOs, and the roles of exchanges, brokers and clearing houses.
- Analyse equities with market capitalisation, multiples and dividend measures.
- Price bonds, compute yields and duration, and interpret credit ratings and yield curves.
- Distinguish mutual funds, ETFs, private equity, venture capital and hedge funds.
- Apply risk-return, diversification and asset-allocation principles; compute risk-adjusted returns.
- Explain futures, forwards, options and swaps; distinguish investing, trading, speculation, hedging and arbitrage.
- Conduct investment research and due diligence and write an investment recommendation.
:::

# Capital Markets: Structure, Primary and Secondary Markets, and IPOs

:::obj In this chapter
How capital markets are organised, who the participants are, how securities are issued and traded, and how an initial public offering works.
:::

## Market structure

| Market | Instruments | Main participants |
|---|---|---|
| Equity markets | Ordinary and preference shares, depositary receipts | Issuers, institutional and retail investors, brokers, market makers, exchanges |
| Debt (fixed income) markets | Government bonds, corporate bonds, sukuk, commercial paper, securitised products | Governments, corporates, banks, pension funds, insurers |
| Money markets | Treasury bills, certificates of deposit, repos, commercial paper (< 1 year) | Banks, corporates, money-market funds, central banks |
| Derivatives markets | Futures, options, swaps, forwards | Hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs, dealers |
| FX markets | Spot, forwards, swaps, options | Banks, corporates, investors |
| Commodities markets | Futures, physical | Producers, consumers, traders |

**Primary market:** issuers sell new securities to investors and receive the cash. **Secondary market:** investors trade existing securities among themselves; the issuer receives nothing, but secondary liquidity makes primary issuance possible and provides price signals.

## Market infrastructure

- **Exchanges** (e.g., Saudi Exchange (Tadawul), Dubai Financial Market, Abu Dhabi Securities Exchange, Boursa Kuwait, Qatar Stock Exchange, the Egyptian Exchange, NYSE, Nasdaq, London Stock Exchange) provide organised trading with listing rules.
- **Over-the-counter (OTC) markets:** bilateral trading through dealers (most bonds, FX, many derivatives).
- **Brokers** execute orders for clients; **dealers/market makers** trade from their own inventory, quoting bid and ask.
- **Central counterparties (CCPs)/clearing houses** interpose themselves between buyers and sellers, reducing counterparty risk through margining.
- **Central securities depositories (CSDs)** hold securities electronically and settle transfers (delivery versus payment).
- **Custodians** safekeep assets for investors.
- **Regulators** (securities commissions, capital market authorities) supervise disclosure, conduct and market integrity.

## Order types and trading

Market orders (execute now at the best price), limit orders (execute only at a set price or better), stop orders (trigger when a price is reached). Liquidity is measured by bid-ask spread, depth and volume. Settlement cycles: many equity markets settle on T+2; the US moved to **T+1** in May 2024 and other markets are planning similar moves.

## Initial public offerings (IPOs)

| Phase | Activities |
|---|---|
| Preparation (6–18 months) | Governance (independent directors, committees), audited IFRS statements (typically 3 years), reporting systems, legal restructuring, choice of exchange |
| Advisers | Lead managers/bookrunners, legal counsel, auditors, IR advisers, receiving banks |
| Due diligence and prospectus | Business, financial and legal due diligence; prospectus approved by regulator |
| Valuation and price range | Comparable companies, DCF; investor education by analysts; anchor/cornerstone investors |
| Bookbuilding | Institutional orders at different prices; retail tranche in many Middle Eastern IPOs |
| Pricing and allocation | Final price set; allocations to investors |
| Listing and trading | First trading day; stabilisation (greenshoe option) in some markets |
| Post-IPO | Continuous disclosure, quarterly reporting, investor relations, lock-up periods for existing shareholders |

Why companies go public: raise growth capital, provide an exit for founders/investors, currency for acquisitions (shares), visibility and credibility, employee incentives. Costs: fees (often several percent of proceeds), disclosure burden, short-term market pressure, loss of privacy and some control.

:::ar الأسواق الأولية والثانوية والطرح العام الأولي
في السوق الأولية تبيع الشركة أوراقاً مالية جديدة وتحصل على الأموال، أما في السوق الثانوية فيتداول المستثمرون الأوراق القائمة فيما بينهم دون أن تحصل الشركة على شيء، لكن سيولة السوق الثانوية هي ما يجعل الإصدار الأولي ممكناً. والطرح العام الأولي (IPO) هو أول بيع لأسهم الشركة للجمهور، ويتطلب حوكمة قوية وقوائم مالية مدققة ونشرة إصدار معتمدة من الجهة الرقابية وعملية بناء سجل الأوامر لتحديد السعر.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A company sells 20 million new shares at $10 in an IPO, while existing shareholders sell 10 million shares. How much does the company receive (before fees)?
2. Why do IPO investors care about lock-up periods?
3. Distinguish a broker from a market maker.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. **$200m** (primary shares only). The $100m from secondary shares goes to selling shareholders.
2. When lock-ups expire, insiders can sell large volumes, potentially pressuring the share price; lock-ups also signal insiders’ commitment.
3. A broker acts as agent, executing client orders for a commission; a market maker acts as principal, buying and selling from its own inventory and earning the bid-ask spread while providing liquidity.
:::

# Equity Investing: Market Capitalisation, Valuation Multiples and Dividends

:::obj In this chapter
What shareholders own, sources of equity returns, valuation multiples and their interpretation, dividend investing, and how to read a stock in a structured way.
:::

## Sources of equity returns

:::formula Total shareholder return
TSR = (Price_end − Price_start + Dividends) ÷ Price_start
Long-run decomposition (approximate): Return ≈ Dividend yield + Earnings growth + Change in valuation multiple
:::

Over long periods, earnings growth and dividends drive most returns; changes in multiples dominate short periods.

## Market capitalisation and classification

Market cap = share price × shares outstanding. Categories (conventional, vary by market): large cap, mid cap, small cap, micro cap. **Free float** — shares available for public trading (excluding strategic and government holdings) — matters for index weights and liquidity; many Gulf companies have large government or family holdings and limited free float.

## Valuation multiples

| Multiple | Formula | Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| P/E | Price ÷ EPS | Most quoted; profitable companies | Distorted by one-offs, leverage, accounting |
| Forward P/E | Price ÷ next-year EPS estimate | Market expectations | Depends on forecasts |
| PEG | P/E ÷ EPS growth rate (%) | Adjust P/E for growth | Growth estimates uncertain |
| EV/EBITDA | EV ÷ EBITDA | Comparing across capital structures | Ignores capex and working capital |
| EV/EBIT | EV ÷ EBIT | Capital-intensive firms | — |
| EV/Sales | EV ÷ revenue | Loss-making or early-stage companies | Ignores profitability |
| P/B | Price ÷ book value per share | Banks, insurers, asset-heavy firms | Book values may be stale |
| Dividend yield | DPS ÷ price | Income investors | High yield may signal risk |
| FCF yield | FCF ÷ market cap (or EV) | Cash generation | FCF volatile |

:::example Reading two fictional listed companies
| | Company P (logistics) | Company Q (software) |
|---|---|---|
| Share price | $24.00 | $60.00 |
| EPS | $2.00 | $1.20 |
| P/E | 12.0× | 50.0× |
| Expected EPS growth | 6% | 30% |
| PEG | 2.0 | 1.7 |
| Dividend yield | 4.5% | 0% |
| Net debt/EBITDA | 2.5× | net cash |

Q looks “expensive” on P/E but cheaper on PEG; P offers income but slower growth and more debt. The right choice depends on confidence in Q’s growth, the investor’s income needs and risk tolerance.
:::

## Dividends

Dividend investing focuses on stable, growing dividends. Key measures: **payout ratio** (dividends ÷ earnings), **dividend cover** (earnings ÷ dividends), **FCF cover**, dividend history and policy. Warning signs: payout above 100% of earnings or FCF, dividends funded by debt, a yield far above peers (the market may expect a cut).

:::formula Dividend discount model (Gordon growth)
P₀ = D₁ ÷ (Ke − g)
Implied cost of equity: Ke = D₁ ÷ P₀ + g
:::

Example: next dividend $1.20, expected growth 4%, required return 10% → value = 1.20 ÷ 0.06 = **$20.00**.

## A structured approach to analysing a stock

1. **Business quality:** model, moat, industry structure, management, capital allocation record.
2. **Financials:** growth, margins, ROIC vs WACC, cash conversion, balance sheet.
3. **Valuation:** multiples vs history and peers, DCF, implied expectations (“what growth does the price assume?”).
4. **Catalysts and risks:** what could change the market’s view; what could go wrong.
5. **Position sizing and exit criteria.**

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Bought a share at $50, received $2 of dividends, sold at $56 after one year. Compute TSR and its components.
2. A stock trades at $40 with expected dividend next year $1.60 and long-term growth 5%. What cost of equity is implied?
3. Why is a P/E ratio not meaningful for a bank-like comparison with an industrial company?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. TSR = (56 − 50 + 2) ÷ 50 = **16%**: price return 12%, dividend yield 4%.
2. Ke = 1.60 ÷ 40 + 5% = **9%**.
3. P/E itself can be computed for both, but comparing it across very different business models, leverage levels and accounting (banks’ earnings depend on provisions and leverage; industrials on capex cycles) is misleading; compare within sectors and use sector-specific metrics (P/B and ROE for banks; EV/EBITDA for industrials).
:::

# Fixed Income: Bond Pricing, Yields, Credit Ratings and the Yield Curve

:::obj In this chapter
How bonds work, how they are priced, yield measures, duration and convexity, credit ratings and spreads, the yield curve, and sukuk.
:::

## Bond terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Face value / par | Amount repaid at maturity (e.g., $1,000 or 100%) |
| Coupon | Annual interest as % of par; paid annually or semi-annually |
| Maturity | Date of final repayment |
| Issue price | Price at issuance (at, above or below par) |
| Yield to maturity (YTM) | Discount rate equating the present value of cash flows to the price |
| Seniority / security | Ranking in default (senior secured, senior unsecured, subordinated) |
| Covenants | Restrictions on the issuer (often incurrence-based for high-yield bonds) |
| Call / put options | Issuer may redeem early / investor may demand early repayment |
| Accrued interest | Interest since last coupon; buyer pays seller “dirty price” = clean price + accrued |

## Pricing

:::formula Bond price
Price = Σ [C ÷ (1 + y)ᵗ] + F ÷ (1 + y)ⁿ
C = coupon payment; F = face value; y = yield per period; n = number of periods.
Price and yield move in opposite directions.
:::

:::example A 5-year 6% annual-coupon bond
- If market yield = 6% → price = **100.00** (par).
- If yield = 7% → price = 6 × 4.1002 + 100 ÷ 1.40255 = 24.60 + 71.30 = **95.90** (discount).
- If yield = 5% → price = 6 × 4.3295 + 100 ÷ 1.27628 = 25.98 + 78.35 = **104.33** (premium).
- Current yield at 95.90 = 6 ÷ 95.90 = **6.26%**.
:::

## Duration and convexity

**Macaulay duration** is the weighted-average time to receive the bond’s cash flows (weights = PV of each cash flow ÷ price). **Modified duration** ≈ Macaulay ÷ (1 + y) measures price sensitivity: %ΔPrice ≈ −Modified duration × Δy.

:::example Duration of the 6% bond at 7%
PVs of cash flows: 5.61, 5.24, 4.90, 4.58, 75.58 (total 95.90). Weighted times: Σ t × PV = 426.97 → Macaulay duration = 426.97 ÷ 95.90 = **4.45 years**; modified duration = 4.45 ÷ 1.07 = **4.16**.
If yields rise 1 point to 8%, estimated price change ≈ −4.16% → 91.91; actual price at 8% = **92.01** — the small difference is **convexity** (prices fall less and rise more than duration predicts).
:::

Practical uses: banks and insurers match asset and liability durations; bond investors adjust duration according to rate views; companies understand why long-term fixed-rate debt changes in market value.

## Credit ratings and spreads

Rating agencies (S&P Global Ratings, Moody’s, Fitch, and regional agencies) assess default risk.

| Category | S&P/Fitch | Moody’s | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investment grade | AAA, AA, A, BBB | Aaa, Aa, A, Baa | Lower default risk; eligible for many institutional mandates |
| High yield (speculative) | BB, B, CCC and below | Ba, B, Caa and below | Higher default risk; higher spreads |
| Default | D (S&P), D/RD (Fitch) | C (often) | In default |

A bond’s yield = benchmark government yield (risk-free in that currency) + **credit spread** (compensation for default risk, liquidity and other factors). Spreads widen in recessions and crises. A downgrade from BBB− to BB+ (“fallen angel”) can force sales by investment-grade-only investors.

## The yield curve

The yield curve plots yields by maturity for the same issuer (usually the government). Shapes: **normal** (upward), **flat**, **inverted** (downward — often associated with expected rate cuts and slowing growth), **humped**. Theories: expectations of future short rates, term premia for longer maturities, and supply/demand segments.

## Sukuk

Sukuk are Sharia-compliant certificates giving holders ownership interests in assets or ventures (ijara sukuk based on leases, murabaha, wakala, mudaraba structures). Economically many resemble bonds (periodic distributions, redemption at maturity), and they are significant in Gulf and Malaysian markets for governments, banks and corporates. Structural features (asset ownership, purchase undertakings) affect credit analysis.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Price a 3-year 8% annual-coupon bond (face 100) at a yield of 6%.
2. A bond has modified duration 6.5. Yields rise by 0.40 percentage points. Estimate the price change.
3. A BBB corporate bond yields 6.1% when the same-maturity government bond yields 4.3%. What is the spread in basis points? What might cause it to widen?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. 8 ÷ 1.06 + 8 ÷ 1.1236 + 108 ÷ 1.191016 = 7.547 + 7.120 + 90.679 = **105.35**.
2. ≈ −6.5 × 0.40% = **−2.6%**.
3. **180 bps**. Widening causes: weaker issuer results, downgrade risk, sector stress, recession fears, lower market liquidity, higher risk aversion.
:::

# Investment Funds and Asset Managers: Mutual Funds, ETFs, Private Equity, Venture Capital and Hedge Funds

:::obj In this chapter
How pooled investment vehicles work, their fee structures, liquidity, strategies and roles; what investment mandates are.
:::

## Comparison of vehicles

| Vehicle | Investors | Liquidity | Typical strategy | Fees (indicative) | Regulation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mutual fund (open-ended) | Retail and institutional | Daily at NAV | Equities, bonds, money market, balanced | Management fee 0.5–2% (index funds lower) | Heavily regulated (e.g., UCITS in the EU) |
| ETF | Retail and institutional | Intraday on exchange | Mostly index tracking; some active | Often 0.03–0.75% | Regulated; market makers keep price near NAV |
| Closed-end fund / REIT | Retail and institutional | Exchange-traded; price may differ from NAV | Real estate, credit, private assets | Varies | Regulated |
| Private equity fund | Institutions, wealthy individuals | Locked ~10 years | Buyouts, growth equity | “2 and 20”: ~1.5–2% management fee + ~20% carried interest above a hurdle | Lighter; professional investors |
| Venture capital fund | Institutions, family offices | Locked ~10+ years | Early-stage startups | Similar to PE | Professional investors |
| Hedge fund | Institutions, wealthy individuals | Monthly/quarterly with notice; lock-ups | Long/short, macro, arbitrage, credit, quant | Management 1–2% + performance 10–20% | Lighter; professional investors |
| Private credit fund | Institutions | Locked | Direct lending to mid-sized companies | Management + performance | Professional investors |
| Sovereign wealth fund | Government-owned | Long-term | Global diversified, strategic | — | Government mandates |

## Key concepts

- **NAV (net asset value) per unit** = (assets − liabilities) ÷ units outstanding.
- **Active vs passive:** active managers try to beat a benchmark; passive funds track it at low cost. After fees, many active funds underperform their benchmarks over long periods — a key argument for passive investing in efficient markets.
- **Private equity mechanics:** limited partners (LPs) commit capital; the general partner (GP) calls capital over an investment period, invests, manages portfolio companies, exits (sale, IPO), and distributes proceeds. Performance measures: IRR, MOIC/TVPI (total value to paid-in), DPI (distributions to paid-in). The **J-curve**: early negative returns from fees and unrealised investments, followed by gains.
- **Venture capital:** power-law returns — a few investments generate most of the fund’s returns.
- **Hedge fund strategies:** equity long/short, global macro, event-driven (merger arbitrage), relative-value/arbitrage, credit, quantitative/systematic, multi-strategy.

## Investment mandates

A mandate specifies objectives (return targets, income), constraints (risk limits, asset classes, ratings, concentration, liquidity, leverage, currency, ESG or Sharia screening), benchmark, reporting and fees. Example: “Global investment-grade bond portfolio; benchmark Bloomberg Global Aggregate (hedged to USD); max 10% BBB; no derivatives except currency forwards for hedging; tracking error ≤ 1.5%.”

:::example Private equity fees and carried interest (simplified, fictional)
Fund of $100m; management fee 2% per year for 5 years then 1.5% on invested capital; hurdle 8% preferred return; carry 20% with catch-up. Over 8 years, total fees ≈ $14m; investments ($86m) return $190m. Profit to distribute = 190 − 100 = $90m (after returning all contributed capital). With full catch-up, the GP receives 20% of the total profit = **$18m**; LPs receive **$72m** profit — net MOIC ≈ 1.72× and net IRR roughly in the low teens, depending on timing.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A fund has assets of $520m, liabilities $20m and 40m units. Compute NAV per unit.
2. Why can an ETF’s market price differ slightly from its NAV, and what keeps the gap small?
3. Explain the J-curve to a new investor in a PE fund.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (520 − 20) ÷ 40 = **$12.50**.
2. ETF shares trade continuously on the exchange; supply and demand move the price. Authorised participants can create or redeem ETF units in exchange for the underlying basket, arbitraging away large differences.
3. In the early years the fund charges fees and holds investments at cost or below while new companies are being improved, so reported returns are negative; as investments mature and are sold, distributions and valuations rise, and returns turn positive — tracing a “J” shape.
:::
