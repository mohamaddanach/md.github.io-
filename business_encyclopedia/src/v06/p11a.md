Physical commodity trading moves the raw materials of the world economy — oil, gas, grain, sugar, coffee, metals, ore, stone and timber — from producers to consumers across oceans and borders. It looks simple (“buy low, sell high”) but is one of the most operationally and financially demanding businesses: margins are thin, volumes are large, prices are volatile, logistics are complex, and a single failed cargo can wipe out a year’s profit.

This part explains how physical trading works in general, then covers energy, agricultural, metal and industrial commodities individually, and ends with three complete fictional deal P&Ls. **All prices, differentials, freight rates and margins in this part are illustrative assumptions for teaching, not market data.**

:::obj Learning objectives for Part XI
- Explain the roles of producers, consumers, brokers, traders and trading houses.
- Understand benchmarks, pricing formulas (flat price, benchmark ± differential, pricing periods), and quality and quantity determination.
- Explain spot, forward and futures transactions, basis risk, physical premiums, spreads and freight economics.
- Describe contracts, inspection, storage, financing, insurance and delivery obligations for each major commodity group.
- Compute the full profitability of a physical commodity deal.
- Distinguish physical trading from trading financial instruments.
:::

# How Physical Commodity Trading Works

:::obj In this chapter
The participants, sources of trading margin, pricing mechanisms, quality and quantity, logistics, contracts, hedging, financing, insurance and risks common to all physical commodities.
:::

## Participants

| Participant | Role | Typical concerns |
|---|---|---|
| Producers | Mines, oil companies, farms and cooperatives, quarries, sawmills | Price realisation, offtake security, financing |
| End users / consumers | Refiners, smelters, mills, food processors, utilities, fabricators, contractors | Supply security, quality, cost, credit terms |
| Traders and trading houses | Buy, transport, store, blend and sell; manage risk and logistics | Margin, risk, financing, counterparty reliability |
| Brokers (physical) | Introduce buyers and sellers for commission; do not take title | Commission; avoiding circumvention |
| Brokers (futures/FCMs) | Execute exchange trades, hold margin | Client margin, compliance |
| Ship brokers | Fix vessels for charterers | Commission on freight |
| Inspection companies | Quantity/quality determination at load/discharge | Independence, accuracy |
| Collateral managers / warehouse operators | Control and certify stored goods for financiers | Integrity, insurance |
| Banks and trade financiers | Finance transactions, issue L/Cs, borrowing bases | Collateral control, fraud, KYC |
| Exchanges and price reporting agencies | Futures markets; published price assessments | Liquidity, methodology |
| Governments and state buyers | Tenders, export controls, strategic reserves | Food and energy security |

## Where does a trader’s margin come from?

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| Location (geographical) arbitrage | Buy wheat FOB Black Sea, sell CFR North Africa when the price difference exceeds freight and costs |
| Time arbitrage | Buy and store when the forward curve is in contango |
| Quality arbitrage / blending | Blend crude or ore grades to meet buyer specifications at lower cost |
| Logistics optimisation | Better freight, vessel scheduling, combining cargoes |
| Credit and financing services | Selling on deferred terms to buyers who lack bank lines (priced into the premium) |
| Information and relationships | Knowing supply and demand flows earlier and better |
| Risk management | Managing price, basis and spread exposures intelligently |

## Pricing mechanisms

- **Flat (fixed) price:** a single agreed price per unit — creates full price exposure until hedged or sold.
- **Benchmark ± differential (premium/discount):** e.g., “average of Dated Brent quotations for the 5 days around B/L date + $1.10/bbl”, or “LME cash settlement average for the month of shipment + $95/t”. The differential reflects quality, location, timing and market tightness.
- **Pricing period (quotational period, QP):** the dates over which the benchmark is averaged, which may differ between purchase and sale — the gap is a price risk to hedge.
- **Formula pricing:** long-term contracts linking price to indices (e.g., LNG priced as a percentage of oil prices plus a constant).
- **Tenders:** state buyers publish requirements; sellers submit offers; lowest compliant offer usually wins.

## Spot, forward, futures, basis and spreads

| Concept | Meaning in physical trading |
|---|---|
| Spot / prompt | Delivery in the near term (days/weeks) |
| Forward (physical) | Contract for delivery at a future period at an agreed price or formula |
| Futures | Exchange contract used mostly for hedging and price discovery; few are physically delivered |
| Basis | Local physical price − futures price; reflects location, quality, timing and local supply/demand |
| Physical premium | Amount above the benchmark (e.g., regional aluminium premium) |
| Calendar spread | Price difference between delivery months (contango/backwardation) |
| Location spread | Price difference between regions (e.g., Brent vs Dubai) |
| Quality spread | Price difference between grades (e.g., high vs low protein wheat; sweet vs sour crude) |
| Crack spread / margin | Product prices minus crude cost (refining margin) |
| Freight economics | Location spreads must exceed freight + insurance + losses + financing for an arbitrage |

:::example Hedging a cargo and the basis
A trader buys 50,000 t of corn at a fixed $210/t FOB (equivalent to futures + $25 basis) and has not yet sold it. To remove flat-price risk, it sells futures equivalent to 50,000 t at $185. Two weeks later it sells the cargo at $201/t (futures have fallen to $180; basis has weakened to +$21).
- Physical: 201 − 210 = **−$9/t**.
- Futures hedge: 185 − 180 = **+$5/t**.
- Net: **−$4/t** = the change in basis (25 → 21). The hedge removed flat-price risk but not basis risk.
:::

## Quality and quantity

Contracts define **specifications** (e.g., protein, moisture and foreign matter for wheat; API gravity and sulphur for crude; Fe content and impurities for iron ore; purity for metals) and **adjustments** (price escalators and de-escalators for deviations, rejection limits). **Quantity** is determined by draft survey (ships), shore-tank or flow-meter measurements (oil), weighbridge (trucks), or count/area (slabs, logs), with an agreed **tolerance** (e.g., ±10% at seller’s or buyer’s option). Which certificate is **final** (loading or discharge, which inspector) is critical: in many commodity contracts quality and quantity are “final at loading”, putting the risk of losses in transit on the buyer (who insures it).

## Logistics, storage and inspection

Commodities move by bulk carriers, tankers, LNG carriers, containers, barges, rail and pipelines. Storage happens in tanks, silos, warehouses, stockyards and exchange-approved warehouses (for futures delivery). Independent inspectors certify quality and quantity; collateral managers control goods financed by banks.

## Contracts

Standard industry contract forms reduce negotiation and disputes: e.g., **GAFTA** forms for grains and feed, **FOSFA** for oils and fats, **ICA** rules for cotton, **LME** rules for exchange metal, oil companies’ **general terms and conditions** for crude and products, **BIMCO** charter-party forms (e.g., GENCON voyage charter) and tanker charter forms, and **ISDA** for OTC derivatives. Many forms include arbitration by the relevant trade association.

## Hedging, financing and insurance

- **Hedging:** futures, swaps and options remove flat-price exposure; residual risks are basis, spread, quality and timing.
- **Financing:** transactional trade finance (L/Cs, import/export loans), borrowing-base facilities (on inventory and receivables), pre-export finance and prepayment to producers, repo of commodity stocks, receivables discounting. Banks typically finance 80–90% of the value of hedged, insured, controlled goods.
- **Insurance:** marine cargo (often required by banks), stock throughput insurance (covers goods in transit and storage), charterer’s liability, political risk, credit insurance.

## Delivery obligations

The seller must deliver the contracted quantity and quality within the delivery period, at the agreed place, with the required documents. Failure can trigger default clauses: the non-defaulting party may buy in or sell out against the defaulter and claim the price difference (market-based damages) under trade-association rules — a powerful mechanism that makes commodity contracts quite different from ordinary supply agreements.

## The main risks

| Risk | Description | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Price (flat) | Price moves before purchase and sale are matched | Hedging, back-to-back pricing, limits |
| Basis and spread | Relationships between prices move | Monitoring, limits, diversified hedges |
| Counterparty / performance | Seller fails to deliver or buyer fails to pay or take delivery | KYC, credit limits, L/Cs, credit insurance, performance guarantees |
| Operational | Vessel delays, demurrage, contamination, quality failure, loss | Experienced operators, inspection, insurance |
| Fraud | Fake documents, non-existent goods, double financing | Due diligence, collateral managers, verification of B/Ls and warehouse receipts |
| Country and political | Export bans, sanctions, war, expropriation | Diversification, political risk insurance, compliance |
| Liquidity | Margin calls on hedges while physical cash comes later | Committed facilities, margin financing lines |
| Regulatory | Sanctions, market-abuse rules, ESG requirements | Compliance function |

## Typical margins and their limits

Gross margins in bulk trading are often a few dollars per tonne on grains or ores, cents per barrel to a few dollars on oil, and tens of dollars per tonne on metal premiums — a small percentage of the cargo value. Speciality products (e.g., natural stone, specialty coffees, niche chemicals) can offer higher percentage margins with lower volumes and higher operational risk. Published figures from public trading houses show that profits vary greatly with volatility and dislocations: margins tend to widen in disrupted markets and compress in calm ones. Any margin estimate must be stress-tested against freight, demurrage, quality claims, financing and counterparty risk.

## Physical trading vs financial trading

| | Physical trading | Financial trading (futures, swaps) |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Actual goods with quality, location, logistics | Standardised contracts |
| Profit sources | Location, time, quality arbitrage; logistics; credit services | Price movements, spreads, market-making |
| Operational complexity | High (vessels, inspection, storage, documents, customs) | Low |
| Capital | Working capital for goods, often bank-financed | Margin |
| Risks | Price, basis, performance, operational, fraud, country | Price, liquidity, counterparty (OTC) |
| Regulation | Trade, customs, sanctions, product rules; increasingly market regulation | Financial market regulation |
| Skills | Logistics, contracts, relationships, risk management | Market analysis, execution, risk management |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A trader buys 30,000 t at futures + $40 and sells at futures + $46 a week later, hedged throughout. Freight and costs are already included in both differentials. What is the gross result?
2. Why is it dangerous for a trader to buy on a fixed price and sell on a floating price without hedging?
3. List five documents a bank might require before financing a commodity cargo.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Basis gain $6/t × 30,000 = **$180,000** (flat-price moves offset by the hedge).
2. If the benchmark falls between purchase and sale pricing, the floating sale price falls while the purchase cost stays fixed — an open flat-price position (speculation).
3. Purchase and sale contracts; L/C or evidence of payment security from the buyer; bill of lading (to order of the bank or endorsed); inspection certificates (quality and quantity); insurance certificate assigned to the bank; hedge confirmations; KYC on counterparties; collateral manager/warehouse receipts if stored.
:::

# Energy: Crude Oil, Refined Products, Natural Gas, LNG and Coal

:::obj In this chapter
How energy commodities are produced, priced, shipped, traded and hedged.
:::

## Crude oil

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Producers | National oil companies (e.g., Saudi Aramco, ADNOC, QatarEnergy, KPC), international oil companies, independents |
| Buyers | Refiners; trading houses; state buyers |
| Quality | API gravity (light/heavy), sulphur (sweet/sour), acidity (TAN), metals; each grade has a typical assay |
| Units | Barrel (bbl) = 42 US gallons ≈ 159 litres; ~7.3 bbl per tonne for medium crude (varies with density) |
| Benchmarks | **Brent** complex (ICE Brent futures; Dated Brent physical assessment by price reporting agencies, with WTI Midland included in the basket since 2023), **WTI** (NYMEX futures, delivered at Cushing), **Dubai/Oman** for Middle East crude to Asia, **Murban** (ICE Futures Abu Dhabi) |
| Pricing | Term contracts with official selling prices (OSPs) set monthly by producers as differentials to benchmarks; spot cargoes priced at benchmark averages ± differentials |
| Transport | Pipelines; tankers (VLCC ~2m bbl, Suezmax ~1m bbl, Aframax ~0.6–0.75m bbl) |
| Contracts | Producers’ GT&Cs; FOB or CIF/DES deliveries; laytime/demurrage; outturn vs B/L quantity |
| Hedging | Brent/WTI/Dubai futures and swaps; spread hedges |
| Key risks | Price, freight, demurrage, quality claims, sanctions (many sanctions regimes target oil trade), counterparty |

## Refined products

Refineries convert crude into products: LPG, naphtha, gasoline, jet/kerosene, diesel/gasoil, fuel oil (high-sulphur and very-low-sulphur marine fuels since the IMO 2020 sulphur cap), bitumen. Prices are assessed by price reporting agencies in regional hubs (Arab Gulf, Singapore, North-West Europe, US Gulf Coast). Futures include ICE Low Sulphur Gasoil and NYMEX RBOB gasoline and ULSD. The **crack spread** (product price − crude price) measures refining margin; refiners hedge crack spreads.

:::example Simple crack spread
Crude $80/bbl; gasoline $95/bbl; diesel $102/bbl. A simplified 3-2-1 crack (3 bbl crude → 2 gasoline + 1 diesel) = (2 × 95 + 1 × 102 − 3 × 80) ÷ 3 = (190 + 102 − 240) ÷ 3 = **$17.33/bbl** gross refining margin (before operating costs).
:::

## Natural gas and LNG

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Units | MMBtu (US, Asia LNG), therm, MWh (Europe); 1 MWh ≈ 3.412 MMBtu |
| Hubs and benchmarks | Henry Hub (US), TTF (Netherlands, Europe’s main hub), NBP (UK), JKM (LNG to Japan/Korea, assessed by a price reporting agency) |
| Pipeline gas | Long-term contracts, often take-or-pay, increasingly hub-indexed in Europe |
| LNG chain | Liquefaction (–162°C), LNG carriers (~170,000+ m³), regasification terminals (including floating FSRUs) |
| LNG pricing | Long-term SPAs often oil-indexed (e.g., slope × Brent + constant) or Henry Hub-linked (Henry Hub × 115% + liquefaction fee, a common US export structure); spot cargoes at JKM or TTF-related prices |
| Delivery terms | FOB (buyer ships) or DES (delivered ex ship) |
| Major exporters | Recent years: United States, Qatar, Australia among the largest |
| Risks | Price, volume (take-or-pay), shipping availability, boil-off, regasification capacity, geopolitical |

:::example Oil-indexed LNG price
Contract: LNG price ($/MMBtu) = 0.13 × Brent ($/bbl) + 0.50. With Brent at $80: 0.13 × 80 + 0.5 = **$10.90/MMBtu**. A cargo of 3.4 million MMBtu is worth about **$37m**.
:::

## Coal

Thermal (steam) coal for power generation; metallurgical (coking) coal for steelmaking. Quality: calorific value (kcal/kg, on NAR or GAR basis), ash, moisture, sulphur, volatile matter. Benchmarks include API2 (CIF Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp), Newcastle (Australia FOB) and Richards Bay (South Africa FOB). Shipped in Panamax and Capesize bulk carriers. Energy-transition policies, financing restrictions by banks and insurers, and carbon pricing increasingly affect the coal trade.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A 1 million bbl cargo is priced at the 5-day average of Dated Brent around the B/L date + $0.85. The average is $78.40. What is the invoice value?
2. A refiner wants to lock in its margin for next quarter. Which hedge would it use?
3. Convert 50 MWh to MMBtu.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (78.40 + 0.85) × 1,000,000 = **$79.25m** (adjusted for final B/L quantity).
2. Sell crack-spread swaps/futures (sell product futures and buy crude futures in the right ratios), locking the margin between crude cost and product prices.
3. 50 × 3.412 = **170.6 MMBtu**.
:::
