# Agricultural Commodities: Wheat, Corn, Sugar and Coffee

:::obj In this chapter
How grains and soft commodities are produced, specified, priced, shipped, financed and hedged — and why food commodities carry special political and seasonal risks.
:::

## Wheat

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Producers/exporters | Russia, EU, Canada, Australia, US, Ukraine, Argentina, Kazakhstan |
| Major importers | Egypt, Indonesia, Turkey, Algeria, Philippines, many Middle East and North African countries |
| Buyers | State agencies (tenders), flour millers, feed compounders, traders |
| Quality | Protein %, moisture %, test weight (kg/hl), falling number, gluten, foreign matter, damaged kernels; classes (e.g., hard red winter, soft red winter, milling vs feed) |
| Units | Tonnes; futures in bushels (wheat bushel = 60 lb ≈ 27.22 kg) in US markets |
| Benchmarks | CBOT SRW wheat and KC HRW wheat (CME Group), Euronext milling wheat (Paris), assessed FOB Black Sea prices |
| Contracts | GAFTA standard forms; FOB or CIF/CFR; tolerance ±5–10%; quality and weight final at loading or discharge as agreed |
| Logistics | Panamax/Supramax/Handysize bulk carriers; port silos; discharge rates; fumigation |
| Risks | Weather, export restrictions (quotas, taxes, bans), war risk in the Black Sea region, sanctions screening, tender rules, quality claims, demurrage |
| Financing | Pre-export finance for origination; L/Cs from state buyers; inventory financing in silos |

## Corn (maize)

Main exporters: United States, Brazil, Argentina, Ukraine. Uses: animal feed, starch, sweeteners, ethanol. Benchmark: CBOT corn futures (corn bushel = 56 lb ≈ 25.40 kg). Quality: moisture, broken kernels, aflatoxin limits (critical for feed and food safety), GMO status (affects market access). Seasonality: northern and southern hemisphere harvests offset each other, creating seasonal export windows.

## Sugar

Raw sugar (for refining) and white (refined) sugar. Leading exporters include Brazil, Thailand and India (subject to export policies). Benchmarks: **ICE Sugar No. 11** (raw sugar, cents per pound) and **ICE London No. 5** (white sugar, $/tonne); the **white premium** (white minus raw, converted to $/t) indicates refining margins. Quality: polarisation (sucrose content) for raw sugar; ICUMSA colour rating for white sugar (e.g., ICUMSA 45 for high-quality refined sugar). Shipped in bulk (raw) or bags/containers (white).

:::warn Advance-fee fraud in sugar and other commodities
Unsolicited offers of large quantities of “ICUMSA 45 sugar” or other commodities at below-market prices, requiring the buyer to issue an SBLC or pay “procedure fees” before seeing goods, are a well-known fraud pattern. Legitimate trades start with verified counterparties, realistic prices and standard documentation.
:::

## Coffee

Arabica (higher quality, grown at altitude; major producers Brazil, Colombia, Ethiopia, Central America) and robusta (hardier, higher caffeine; major producers Vietnam, Brazil, Indonesia, Uganda). Benchmarks: **ICE Coffee “C”** (arabica, cents/lb) and **ICE Robusta** (London, $/t). Physical coffee trades at **differentials** to futures that depend on origin, grade, screen size, defects and cup quality; specialty coffee trades at large premiums based on cupping scores. Shipped green in containers (jute bags or bulk liners). Risks: weather and frost, quality deterioration, certification requirements, sustainability and deforestation regulations, price volatility.

# Metals: Aluminium, Copper, Gold and Iron Ore

:::obj In this chapter
Base metals, precious metals and bulk ores: production chains, exchange benchmarks, physical premiums, contracts, financing and risks.
:::

## Base metals and the LME

The **London Metal Exchange (LME)** provides benchmark prices and futures for aluminium, copper, zinc, nickel, lead and other metals; trading also occurs on COMEX (copper) and the Shanghai Futures Exchange. LME contracts are deliverable through LME-approved warehouses via warrants. Physical metal trades at **LME price + physical premium**, where the premium reflects location (e.g., delivered to a region, duty-paid or unpaid), shape and brand, logistics and local supply-demand.

## Aluminium

Produced from bauxite → alumina → smelting (very energy-intensive; Gulf countries host large smelters thanks to energy availability). Products: primary ingots (P1020 standard), billets, slabs, wire rod. Pricing: LME aluminium + regional premium (e.g., Rotterdam duty-paid/unpaid, US Midwest, Japan quarterly premium). Value-added products (billets) command additional premiums. Risks: energy costs, trade measures (tariffs, anti-dumping), premium volatility, warehouse logistics.

## Copper

Mined ore → concentrate (traded with **treatment and refining charges, TC/RCs**, paid by miners to smelters) → refined cathode (Grade A, LME-deliverable) → wire rod, tubes, sheets. Major producers: Chile, Peru, DRC, China (refining). Demand linked to construction, power grids, electric vehicles and electronics. Pricing: LME/COMEX + premium; concentrates priced on payable metal content minus TC/RCs and penalties for impurities.

## Gold

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Units | Troy ounce = 31.1035 g; kilo bars and 400-oz London Good Delivery bars |
| Benchmarks | LBMA Gold Price (set twice daily by electronic auction); COMEX gold futures |
| Hubs | London (OTC), New York (futures), Zurich (refining), Dubai, Shanghai, Mumbai, Hong Kong |
| Participants | Miners, refiners, central banks, jewellery makers, investors (ETFs, bars and coins), bullion banks |
| Quality | Fineness (e.g., 995, 999.9); accredited refiners (LBMA Good Delivery list) |
| Risks | Price, fraud (counterfeit bars, false assay), money laundering, conflict-mineral sourcing — responsible sourcing rules (e.g., LBMA Responsible Gold Guidance, OECD due-diligence guidance) and AML obligations for dealers |

## Iron ore

Seaborne iron ore is dominated by Australia and Brazil; China is the largest importer. Products: fines, lump, pellets, concentrates. Benchmark: **62% Fe fines CFR China** indices published by price reporting agencies, with futures on SGX and the Dalian Commodity Exchange. Prices adjust for Fe content and impurities (silica, alumina, phosphorus). Shipped in Capesize and Valemax-type vessels, so freight economics (e.g., Brazil–China vs Australia–China routes) are central.

:::example Value-in-use adjustment (iron ore, illustrative)
Index 62% Fe at $105/dmt (dry metric tonne). A cargo of 60% Fe with Fe adjustment $2.50 per 1% Fe and an alumina penalty of $3/dmt: price ≈ 105 − 2 × 2.50 − 3 = **$97/dmt**. For 170,000 wmt at 8% moisture → 156,400 dmt → invoice ≈ **$15.17m**.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A copper cathode sale: LME cash average $9,300/t + premium $150/t for 2,000 t. Invoice value?
2. Convert 1 kg of gold to troy ounces and value it at $2,300/oz.
3. Why do aluminium buyers care about regional premiums as much as the LME price?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (9,300 + 150) × 2,000 = **$18.9m**.
2. 1,000 ÷ 31.1035 = **32.15 oz** → ≈ **$73,950**.
3. The premium can be a large share of the delivered price and moves independently of the LME price (driven by logistics, tariffs and regional supply); an LME hedge does not hedge the premium.
:::

# Industrial Materials: Marble, Granite and Timber

:::obj In this chapter
Commodities without exchange benchmarks: natural stone and timber — specifications, pricing, logistics, inspection, regulation and risks.
:::

## Marble and granite

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Producers | Quarries and processors in Italy, Turkey, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Egypt, India, China, Brazil, Iran and others |
| Products | Blocks (raw), slabs (cut, often 2 cm or 3 cm, polished/honed), tiles, finished products |
| Buyers | Fabricators, contractors, developers (hotels, towers), distributors, retailers |
| Specification | Stone name and origin, colour and vein pattern, grade (commercial/standard/premium/“first choice”), finish, thickness tolerance, dimensions, defects (cracks, holes, resin filling), water absorption, density, flexural strength (technical tests) |
| Units and pricing | Blocks per m³ or per tonne; slabs and tiles per m²; prices negotiated — **no exchange benchmark**; strong variation by aesthetics |
| Logistics | Blocks as breakbulk or in open-top/flat-rack containers; slabs in bundles on A-frames inside containers (weight limits ~20–25 t per 20ft); tiles in crates |
| Inspection | Buyer’s visual selection at the quarry or warehouse (“block selection”, photographs of each slab — “book-matched” sequences); third-party inspection for large projects |
| Contracts | Purchase orders with photo-approved selections, tolerance, packing, breakage allowance, payment (often advance + balance against documents) |
| Risks | Colour/vein variation vs approved samples, breakage in transit, project delays, buyer rejection, mis-description, weight limits on roads, project-specific quantity mismatch |

:::tip Why stone trading can have higher margins
Each block and slab is unique; value comes from selection, consistency across a project, and reliable supply. Traders who understand stone, maintain quarry relationships and manage quality claims can earn margins far above bulk commodities — but working capital (stock of varied colours), breakage and claims require careful control.
:::

## Timber

| Topic | Key points |
|---|---|
| Types | Softwood (pine, spruce — construction, packaging), hardwood (oak, beech, tropical species — furniture, flooring), panels (plywood, MDF, OSB), logs |
| Producers | Russia (subject to sanctions by several jurisdictions), Canada, US, Scandinavia, Germany, Brazil, Malaysia, New Zealand, African countries |
| Units | m³ (cubic metres), board feet (US), tonnes for logs in some trades |
| Grading | Grading rules by species and region (knots, defects, moisture content, kiln-dried vs air-dried) |
| Pricing | Negotiated by grade and specification; CME lumber futures exist for North American softwood lumber |
| Regulation | Legality and sustainability: EU Timber Regulation and the EU Deforestation Regulation (application dates have been postponed; verify current status), US Lacey Act, certifications (FSC, PEFC); phytosanitary rules and ISPM 15 for wood packaging |
| Logistics | Containers (sawn timber), breakbulk (logs), moisture control, fumigation |
| Risks | Species misdeclaration, illegal logging, sanctions on origin, moisture damage, price swings driven by construction cycles |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A contractor approves marble from photographs of three slab bundles; the delivered slabs show darker veins. What contract terms should have prevented the dispute?
2. Why is timber origin due diligence important for a Gulf importer that re-exports furniture to Europe?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Reference to specific approved bundles/slab numbers with photographs forming part of the contract; definitions of acceptable variation; pre-shipment inspection by the buyer or its agent with authority to reject; a dispute mechanism (independent expert); breakage and rejection allowances; payment balance only after inspection or against inspection certificate.
2. EU rules on timber legality and deforestation require due diligence on the wood’s origin throughout the supply chain; products from illegal or sanctioned origins can be blocked, fined or seized; the importer needs traceability documents and certifications.
:::

# Commodity Deal Economics: Three Complete Trade P&Ls

:::obj In this chapter
Three fictional deals — a bulk wheat cargo, a copper cathode back-to-back trade, and a container trade of marble slabs — with full calculations of purchase price, transport, insurance, financing and other costs, selling price, gross and net margins, and the risks behind the numbers. **All figures are training assumptions.**
:::

## Deal 1 — Wheat cargo: Black Sea to North Africa

**Structure:** A trading company (fictional “Meridian Grains”) buys 60,000 t (±5% at seller’s option) of 12.5% protein milling wheat **FOB** a Black Sea port at **$230/t**, and sells to a state buyer in North Africa **CFR** at **$262/t** (won in a tender). Pricing fixed back-to-back on the same day (no flat-price exposure). Payment from the buyer: L/C at sight, confirmed by an international bank at the trader’s cost. Payment to the seller: trader’s L/C at sight, financed by its bank at 90% for ~35 days until the buyer’s L/C pays. Quality and weight final at loading per independent inspection. Under CFR the buyer insures the cargo; the trader buys contingency (seller’s interest) cover for its own exposure until it is paid (included below).

| Item | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales revenue** | 60,000 × 262 | **15,720,000** |
| Purchase cost | 60,000 × 230 | (13,800,000) |
| Ocean freight (Supramax voyage charter) | 60,000 × $18.00 | (1,080,000) |
| **Gross trading margin** | ($14.00/t) | **840,000** |
| Chartering broker commission | 1.25% of freight | (13,500) |
| Contingency cargo insurance | 0.08% × 110% × 15.72m | (13,834) |
| Inspection and superintendence (load port) | 60,000 × $0.30 | (18,000) |
| Purchase L/C issuance | 0.15% × 13.8m | (20,700) |
| Confirmation of buyer’s L/C | 0.40% × 15.72m | (62,880) |
| Financing | 90% × 13.8m × 7.5% × 35/360 | (90,563) |
| Demurrage (net of despatch) | estimated $0.50/t | (30,000) |
| Quality/weight claim reserve | 0.2% of sales | (31,440) |
| **Net trading profit (before overheads and tax)** | **$9.32/t; 3.6% of sales** | **559,083** |

**Sensitivities and failure scenarios:**

| Event | Impact |
|---|---|
| Freight rises $3/t before vessel fixed (fixed after the sale) | −$180,000 (hedge with freight forward agreements or fix vessel early) |
| Discharge delayed 6 days beyond laytime at $22,000/day demurrage, not recoverable from buyer | −$132,000 |
| Documentary discrepancy causing 20-day payment delay | Extra financing ≈ −$52,000 |
| Buyer refuses cargo (with confirmed L/C, compliant documents are paid by the confirming bank) | Protected if documents comply |
| Seller defaults (does not load) after prices rose $15/t | Trader must buy replacement cargo ≈ −$900,000, then claim against seller under GAFTA default clause — recovery uncertain |
| Export restriction imposed by the origin country | Force majeure/prohibition clauses determine outcome; potential large loss |

**Lesson:** a $14/t gross margin becomes $9/t after costs, and several plausible events can erase it. Disciplined chartering, documentation, counterparty selection and contract terms are what make the business profitable.

## Deal 2 — Copper cathodes: back-to-back with a pricing-period mismatch

**Structure:** Meridian Metals (fictional) buys 500 t of LME Grade A copper cathodes **CIF Gemlik (Turkey)** from a South American producer at **LME cash average of month M + $110/t**, paying by sight L/C. It sells to a Turkish wire-rod maker **delivered to the buyer’s plant** at **LME cash average of month M+1 + $285/t**, payment 30 days after delivery (credit-insured). The mismatch of pricing periods creates price exposure, hedged on the LME: the trader **sells** futures as the purchase price is fixed during month M and **buys them back** as the sale price is fixed during month M+1.

Outcome: LME average month M = $9,400; month M+1 = $9,550.

| Item | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| Sales revenue | 500 × (9,550 + 285) | 4,917,500 |
| Purchase cost | 500 × (9,400 + 110) | (4,755,000) |
| **Physical result** | | **162,500** |
| LME hedge result | Sold at avg 9,400, bought back at avg 9,550: −150 × 500 | (75,000) |
| **Hedged gross margin (= premium spread 285 − 110 = $175/t)** | | **87,500** |
| Port handling, customs formalities, trucking to plant | 500 × $18 | (9,000) |
| Inland transit and stock insurance | 0.03% of sales | (1,475) |
| L/C costs (purchase) | 0.15% | (7,133) |
| Financing (purchase price outstanding ~45 days until buyer pays) | 4.755m × 6.5% × 45/360 | (38,634) |
| Credit insurance on buyer | 0.25% of sales | (12,294) |
| LME execution and clearing fees | 20 lots each way | (400) |
| **Net profit** | **$37/t; 0.38% of sales** | **18,564** |

**Lesson:** without the hedge the trader would have made $93,564 — or, if copper had fallen $150, lost $56,436. The hedge converts a speculative result into a predictable premium margin. Most of the gross margin pays for **credit and financing** provided to the buyer; the net margin is very thin, so volume, financing costs and operational precision drive profitability.

## Deal 3 — Marble slabs from Turkey to Kuwait

**Structure:** Levant Stone Trading (fictional) buys 3,600 m² of 2 cm polished white marble slabs (photo-approved bundles) from a Turkish processor at **$30/m² FOB Izmir**: 30% advance, 70% before loading. It sells to a Kuwaiti contractor for a hotel project at **$46/m² CFR Shuwaikh**: 30% advance, 70% by documentary collection (D/P). Shipment: 8 × 20ft containers (~450 m² each).

| Item | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales revenue** | 3,600 × 46 | **165,600** |
| Purchase cost | 3,600 × 30 | (108,000) |
| Ocean freight Izmir → Kuwait | 8 × $1,900 | (15,200) |
| **Gross margin** | (25.6%) | **42,400** |
| Cargo insurance incl. breakage clause | 0.3% × 110% × 123,200 | (407) |
| Quality agent at processor (selection, loading supervision) | | (1,500) |
| Breakage allowance (credit note to buyer) | 3% of sales | (4,968) |
| Colour-variation claim reserve | 2% of sales | (3,312) |
| Bank collection charges | 0.2% of sales | (331) |
| Financing (advances and inventory before buyer pays) | ~$75,600 for ~60 days at 8% (approx.) | (1,600) |
| **Net profit** | **18.3% of sales** | **30,282** |

**Risks:** buyer refuses documents under D/P (goods stranded in Kuwait with demurrage); colour mismatch claims larger than reserved; project delays leading the contractor to postpone delivery; breakage above allowance; processor substitutes lower-grade slabs. Controls: photo-numbered slab lists in both contracts, loading supervision, higher advance from the buyer or L/C for new customers, insurance with breakage cover, and clear claim procedures.

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Extra confirmation: 0.35% × 15.72m = $55,020; extra demurrage: $1.50 × 60,000 = $90,000 → net ≈ 559,083 − 55,020 − 90,000 = **$414,063** ($6.90/t).
2. Physical result: 500 × (9,250 + 285) − 4,755,000 = 4,767,500 − 4,755,000 = 12,500; less costs ex-hedge (68,936) → **−$56,436** (a loss) without the hedge; with the hedge the result would still be **+$18,564** (hedge gain +75,000).
3. Credit assessment of the contractor; credit insurance or a bank guarantee/SBLC; higher advance; price increase for the 90-day credit (≈ 2% at 8% p.a.); retention of title clause (subject to Kuwaiti law); and monitoring of project payment status.
:::

:::summary Part XI summary
- Physical traders earn from location, time, quality, logistics and credit services — not from guessing prices.
- Contracts use benchmarks ± differentials; hedging removes flat-price risk but leaves basis and premium risk.
- Quality, quantity, inspection and delivery terms are central to value and disputes.
- Bulk margins are thin and erased by small operational failures; speciality trades earn more but carry quality risk.
- Physical trading is a logistics, credit and risk-management business with heavy financing needs.
:::
