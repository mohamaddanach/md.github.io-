# Portfolio Management: Risk, Return, Diversification and Asset Allocation

:::obj In this chapter
Expected return and risk, diversification and correlation, asset allocation, risk-adjusted performance measures, and practical risk measures such as drawdown and value at risk.
:::

## Risk and return

Investors demand higher expected returns for bearing more risk. Risk can be measured as volatility (standard deviation of returns), downside deviation, maximum drawdown, probability of loss, or — for long-term investors — the risk of failing to meet objectives.

:::formula Two-asset portfolio
E(Rp) = w₁E(R₁) + w₂E(R₂)
σp² = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂ρ₁₂σ₁σ₂
Diversification benefit is larger when correlation ρ is low.
:::

:::example A 60/40 portfolio (illustrative assumptions)
Equities: expected return 9%, volatility 18%. Bonds: 4.5%, volatility 6%. Correlation 0.2. Risk-free rate 4%.
- E(Rp) = 0.6 × 9 + 0.4 × 4.5 = **7.2%**.
- σp² = 0.36 × 0.0324 + 0.16 × 0.0036 + 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.2 × 0.18 × 0.06 = 0.013277 → σp = **11.5%** (less than the weighted average volatility of 13.2%).
- Sharpe ratio = (7.2 − 4.0) ÷ 11.5 = **0.28**.
:::

## Systematic and idiosyncratic risk

**Idiosyncratic** (company-specific) risk can be diversified away by holding many securities; **systematic** (market) risk cannot. CAPM states that only systematic risk (beta) is rewarded with higher expected returns. Practitioners also recognise other systematic factors (size, value, momentum, quality, low volatility) used in factor investing.

## Asset allocation

Strategic asset allocation — the long-term mix of asset classes — explains most of the variability of a diversified portfolio’s returns over time. It depends on the investor’s:

| Factor | Example effect |
|---|---|
| Objectives | Growth vs income vs capital preservation |
| Time horizon | Longer horizon → more equities, illiquid assets |
| Risk tolerance (ability and willingness) | Lower tolerance → more bonds and cash |
| Liquidity needs | Pension payouts, business capital needs |
| Constraints | Regulation, Sharia compliance, ESG policy, currency of liabilities |
| Tax | Location and choice of vehicles |

Tactical asset allocation makes short-term deviations; **rebalancing** restores target weights, systematically selling assets that have risen and buying those that have fallen.

:::example Investment policy for a family business’s surplus cash (fictional)
Surplus $20m not needed for 5+ years, plus $5m liquidity reserve. Policy: liquidity reserve in deposits and T-bills (≤ 12 months, A-rated banks, ≤ 25% per bank). Long-term pool: 50% global equities (index funds), 30% investment-grade bonds/sukuk (duration 3–6 years), 10% real estate (listed REITs), 10% private markets via diversified funds; rebalance when weights deviate by more than 5 points; no single security above 5%; no leverage.
:::

## Risk-adjusted performance measures

| Measure | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Sharpe ratio | (Rp − Rf) ÷ σp | Excess return per unit of total risk |
| Sortino ratio | (Rp − Rf) ÷ downside deviation | Penalises only downside volatility |
| Treynor ratio | (Rp − Rf) ÷ βp | Excess return per unit of systematic risk |
| Jensen’s alpha | Rp − [Rf + βp(Rm − Rf)] | Return above CAPM expectation |
| Information ratio | (Rp − Rb) ÷ tracking error | Active return per unit of active risk |
| Maximum drawdown | Largest peak-to-trough fall | Pain experienced by investors |

## Value at risk (VaR)

VaR estimates the maximum loss over a horizon at a confidence level under normal conditions. Parametric example: portfolio $10m, annual volatility 12% → daily σ ≈ 12% ÷ √252 = 0.756%; 95% one-day VaR ≈ 1.65 × 0.756% × $10m ≈ **$125,000**. VaR says nothing about losses beyond the threshold; **stress tests** and **expected shortfall** complement it.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Two assets: A (return 10%, σ 20%), B (return 5%, σ 8%), correlation −0.1. Compute expected return and volatility of a 50/50 portfolio.
2. Portfolio return 11%, benchmark 9%, tracking error 4%. Information ratio?
3. Portfolio return 12%, beta 1.2, risk-free 4%, market return 10%. Compute Jensen’s alpha.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. E = **7.5%**; σ² = 0.25 × 0.04 + 0.25 × 0.0064 + 2 × 0.25 × (−0.1) × 0.2 × 0.08 = 0.01 + 0.0016 − 0.0008 = 0.0108 → σ = **10.4%**.
2. (11 − 9) ÷ 4 = **0.5**.
3. Expected = 4 + 1.2 × (10 − 4) = 11.2% → alpha = **+0.8%**.
:::

# Derivatives: Forwards, Futures, Options and Swaps

:::obj In this chapter
How the main derivatives work, their payoffs, margining and valuation intuition; and the differences between investing, trading, speculation, hedging and arbitrage, with numerical examples.
:::

## The four building blocks

| Instrument | Definition | Traded | Obligation | Upfront cost | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forward | Agreement to buy/sell at a future date at a fixed price | OTC | Both parties | None (credit lines/collateral) | Counterparty |
| Future | Standardised forward traded on an exchange, cleared, marked to market daily | Exchange | Both | Initial margin | Margin calls (liquidity) |
| Option | Right (not obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put) at a strike price | Exchange or OTC | Seller (writer) only | Premium paid by buyer | Buyer: premium; seller: potentially large losses |
| Swap | Exchange of cash-flow streams (fixed vs floating rates, currencies, commodity prices) | OTC (many cleared) | Both | Usually none | Counterparty, market value changes |

## Futures margining

:::example Crude oil futures (illustrative)
A refiner buys 10 futures contracts (1,000 barrels each) at $70.00 to hedge future purchases. Initial margin $6,000 per contract = $60,000.
- Day 1 settlement $68.50 → loss 1.50 × 10,000 = **$15,000** variation margin paid.
- Day 2 settlement $71.20 → gain 2.70 × 10,000 = **$27,000** received.
Daily mark-to-market eliminates most counterparty risk but creates **liquidity risk**: a hedger may need cash for margin calls before the physical benefit arrives.
:::

## Option payoffs

:::formula Option payoffs at expiry
Long call: max(S − K, 0) − premium    Long put: max(K − S, 0) − premium
Short call: premium − max(S − K, 0)    Short put: premium − max(K − S, 0)
Break-even (long call) = K + premium; (long put) = K − premium
:::

:::example Call option on a share
Strike $100, premium $5. At expiry: S = 90 → payoff −5 (premium lost); S = 105 → 0 (break-even); S = 120 → +15. The buyer’s loss is limited to $5; the seller’s potential loss is unlimited for an uncovered call.
:::

Option value depends on the underlying price, strike, time to expiry, volatility, interest rates and dividends (Black–Scholes and binomial models). Volatility is the key input — options are, in effect, priced volatility.

## Swaps

- **Interest-rate swap:** pay fixed, receive floating (or vice versa) on a notional amount. Used by borrowers to fix rates (see Part XVIII) and by banks to manage interest-rate risk.
- **Cross-currency swap:** exchange principal and interest in two currencies.
- **Commodity swap:** pay fixed price, receive floating index price (or vice versa) for a quantity — used by airlines (jet fuel), producers, consumers.
- **Credit default swap (CDS):** protection buyer pays a premium; seller pays if the reference entity defaults.

ISDA documentation (master agreement, schedule, credit support annex) governs most OTC derivatives.

## Investing, trading, speculation, hedging and arbitrage

| Activity | Purpose | Time horizon | Source of return / benefit | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Investing | Own productive assets for long-term returns | Years | Cash flows (dividends, interest) and growth | Buy a diversified equity index fund |
| Trading | Profit from short-term price movements and liquidity | Seconds to months | Price changes, spreads | Market-making in bonds |
| Speculation | Take risk on a view of future prices | Varies | Correct forecasts | Buying oil futures expecting a price rise without any oil exposure |
| Hedging | Reduce an existing risk | Matches exposure | Stability of cash flows and margins | Airline buys jet-fuel swaps |
| Arbitrage | Exploit price differences for the same or equivalent asset with little or no risk | Very short | Convergence of prices | Buying a commodity spot and selling futures when the spread exceeds carry costs |

:::example Same instrument, three purposes
Wheat futures at $6.00/bushel.
- **Hedger:** a flour mill that must buy wheat in 3 months buys futures; if wheat rises to $7.00, the futures gain offsets the higher physical cost.
- **Speculator:** a fund with no wheat exposure buys futures expecting a drought; it gains if prices rise, loses if they fall.
- **Arbitrageur:** if spot wheat is $5.40 and 3-month futures $6.00 while storage, insurance and financing cost $0.35, it buys spot, stores, and sells futures, locking in about $0.25 per bushel (less transaction costs and basis risks).
:::

## Market liquidity and volatility

Liquidity determines how easily positions are opened or closed without moving prices; it can vanish in stress. Volatility drives option prices, margin requirements and VaR. Volatility indices (e.g., VIX for the S&P 500) measure implied volatility expectations.

:::warn Derivatives risks for companies
- Leverage: small price moves create large gains/losses relative to cash invested.
- Liquidity: margin calls and collateral requirements.
- Basis risk: hedges do not move exactly with the exposure.
- Counterparty risk in OTC contracts.
- Complexity: structured products can contain sold options with hidden downside.
- Accounting volatility without hedge accounting.
- Governance: unauthorised trading, poor controls.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A put option with strike $50 costs $3. Compute payoff at expiry prices $40, $47, $55 and the break-even.
2. An airline will buy 1 million gallons of jet fuel in 6 months. Which derivative positions could hedge this, and what is the trade-off between a swap and a call option?
3. Classify: (a) a pension fund buying government bonds to match pension liabilities; (b) a trader buying gold in London and simultaneously selling it at a higher price in Dubai; (c) a CFO buying USD forward although the company has no USD payments, because she expects the local currency to weaken.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. S = 40: 10 − 3 = **+7**; S = 47: 3 − 3 = **0**; S = 55: 0 − 3 = **−3**. Break-even **$47**.
2. Pay-fixed jet-fuel swap (or long futures in a related contract such as heating oil/gasoil, with basis risk) or buy call options/caps. Swap: no premium, locks price, but no benefit if prices fall. Call: premium cost, protects against rises while keeping the benefit of falls.
3. (a) Investing/hedging liabilities (asset-liability matching); (b) arbitrage (if simultaneous, with costs and logistics accounted for); (c) speculation — not permitted under a typical treasury policy.
:::

# Investment Research, Due Diligence and a Fictional Investment Case

:::obj In this chapter
How professional investors research opportunities, conduct due diligence, and write an investment memorandum — illustrated with a complete fictional case.
:::

## The research process

1. **Idea generation:** screens (valuation, quality, momentum), industry themes, events (spin-offs, IPOs), contrarian situations.
2. **Business understanding:** annual reports, industry studies, customer and supplier conversations, competitor analysis, management meetings.
3. **Financial analysis:** historical normalisation, drivers, quality of earnings, balance sheet strength.
4. **Valuation:** multiple methods; reverse-engineering market expectations.
5. **Thesis and variant perception:** why the market is wrong, and what evidence will prove it.
6. **Risk assessment:** downside scenarios, what would make the thesis wrong (pre-mortem).
7. **Decision and sizing:** expected return vs risk; position size limited by conviction and liquidity.
8. **Monitoring:** milestones, thesis review, exit discipline.

## Due diligence for investments

| Area | Questions |
|---|---|
| Business and market | Is demand durable? What is the competitive position and moat? |
| Management and governance | Track record, incentives, related-party transactions, board independence |
| Financial | Accuracy of reported figures, cash conversion, off-balance-sheet items, accounting policies |
| Legal and regulatory | Licences, litigation, compliance history |
| ESG | Environmental liabilities, labour practices, governance quality |
| For funds (manager due diligence) | Strategy consistency, team stability, track record attribution, risk management, operations, fees and terms, alignment of interests |

## Fictional investment case: Gulf Cold Chain Holdings (listed)

:::case Investment memorandum (training simulation)
**Company:** Gulf Cold Chain Holdings (fictional), listed; operates temperature-controlled warehouses and refrigerated trucking in three Gulf countries.
**Share price:** $8.00 · **Shares:** 250m · **Market cap:** $2.0bn · **Net debt:** $500m (including leases) · **EV:** $2.5bn.

| $m | FY-2 | FY-1 | FY0 | FY+1E | FY+2E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 380 | 425 | 470 | 530 | 595 |
| EBITDA | 110 | 125 | 140 | 162 | 185 |
| EBITDA margin | 28.9% | 29.4% | 29.8% | 30.6% | 31.1% |
| Capex | 120 | 150 | 160 | 140 | 120 |
| Free cash flow | (40) | (55) | (45) | 0 | 35 |
| EPS ($) | 0.20 | 0.23 | 0.26 | 0.30 | 0.36 |

**Valuation:** EV/EBITDA FY+1E = 2,500 ÷ 162 = **15.4×**; peers (global cold-chain operators) 12–16×; P/E FY+1E = 26.7×.

**Thesis:** (1) structural growth in chilled and frozen food and pharma distribution, driven by population growth, food-security policies and e-grocery; (2) high switching costs and limited licensed cold-storage capacity in prime locations; (3) capex peak passing — FCF turning positive in FY+2, enabling dividends and deleveraging.

**What the price implies:** reverse DCF at WACC 9% suggests the market assumes ~10% revenue growth for 10 years and stable margins — in line with management guidance, so the upside depends on margin expansion and capital discipline.

**Risks:** new capacity from competitors and government-backed logistics zones; customer concentration (top 5 = 35% of revenue); energy costs; execution of capex projects; leverage at 3.6× net debt/EBITDA.

**Scenarios (12-month target):**

| Scenario | Probability | FY+1 EBITDA | Multiple | Implied price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bull | 25% | 170 | 16× | $8.88 |
| Base | 50% | 162 | 14.5× | $7.40 |
| Bear | 25% | 145 | 11× | $4.38 |
| **Probability-weighted** | | | | **$7.02** |

(Implied price = (EBITDA × multiple − net debt $500m) ÷ 250m shares.)

**Recommendation:** **Do not buy at $8.00** — the expected value ($7.02) is below the price, and the bear case shows meaningful downside given leverage. Revisit if the price falls below ~$6.50 or if evidence emerges that margins can exceed 32% (e.g., pharma contracts).

*Training simulation — fictional company and figures.*
:::

:::tip Graduate vs professional
The graduate likes the growth story and concludes “buy”. The professional asks what growth is already in the price, how leverage amplifies the bear case, whether free cash flow will actually arrive, and what the expected value is relative to the price.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Recompute the implied price in the base case if net debt were $300m instead of $500m.
2. What three pieces of evidence would make you change the recommendation to “buy”?
3. List five due-diligence questions you would ask management.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (162 × 14.5 − 300) ÷ 250 = (2,349 − 300) ÷ 250 = **$8.20**.
2. Signed long-term pharma contracts at higher margins; capex guidance cut with no growth loss (FCF earlier); occupancy data showing pricing power (rate increases accepted); share price fall that leaves the base case with upside.
3. What is the contract length and renewal rate of top customers? What are utilisation rates by site and pricing trends? What return do you expect on new warehouses and how have past projects performed? How are energy costs managed/hedged? What is the capital allocation priority between growth, dividends and debt reduction?
:::

:::summary Part X summary
- Primary markets raise capital; secondary markets provide liquidity and pricing.
- Equity returns come from dividends, earnings growth and multiple changes; compare multiples within sectors.
- Bond prices move inversely to yields; duration measures sensitivity; spreads price credit risk.
- Funds differ in liquidity, strategy, fees and investor eligibility; mandates set the rules.
- Diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk; asset allocation drives portfolio outcomes; measure performance on a risk-adjusted basis.
- Derivatives can hedge, speculate or arbitrage — purpose and governance matter.
- Good investment decisions compare expected value with price and respect the downside.
:::
