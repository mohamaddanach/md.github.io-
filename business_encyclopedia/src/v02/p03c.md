# Consolidation, IFRS vs US GAAP and the Limits of Financial Reporting

:::obj In this chapter
How group accounts are prepared; the main differences between IFRS and US GAAP that a business professional should recognise; and what financial statements cannot tell you.
:::

## Consolidated financial statements (IFRS 10)

A parent that **controls** subsidiaries presents consolidated statements as if the group were a single economic entity. Control exists when the investor has power over the investee, exposure to variable returns, and the ability to use its power to affect those returns — usually, but not always, through more than 50% of voting rights.

| Relationship | Typical ownership | Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Subsidiary (control) | > 50% or control by agreement | Full consolidation; non-controlling interest (NCI) shown in equity |
| Joint venture (joint control, rights to net assets) | Often 50/50 | Equity method (IFRS 11) |
| Joint operation (rights to assets and obligations for liabilities) | Varies | Recognise share of assets, liabilities, revenue, expenses |
| Associate (significant influence) | Typically 20–50% | Equity method (IAS 28): one line in balance sheet and income statement |
| Financial investment | < 20%, no influence | Fair value (IFRS 9) |

### Consolidation steps

1. Add together line by line the parent’s and subsidiaries’ assets, liabilities, income and expenses (after aligning accounting policies and translating foreign subsidiaries into the presentation currency).
2. Eliminate the parent’s investment against the subsidiary’s equity at acquisition; recognise goodwill and NCI.
3. Eliminate **intra-group** balances (receivables/payables, loans) and transactions (sales, dividends, management fees), and unrealised profits in inventory sold within the group.
4. Allocate profit and equity between owners of the parent and NCI.

:::example Simple consolidation with NCI (fictional)
Parent P acquires 80% of S for $800k when S’s net assets (fair value) are $750k. NCI measured at its share of net assets: 20% × 750 = $150k. Goodwill = 800 + 150 − 750 = **$200k**.

During the year S earns $100k profit and sells goods to P for $60k with a $15k margin; P still holds half of them at year-end. Intra-group sale of $60k is eliminated from revenue and COGS; unrealised profit of $7.5k is eliminated from inventory. S’s adjusted profit 92.5k → NCI share 18.5k; parent share 74k.
:::

:::warn Why consolidation matters to bankers and investors
- **Structural subordination:** a lender to the holding company is paid only from dividends upstreamed by subsidiaries, after subsidiaries’ own creditors.
- **Trapped cash:** consolidated cash may sit in countries with exchange controls.
- **Intra-group transactions** can inflate the revenue of individual entities; always ask for consolidated and standalone figures.
- **NCI:** consolidated EBITDA includes 100% of subsidiaries’ EBITDA even if the parent owns 51%.
:::

## Foreign currency (IAS 21) — essentials

Each entity has a **functional currency** (the currency of its primary economic environment). Foreign-currency transactions are recorded at the spot rate; monetary items (receivables, payables, loans) are retranslated at closing rates, with differences in profit or loss. Foreign subsidiaries are translated into the group’s presentation currency (assets/liabilities at closing rate, income at average rates), with differences in other comprehensive income (the translation reserve). Hyperinflationary economies require IAS 29 restatement — relevant in several countries in recent years.

## IFRS vs US GAAP: differences a professional should recognise

| Topic | IFRS | US GAAP |
|---|---|---|
| Framework style | More principles-based | More rules and industry guidance |
| Inventory cost | FIFO or weighted average; LIFO prohibited | LIFO permitted |
| Inventory write-down reversals | Required when NRV recovers | Not permitted |
| Development costs | Capitalised when criteria met | Generally expensed (exceptions, e.g., certain software) |
| PP&E revaluation | Permitted (revaluation model) | Not permitted |
| Impairment reversals (other than goodwill) | Permitted | Not permitted for assets held and used |
| Leases (lessee) | Single model: depreciation + interest | Finance vs operating leases; operating leases show straight-line lease cost |
| Interest paid in cash flow | Operating or financing (until IFRS 18 changes) | Operating |
| Extraordinary items | Prohibited | Eliminated |
| Debt covenant breach before year-end | Classification depends on rights at reporting date (IAS 1 amendments) | Specific rules on waivers |
| Financial statement titles | Statement of financial position; statement of profit or loss | Balance sheet; income statement |

Revenue recognition (IFRS 15 / ASC 606) is largely converged; differences remain in details.

## The limits of financial reporting

Financial statements are indispensable but incomplete:

1. **Historical:** they report the past, often 2–4 months after period-end.
2. **Estimates and judgement:** useful lives, provisions, expected credit losses, fair values, revenue over time.
3. **Unrecognised value:** internally generated brands, customer relationships, talent, data and culture do not appear.
4. **Management incentives:** choices can smooth or flatter results within the rules (and fraud can occur outside them).
5. **Aggregation:** consolidated numbers hide segment problems.
6. **Off-balance-sheet exposures:** guarantees, commitments, some supplier finance, contingent liabilities appear only in notes.
7. **Non-financial risks:** climate, cyber, regulatory, key-person risk — partly addressed by sustainability reporting (e.g., the ISSB’s IFRS S1 and S2 standards, issued 2023 and adopted by an increasing number of jurisdictions).
8. **Comparability limits:** different standards, policies, and non-GAAP “adjusted” metrics.

:::tip How professionals compensate
They read the **notes** (accounting policies, segment information, related parties, contingencies, debt maturities, subsequent events), the **auditor’s report** (including key audit matters), management commentary, and they triangulate with non-financial data: customer interviews, site visits, industry data, payment behaviour, credit bureau reports.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A group owns 60% of a subsidiary with EBITDA of $10m and net debt of $30m; the parent standalone has EBITDA $5m and net debt $20m. Compute consolidated EBITDA and net debt and the parent’s “look-through” proportional figures. Which matters for a lender to the parent?
2. Name four items you would look for in the notes before lending to a company.
3. A company switches from expensing to capitalising development costs. What happens to EBITDA, net income and operating cash flow?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Consolidated EBITDA = **$15m**; net debt **$50m** (leverage 3.3×). Proportional: EBITDA 5 + 6 = $11m; net debt 20 + 18 = $38m (3.5×). A lender to the parent cares about the parent’s own cash and its access to subsidiary cash (dividends 60% after the subsidiary’s debt service), and about structural subordination — consolidated ratios can overstate the parent lender’s position.
2. Debt maturities and covenants; contingent liabilities and guarantees; related-party transactions; segment results; accounting policy changes; subsequent events; lease commitments; pledged assets.
3. EBITDA rises (costs move from operating expenses to an asset, later amortised below EBITDA); net income rises initially; operating cash flow rises while investing cash flow falls by the same amount — total cash is unchanged.
:::

# Management Accounting, Cost Accounting, Budgeting and Variance Analysis

:::obj In this chapter
Accounting for decision-making: cost behaviour and costing methods, contribution analysis, relevant costs, budgeting and forecasting, and variance analysis that explains why results differ from plan.
:::

## Financial vs management accounting

| | Financial accounting | Management accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Users | External: investors, lenders, regulators, tax | Internal: managers, board |
| Rules | IFRS/GAAP, law | Whatever is useful |
| Focus | Entity as a whole, historical | Products, customers, projects, future |
| Frequency | Quarterly/annual | Daily to monthly |
| Precision | High, audited | Timely and relevant beats precise |

## Costing methods

- **Job costing:** costs accumulated per order or project (construction, consulting, custom manufacturing).
- **Process costing:** costs averaged over continuous output (cement, beverages, chemicals).
- **Absorption costing:** product cost includes fixed production overheads (required for external inventory valuation).
- **Marginal (variable) costing:** only variable costs are product costs; fixed costs are period costs — better for short-term decisions.
- **Activity-based costing (ABC):** overheads assigned according to the activities products/customers consume (orders, deliveries, set-ups). ABC often reveals that small, complex customers are unprofitable.
- **Standard costing:** predetermined costs per unit used for planning and variance analysis.

:::example Customer profitability with ABC (fictional distributor)
| | Customer A (hotel chain) | Customer B (small restaurants, 40 accounts) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Gross margin | 18% → $180,000 | 30% → $300,000 |
| Orders per year (cost $45 per order processed) | 120 → $5,400 | 4,000 → $180,000 |
| Deliveries ($60 each) | 150 → $9,000 | 3,500 → $210,000 |
| Credit control and collection | $6,000 | $40,000 |
| Account management | $25,000 | $20,000 |
| **Customer contribution** | **$134,600** | **−$150,000** |

The high-margin small customers destroy value once order and delivery costs are assigned. Actions: minimum order values, delivery charges, digital ordering, or serving them through a wholesaler.
:::

## Relevant costs for decisions

A cost is **relevant** to a decision if it is a future cash flow that differs between alternatives. Ignore sunk costs and allocated fixed costs that will not change. Include opportunity costs.

:::example Special order decision
A factory with spare capacity receives a one-off export order for 10,000 units at $8.00. Full absorption cost is $9.20 (variable $6.50 + fixed overhead allocation $2.70). Relevant cost = variable $6.50 + special packaging $0.30 = $6.80 → contribution **$12,000**. Accept, provided it does not undermine domestic pricing, uses genuinely spare capacity, and the buyer’s credit risk is acceptable.
:::

## Budgeting

A budget is a quantified plan for a period, usually a year, broken into months. It translates strategy into targets, allocates resources, and provides a benchmark for control.

The budget sequence for a trading or manufacturing company:

1. Sales budget (volume × price by product/customer/region) — the hardest and most important.
2. Production or purchasing budget (sales + desired closing stock − opening stock).
3. Direct cost budgets (materials, labour, freight).
4. Overhead budgets by cost centre.
5. Capex budget.
6. Budgeted income statement.
7. **Cash budget** (timing of receipts and payments) — reveals funding needs.
8. Budgeted balance sheet.

| Approach | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incremental | Last year + x% | Simple | Perpetuates inefficiency |
| Zero-based (ZBB) | Every cost justified from zero | Challenges spending | Time-consuming |
| Rolling forecast | Continuously extends 12–18 months ahead | Adapts to change | Requires discipline and systems |
| Activity-based | Based on planned activity drivers | Links cost to operations | Complex |
| Flexible budget | Adjusts to actual volume | Fair performance comparison | Needs cost behaviour data |

:::example Cash budget (fictional, three months)
Sales: Jan $400k, Feb $500k, Mar $600k; 30% cash, 70% collected next month (December sales $380k). Purchases = 70% of next month’s sales, paid the month after purchase (December purchases $280k). Fixed cash costs $90k/month. Opening cash $60k; overdraft limit $200k.

| $000 | Jan | Feb | Mar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash sales (30%) | 120 | 150 | 180 |
| Collections (70% of last month) | 266 | 280 | 350 |
| Payments to suppliers (prior month’s purchases) | (280) | (350) | (420) |
| Fixed costs | (90) | (90) | (90) |
| Net cash flow | 16 | (10) | 20 |
| Opening cash | 60 | 76 | 66 |
| Closing cash | 76 | 66 | 86 |

Purchases: Jan purchases = 70% × Feb sales = 350 (paid Feb); Feb purchases = 70% × 600 = 420 (paid Mar). Growth absorbs cash: despite rising sales, cash barely grows. If April sales jump to $900k, March purchases (630) paid in April would push cash negative — the budget gives the treasurer time to arrange funding.
:::

## Variance analysis

Variance = actual − budget (or standard). **Favourable (F)** variances increase profit; **adverse (A)** reduce it. The power of variance analysis is decomposing a variance into causes that someone can act on.

:::formula Sales and cost variances
Sales price variance = (Actual price − Budget price) × Actual volume
Sales volume variance (contribution basis) = (Actual volume − Budget volume) × Budget contribution per unit
Material price variance = (Actual price − Standard price) × Actual quantity purchased
Material usage variance = (Actual quantity used − Standard quantity for actual output) × Standard price
Labour rate variance = (Actual rate − Standard rate) × Actual hours
Labour efficiency variance = (Actual hours − Standard hours for actual output) × Standard rate
:::

:::example Variance analysis (fictional month)
Budget: 10,000 units at $50; variable cost $30 (material 2 kg × $10 + labour 0.5 h × $20); fixed costs $120k. Budget profit = 10,000 × 20 − 120,000 = $80,000.

Actual: 11,000 units sold at $48; material 23,500 kg bought and used at $10.40; labour 5,200 h at $21; fixed costs $126k. Actual profit = 528,000 − 244,400 − 109,200 − 126,000 = $48,400.

| Variance | Calculation | $ |
|---|---|---|
| Sales volume | (11,000 − 10,000) × 20 | +20,000 F |
| Sales price | (48 − 50) × 11,000 | −22,000 A |
| Material price | (10.40 − 10) × 23,500 | −9,400 A |
| Material usage | (23,500 − 22,000) × 10 | −15,000 A |
| Labour rate | (21 − 20) × 5,200 | −5,200 A |
| Labour efficiency | (5,200 − 5,500) × 20 | +6,000 F |
| Fixed overhead spending | 126,000 − 120,000 | −6,000 A |
| **Total** | 80,000 → 48,400 | **−31,600** |

Story for management: volume beat budget but was “bought” with a 4% price cut; material usage was 6.8% over standard (quality or waste issue at the plant); labour was efficient but paid overtime rates.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A product sells at $40, variable cost $26, fixed overhead allocation $9. A customer offers $30 for 2,000 units using spare capacity. Accept?
2. Budget: 5,000 units at $100, contribution $35/unit. Actual: 4,600 units at $104. Compute sales price and volume variances.
3. Why might zero-based budgeting be useful after an acquisition?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Relevant contribution = 30 − 26 = $4 per unit → **+$8,000**; accept if capacity is truly spare, there is no cannibalisation of $40 sales, and credit risk is acceptable. The $9 overhead allocation is irrelevant.
2. Price: (104 − 100) × 4,600 = **+$18,400 F**; volume: (4,600 − 5,000) × 35 = **−$14,000 A**; net +$4,400 F.
3. It forces the combined organisation to justify every cost from zero, exposing duplicated functions and unnecessary spending that incremental budgeting would carry over — supporting synergy delivery.
:::

# Financial Statement Analysis and Ratios

:::obj In this chapter
A structured method to analyse a company’s statements: profitability, efficiency, liquidity, leverage and coverage, returns, cash-flow quality, and earnings quality, with a full worked analysis.
:::

## A method, not a list of ratios

1. **Understand the business** first: model, industry, strategy, risks. Ratios without context mislead.
2. **Read the statements and notes**, including the auditor’s report.
3. **Normalise:** remove one-off items, align IFRS 16 treatment, adjust for acquisitions.
4. **Compute ratios** over at least 3 years and against peers.
5. **Explain the drivers** (why did margins change?).
6. **Check cash:** does profit convert to cash?
7. **Conclude** for the decision at hand: lend, invest, partner, supply on credit.

## The main ratio families

| Family | Ratio | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth | Revenue growth | (Rev_t ÷ Rev_t−1) − 1 | Split into price, volume, mix, FX, acquisitions |
| Profitability | Gross margin | Gross profit ÷ revenue | Pricing power, input costs |
| | EBITDA margin | EBITDA ÷ revenue | Operating cash earnings proxy |
| | Operating (EBIT) margin | EBIT ÷ revenue | After asset consumption |
| | Net margin | Net income ÷ revenue | After financing and tax |
| Efficiency | Asset turnover | Revenue ÷ average total assets | Capital intensity |
| | DSO | Receivables ÷ revenue × 365 | Collection speed |
| | DIO | Inventory ÷ COGS × 365 | Stock holding |
| | DPO | Payables ÷ COGS × 365 | Supplier credit |
| | Cash conversion cycle | DIO + DSO − DPO | Working-capital intensity |
| Liquidity | Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | Short-term coverage (crude) |
| | Quick ratio | (Current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities | Excludes slower assets |
| | Cash ratio | Cash ÷ current liabilities | Strictest |
| Leverage | Debt / equity | Total debt ÷ equity | Balance-sheet gearing |
| | Net debt / EBITDA | (Debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA | Years to repay from EBITDA (rough) |
| | Debt / capital | Debt ÷ (debt + equity) | Capital structure |
| Coverage | Interest cover | EBIT (or EBITDA) ÷ interest | Ability to pay interest |
| | DSCR | Cash available for debt service ÷ (interest + principal) | Ability to pay all debt service |
| | Fixed-charge cover | (EBITDA + rent) ÷ (interest + rent) | Includes lease obligations |
| Returns | ROA | Net income ÷ average total assets | Asset productivity |
| | ROE | Net income ÷ average equity | Owners’ return |
| | ROCE | EBIT ÷ (total assets − current liabilities) | Return on long-term capital |
| | ROIC | NOPAT ÷ invested capital | Value creation vs WACC |
| Cash quality | Cash conversion | CFO ÷ EBITDA (or ÷ net income) | Earnings turning into cash |
| | FCF margin | (CFO − capex) ÷ revenue | Cash generation |
| | Capex / depreciation | Capex ÷ D&A | Under-investment if persistently < 1 |
| Market | P/E, EV/EBITDA, dividend yield | See Part X | Valuation relative to earnings |

:::formula DuPont decomposition of ROE
ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier
= (Net income ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity)

Extended: ROE = (NI ÷ PBT) × (PBT ÷ EBIT) × (EBIT ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity)
= tax burden × interest burden × operating margin × asset turnover × leverage
:::

## Worked analysis: two fictional distributors

| $m | Alpha Trading | Beta Distribution |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 100 | 100 |
| Gross profit | 22 | 30 |
| EBITDA | 8 | 12 |
| Depreciation | 1 | 4 |
| EBIT | 7 | 8 |
| Interest | 1.5 | 3.5 |
| Net income (tax 15%) | 4.68 | 3.83 |
| Total assets | 40 | 70 |
| Equity | 20 | 18 |
| Debt | 14 | 42 |
| Cash | 2 | 4 |
| Receivables | 16 | 20 |
| Inventory | 12 | 25 |
| Payables | 8 | 9 |
| CFO | 6.5 | 4.0 |
| Capex | 1.0 | 6.0 |

| Ratio | Alpha | Beta |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | 22% | 30% |
| EBITDA margin | 8% | 12% |
| Net margin | 4.7% | 3.8% |
| Asset turnover | 2.5× | 1.43× |
| Equity multiplier | 2.0× | 3.9× |
| ROE | 23.4% | 21.3% |
| DSO | 58 days | 73 days |
| DIO (on COGS) | 56 days | 130 days |
| DPO | 37 days | 47 days |
| CCC | 77 days | 156 days |
| Net debt / EBITDA | 1.5× | 3.2× |
| Interest cover (EBIT) | 4.7× | 2.3× |
| CFO / EBITDA | 81% | 33% |
| FCF | 5.5 | −2.0 |

**Conclusion:** Beta has better margins but ties up far more capital in inventory and receivables, has twice the leverage and burns cash after capex. Alpha’s similar ROE comes from efficiency, not leverage. A lender would prefer Alpha; an investor in Beta would need to believe inventory is saleable and that capex will lift returns.

## Earnings quality: warning signs

| Signal | Possible issue |
|---|---|
| Net income growing, CFO flat or falling | Aggressive revenue recognition; working-capital build-up |
| DSO rising quarter after quarter | Weak customers, channel stuffing, disputed invoices |
| Inventory growing faster than sales | Obsolescence; over-buying; inflated stock |
| Capitalised costs rising (development, “other assets”) | Expenses shifted to the balance sheet |
| Frequent “one-off” restructuring charges | Recurring costs presented as exceptional |
| Large gap between adjusted EBITDA and reported EBIT | Generous adjustments |
| Changes in auditors, accounting policies or useful lives | Smoothing; disagreement |
| Related-party sales or purchases | Non-arm’s-length transactions |
| Low effective tax rate vs peers without explanation | Tax risk |
| Complex structures, frequent acquisitions | Opaque organic growth |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Compute DuPont components and ROE: net income $6m, revenue $80m, average assets $60m, average equity $24m.
2. A borrower: EBITDA $20m, interest $4m, scheduled principal $10m, tax paid $2m, maintenance capex $3m. Compute DSCR (cash available = EBITDA − tax − maintenance capex).
3. Revenue +15%, receivables +45%, inventory +30%, net income +20%, CFO −40%. What story would you investigate?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Net margin 7.5%; asset turnover 1.33×; equity multiplier 2.5× → ROE = 0.075 × 1.333 × 2.5 = **25%** (check: 6 ÷ 24 = 25%).
2. Cash available = 20 − 2 − 3 = $15m; debt service = 4 + 10 = $14m → DSCR = **1.07×** — thin; most lenders want ≥ 1.20–1.30×.
3. Earnings are not converting to cash: receivables growing three times faster than sales (extended terms, collection issues, possibly fictitious or premature revenue) and inventory build-up. Investigate customer ageing, top new customers, credit terms granted, returns after period-end, and inventory composition.
:::

# Internal Controls and Audit

:::obj In this chapter
What internal control is, the COSO framework, key controls in the main business cycles, fraud risks, and how external and internal audits work — including what an audit opinion does and does not guarantee.
:::

## Internal control and the COSO framework

**Internal control** is a process designed to provide reasonable assurance about the achievement of objectives in operations, reporting and compliance. The most widely used framework is COSO’s *Internal Control — Integrated Framework* (2013), with five components:

| Component | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Control environment | Tone at the top, integrity, governance, structure | Code of conduct, independent board, competent finance team |
| Risk assessment | Identifying and analysing risks to objectives, including fraud | Annual risk assessment, fraud risk review |
| Control activities | Policies and procedures that mitigate risks | Approvals, reconciliations, segregation of duties, access controls |
| Information and communication | Quality information flows | Reporting lines, whistleblowing channel |
| Monitoring activities | Ongoing and separate evaluations | Internal audit, management reviews |

Control types: **preventive** (stop errors/fraud: approvals, system validations) vs **detective** (find them: reconciliations, exception reports); **manual** vs **automated** (system-enforced, more reliable if IT general controls are sound).

## Key controls by business cycle

| Cycle | Key risks | Key controls |
|---|---|---|
| Order-to-cash | Sales to bad credit risks; unbilled deliveries; fictitious sales; misappropriated receipts | Credit limits enforced in ERP; delivery-to-invoice matching; sequential invoice numbers; lockbox/bank collections; receivables reconciliation; credit-note approval |
| Procure-to-pay | Fictitious suppliers; duplicate payments; overpricing; kickbacks | Vendor master changes controlled and verified by call-back; three-way match; DoA approvals; dual signatures for payments; supplier statement reconciliation |
| Payroll | Ghost employees; unauthorised changes | HR/payroll segregation; payroll change approvals; headcount reconciliations |
| Inventory | Theft; obsolescence; misstatement | Physical access control; cycle counts; full year-end count; ageing review |
| Treasury | Unauthorised payments; fraud; FX losses | Bank mandates; dual authorisation; payment limits; daily bank reconciliation; segregation of dealing and settlement |
| Financial close | Misstatement | Account reconciliations; review of journals (especially manual, round-sum, period-end); management review |
| IT general controls | Unauthorised changes or access | User access reviews; change management; backups; segregation of developers and production |

:::warn Classic fraud schemes
- **Business email compromise:** fake email from a “supplier” or “CEO” changes bank details or requests an urgent transfer.
- **Fictitious vendor:** an employee creates a supplier and approves its invoices.
- **Lapping:** stealing one customer’s payment and covering it with the next customer’s payment.
- **Kickbacks:** purchasing manager receives payments for awarding contracts at inflated prices.
- **Financial statement fraud:** fictitious revenue, hidden liabilities, inventory inflation — often under pressure to meet targets or covenants.
- **Trade fraud:** duplicate financing of the same goods with different banks; fake warehouse receipts.

Fraud triangle (Cressey): **pressure**, **opportunity** and **rationalisation**. Controls reduce opportunity; culture and incentives address the others.
:::

## External audit

An external audit provides **reasonable (not absolute) assurance** that financial statements are free from material misstatement. Audits of most entities follow International Standards on Auditing (ISAs) issued by the IAASB; US public companies are audited under PCAOB standards.

The audit process:

1. **Acceptance and planning:** understand the entity, assess risks, set materiality (often a percentage of profit before tax, revenue or total assets).
2. **Risk assessment:** identify significant risks (revenue recognition and management override of controls are presumed risks under ISA 240).
3. **Tests of controls** where the auditor relies on them.
4. **Substantive procedures:** analytical review; tests of details — confirmations (bank, receivables), attendance at inventory counts, vouching to invoices, recalculation, cut-off tests, review of subsequent events.
5. **Completion:** going-concern assessment, management representations, evaluation of misstatements.
6. **Reporting:** opinion; key audit matters (for listed entities); management letter on control weaknesses.

| Opinion | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unmodified (clean) | Statements are fairly presented in all material respects |
| Unmodified with “material uncertainty related to going concern” section | Fair presentation, but significant doubt about continuing as a going concern |
| Emphasis of matter | Fair, but the auditor draws attention to a disclosed matter |
| Qualified | Fair except for a specific material (not pervasive) issue |
| Adverse | Statements are materially and pervasively misstated |
| Disclaimer | Auditor could not obtain sufficient evidence to form an opinion |

:::tip What an audit does not guarantee
A clean audit opinion does not certify that the business is healthy, that management is honest, that there is no fraud below materiality, or that the company will survive. It says the statements are fairly presented under the framework, based on evidence gathered on a sampling and risk basis. Lenders still perform their own analysis; acquirers still do due diligence.
:::

## Internal audit

Internal audit is an independent, objective assurance and consulting function within the organisation, reporting functionally to the board’s audit committee. Under the Institute of Internal Auditors’ framework (Global Internal Audit Standards, effective January 2025), it evaluates governance, risk management and control processes. In the **three lines model**: management owns and manages risk (first line), risk and compliance functions provide expertise and oversight (second line), and internal audit provides independent assurance (third line).

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A purchasing manager can create suppliers in the ERP, approve purchase orders up to $100k and approve invoices for payment. Identify the risk and propose controls.
2. Name three audit procedures to verify trade receivables.
3. The auditor’s report includes a “material uncertainty related to going concern” section. You are a supplier asked to give 90-day credit. What do you do?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Lack of segregation of duties: he could create a fictitious supplier, raise POs and approve payment. Controls: vendor master creation by a separate team with independent verification (registration, bank letter, call-back); invoice approval by budget owner and AP; three-way match; periodic review of new suppliers and payments; rotation and audit analytics (duplicate bank accounts between employees and vendors).
2. External confirmation of balances with customers; testing subsequent receipts after year-end; reviewing the ageing and allowance; cut-off testing of invoices and delivery notes around year-end; vouching sample balances to contracts and proofs of delivery.
3. Treat as high risk: request recent management accounts and cash forecast, reduce terms (advance payment or cash against documents), require a bank guarantee/SBLC or credit insurance cover, set a low credit limit, and monitor payments closely.
:::

:::summary Part III summary
- Double entry and the accounting equation make the three statements mutually consistent; master the three-statement effects of every common transaction.
- Revenue follows transfer of control (IFRS 15); expenses follow consumption of benefits; this creates the timing gap with cash.
- Balance-sheet judgements — inventory, receivables, useful lives, provisions, leases — drive the quality of reported numbers.
- Management accounting turns data into decisions: contribution, relevant costs, budgets and variances.
- Analysis is about drivers and cash conversion, not computing every ratio.
- Controls and audits reduce, but never eliminate, the risk of error and fraud.
:::
