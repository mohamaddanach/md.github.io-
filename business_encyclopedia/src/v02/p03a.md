Accounting is the measurement system of business. A finance graduate knows the statements; the aim of this part is fluency: being able to record any transaction, see instantly how it moves the three statements, judge whether reported numbers are credible, and use accounting information for management decisions.

The part follows the accounting cycle: principles and bookkeeping mechanics ({{ch:Accounting Foundations}}), recognition rules ({{ch:Revenue and Expense Recognition}}), specific assets and liabilities ({{ch:Assets and Liabilities in Detail}}), the statements themselves with a complete worked example ({{ch:Three Financial Statements}}), group accounts and standards ({{ch:Consolidation, IFRS}}), management accounting ({{ch:Management Accounting}}), analysis ({{ch:Financial Statement Analysis}}) and controls and audit ({{ch:Internal Controls and Audit}}).

:::obj Learning objectives for Part III
- Record transactions with correct journal entries and produce a trial balance and financial statements.
- Apply accrual concepts, revenue recognition (IFRS 15) and expense recognition.
- Account for inventory, receivables, fixed assets, intangibles, provisions and leases.
- Show how any single transaction affects the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement.
- Use budgets, cost accounting and variance analysis for management decisions.
- Analyse financial statements with ratios and judge earnings quality.
- Understand internal controls and what an audit does — and does not — provide.
:::

:::framework Which standards are referred to
**IFRS Accounting Standards** are issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) of the IFRS Foundation; older standards are called IAS. They are required or permitted for listed companies in many jurisdictions, including the EU, the UK, most of the Middle East and much of Asia; in several Gulf countries IFRS applies broadly to companies, but local requirements must be checked. **US GAAP** is set by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) and codified in the Accounting Standards Codification (ASC). Small entities may use simplified frameworks (e.g., IFRS for SMEs). This part explains principles at a level suitable for business professionals; accountants preparing statements must use the full current standards.
:::

# Accounting Foundations: Principles, Double Entry, Ledgers and Trial Balance

:::obj In this chapter
The concepts underlying financial statements, the rules of debit and credit, the chart of accounts, journal entries, the general ledger and the trial balance.
:::

## The purpose and qualities of financial information

The IASB’s Conceptual Framework says the objective of general-purpose financial reporting is to provide information useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions about providing resources to the entity.

| Quality | Meaning | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Capable of making a difference to decisions (predictive and confirmatory value; materiality) | Report what matters, in enough detail |
| Faithful representation | Complete, neutral, free from error; substance over legal form | Do not hide liabilities in clever structures |
| Comparability | Users can compare across periods and entities | Consistent policies; disclose changes |
| Verifiability | Independent observers could reach consensus | Evidence and audit trails |
| Timeliness | Available in time to influence decisions | Fast monthly close |
| Understandability | Classified, characterised and presented clearly | Clear notes and segment information |

### Key underlying concepts

- **Going concern:** statements assume the entity will continue operating for the foreseeable future. If not, a different basis applies and assets may be worth far less.
- **Accrual basis:** effects of transactions are recorded when they occur, not when cash moves.
- **Entity concept:** the business is separate from its owners — the owner’s personal expenses are not company expenses.
- **Prudence (caution):** exercising caution under uncertainty, without deliberately understating assets or overstating liabilities.
- **Substance over form:** economic reality prevails over legal form (e.g., a “sale” with an obligation to repurchase may in substance be a loan).
- **Materiality:** information is material if omitting or misstating it could reasonably influence users’ decisions.
- **Measurement bases:** historical cost, fair value, current value; most operating assets use historical cost less depreciation and impairment; many financial instruments use fair value or amortised cost.

## The accounting equation and the rules of debit and credit

:::formula The accounting equation
Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Equity = Share capital + Retained earnings (+ other reserves)
Retained earnings increase with revenue and decrease with expenses and dividends, so the expanded form is:
Assets + Expenses + Dividends = Liabilities + Share capital + Retained earnings (opening) + Revenue
:::

Every transaction keeps the equation in balance. **Debit** (Dr) means the left side of an account; **credit** (Cr) the right side.

| Account type | Increase | Decrease | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assets | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Dividends / drawings | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Equity (capital, retained earnings) | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Revenue / income | Credit | Debit | Credit |

:::tip A memory aid that actually works
Think about **cash** first. Receiving cash is always a debit to cash (an asset increases). Paying cash is always a credit to cash. Then ask what the other side is: if you received cash because you sold something, credit revenue; because you borrowed, credit loans; because a customer paid an invoice, credit receivables. Bank statements confuse beginners because they are written from the bank’s point of view: your deposit is the bank’s liability, so the bank “credits” your account.
:::

:::ar المدين والدائن
كلمتا «مدين» و«دائن» لا تعنيان «جيد» و«سيئ»، بل هما جانبا الحساب: المدين هو الجانب الأيسر، والدائن هو الجانب الأيمن. الأصول والمصاريف تزيد بالمدين، أما الالتزامات وحقوق الملكية والإيرادات فتزيد بالدائن. ولكل عملية طرفان على الأقل، ويجب أن يتساوى مجموع المدين مع مجموع الدائن. أسهل طريقة للتعلّم هي البدء بالنقد: استلام النقد يكون دائماً مديناً في حساب النقدية، ودفعه يكون دائناً.
:::

## The chart of accounts

A chart of accounts (CoA) lists all accounts with codes, grouped to produce the statements and management reports. A typical numbering:

| Range | Group | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1000–1999 | Assets | 1010 Cash at bank – USD; 1100 Trade receivables; 1200 Inventory; 1300 Prepayments; 1500 Property, plant and equipment; 1590 Accumulated depreciation; 1600 Intangible assets |
| 2000–2999 | Liabilities | 2010 Trade payables; 2100 Accrued expenses; 2200 VAT payable; 2300 Deferred revenue; 2500 Bank loans; 2600 Lease liabilities; 2700 Provisions |
| 3000–3999 | Equity | 3010 Share capital; 3100 Statutory reserve; 3200 Retained earnings |
| 4000–4999 | Revenue | 4010 Sales – products; 4020 Sales – services; 4900 Other income |
| 5000–5999 | Cost of sales | 5010 Cost of goods sold; 5020 Inbound freight; 5030 Customs duties |
| 6000–7999 | Operating expenses | 6010 Salaries; 6100 Rent; 6200 Marketing; 6500 Depreciation; 6600 IT |
| 8000–8999 | Finance and tax | 8010 Interest expense; 8100 FX gains/losses; 8900 Income tax |

Good CoA design matters for a CS-minded professional: it is a data model. Combine the account code with **dimensions** (cost centre, project, product line, region, customer) rather than creating thousands of accounts. This enables multi-dimensional reporting in BI tools without duplicating the ledger.

## Journal entries

A journal entry records the date, accounts, debit and credit amounts and a description, with supporting evidence (invoice, contract, bank advice).

:::example Ten journal entries for a new trading company (fictional, USD)
| # | Transaction | Debit | Credit | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Owners inject capital | Cash at bank | Share capital | 200,000 |
| 2 | Bank loan received | Cash at bank | Bank loan | 100,000 |
| 3 | Buy delivery van for cash | Vehicles (PP&E) | Cash at bank | 45,000 |
| 4 | Buy inventory on credit | Inventory | Trade payables | 120,000 |
| 5 | Pay import duty and freight on that inventory | Inventory | Cash at bank | 9,000 |
| 6 | Sell goods on credit (selling price) | Trade receivables | Sales revenue | 150,000 |
| 6b | Cost of the goods sold | Cost of goods sold | Inventory | 96,750 |
| 7 | Pay supplier part | Trade payables | Cash at bank | 80,000 |
| 8 | Customer pays | Cash at bank | Trade receivables | 110,000 |
| 9 | Pay salaries | Salaries expense | Cash at bank | 18,000 |
| 10 | Month-end: rent for the month not yet invoiced | Rent expense | Accrued expenses | 6,000 |

Entry 6b: the goods sold were 75% of the inventory purchased (120,000 + 9,000 = 129,000 × 75% = 96,750). Inventory cost includes freight and duty (IAS 2 includes costs of purchase, including import duties and transport, in the cost of inventories).
:::

## The general ledger and T-accounts

Each journal entry is posted to ledger accounts. A **T-account** shows debits on the left, credits on the right.

```
              Cash at bank (1010)
  Dr                                Cr
  (1) Capital       200,000  | (3) Van            45,000
  (2) Loan          100,000  | (5) Duty/freight    9,000
  (8) Customer      110,000  | (7) Supplier       80,000
                             | (9) Salaries       18,000
  ---------------------------+------------------------------
  Total             410,000  | Total             152,000
  Balance c/d (Dr)  258,000
```

## The trial balance

A trial balance lists all ledger balances. Total debits must equal total credits. It does **not** prove the accounts are correct: an entry posted to the wrong account, or omitted entirely, still balances.

:::example Trial balance after the ten entries
| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Cash at bank | 258,000 | |
| Trade receivables | 40,000 | |
| Inventory (129,000 − 96,750) | 32,250 | |
| Vehicles | 45,000 | |
| Trade payables (120,000 − 80,000) | | 40,000 |
| Accrued expenses | | 6,000 |
| Bank loan | | 100,000 |
| Share capital | | 200,000 |
| Sales revenue | | 150,000 |
| Cost of goods sold | 96,750 | |
| Salaries expense | 18,000 | |
| Rent expense | 6,000 | |
| **Totals** | **496,000** | **496,000** |
:::

Profit for the month (before depreciation and interest): 150,000 − 96,750 − 18,000 − 6,000 = **29,250**. {{ch:Three Financial Statements}} adds depreciation, interest and tax and produces full statements.

## The accounting cycle and the month-end close

1. Capture source documents (invoices, receipts, contracts, bank data).
2. Record journal entries (mostly automatically from sub-ledgers: sales, purchases, payroll, fixed assets).
3. Post to the general ledger.
4. Reconcile: bank, receivables and payables control accounts vs sub-ledgers, intercompany, inventory counts, VAT.
5. Adjusting entries: accruals, prepayments, depreciation, provisions, FX revaluation, inventory adjustments, deferred revenue.
6. Adjusted trial balance.
7. Financial statements and management reports.
8. Closing entries at year-end: revenue and expense accounts are closed to retained earnings.

Leading finance teams close the month in 3–5 working days, using automation for bank reconciliation, intercompany matching and accrual calculations.

:::warn Common bookkeeping mistakes
- Recording personal or owner expenses in the company books.
- Treating loan principal repayments as expenses (only interest is an expense).
- Expensing capital items (a $40k machine) or capitalising repairs.
- Forgetting accruals: costs of the month invoiced next month.
- Recording customer advances as revenue.
- Not reconciling the bank account monthly.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
Record the journal entries:
1. A company issues a sales invoice of $11,500 including 15% VAT, payable in 30 days.
2. It pays a $24,000 annual insurance premium on 1 January for the calendar year.
3. Month-end entry for insurance at 31 January.
4. It repays $10,000 of a bank loan plus $750 interest.
5. A customer pays a $5,000 advance for goods to be delivered next month.
6. A trial balance balances. Does that mean the accounts are correct? Give two errors it would not detect.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Dr Trade receivables 11,500 / Cr Sales revenue 10,000 / Cr VAT payable 1,500. (VAT collected for the government is a liability, not revenue.)
2. Dr Prepayments 24,000 / Cr Cash 24,000.
3. Dr Insurance expense 2,000 / Cr Prepayments 2,000.
4. Dr Bank loan 10,000 / Dr Interest expense 750 / Cr Cash 10,750.
5. Dr Cash 5,000 / Cr Deferred revenue (contract liability) 5,000. Revenue is recognised next month on delivery.
6. No. It would not detect: an omitted transaction; a posting to the wrong account of the same type (e.g., rent posted to salaries); a compensating error; an entry with the wrong amount on both sides; a reversed entry (debit and credit swapped).
:::

# Accrual Accounting, Revenue and Expense Recognition

:::obj In this chapter
When revenue and expenses are recognised; IFRS 15’s five-step model with commercial examples; principal vs agent; how Incoterms and contract terms affect revenue timing; accruals and cut-off.
:::

## Cash accounting vs accrual accounting

| Event | Cash accounting | Accrual accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Goods delivered in March, customer pays in May | Revenue in May | Revenue in March; receivable until May |
| Annual software licence paid in January | Expense in January | Prepayment, expensed monthly |
| Electricity used in December, billed in January | Expense in January | Accrued expense in December |
| Customer advance received in June for September delivery | Revenue in June | Contract liability until September |
| Machine bought for cash | Expense when paid | Asset; depreciation over useful life |

Cash accounting is simple but misleading about performance; accrual accounting reflects performance but requires judgement and can be manipulated. Professionals use both views together: the income statement (accrual) and the cash flow statement (cash).

## Revenue recognition: IFRS 15 and ASC 606

IFRS 15 *Revenue from Contracts with Customers* and the converged US standard ASC 606 use a five-step model:

| Step | Requirement | Commercial meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify the contract | Enforceable agreement, approved, rights and payment terms identifiable, commercial substance, collection probable | No contract (or no probable collection) → no revenue |
| 2. Identify performance obligations | Distinct goods or services promised | A sale of equipment + installation + 2-year maintenance may be 3 obligations |
| 3. Determine the transaction price | Consideration expected, including variable consideration (rebates, penalties, bonuses) constrained to amounts highly probable not to reverse; significant financing components | Volume rebates and late-delivery penalties reduce revenue |
| 4. Allocate the price | Based on relative standalone selling prices | Discounts spread across obligations |
| 5. Recognise revenue | When (or as) control transfers — at a point in time or over time | Delivery, acceptance, progress of work |

### Point in time vs over time

Revenue is recognised **over time** if one of three criteria is met: the customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefit (e.g., services, cleaning, SaaS); the work creates or enhances an asset the customer controls (e.g., building on the customer’s land); or the asset has no alternative use to the seller and the seller has an enforceable right to payment for work done to date (e.g., some custom manufacturing). Otherwise revenue is recognised at a **point in time** when control passes, considering indicators: right to payment, legal title, physical possession, risks and rewards, customer acceptance.

:::example Bundled contract (fictional)
A supplier sells a cold-storage system for $500,000 including installation and 2 years of maintenance. Standalone selling prices: equipment $440,000; installation $40,000; maintenance $80,000 (total $560,000). The $60,000 discount is allocated proportionally.

| Obligation | SSP | Allocation % | Allocated price | Recognised |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equipment | 440,000 | 78.57% | 392,857 | On delivery (point in time) |
| Installation | 40,000 | 7.14% | 35,714 | When installed (or over the short installation period) |
| Maintenance | 80,000 | 14.29% | 71,429 | Evenly over 24 months: ≈ 2,976 per month |
| Total | 560,000 | 100% | 500,000 | |

If the customer pays $500,000 upfront, the unearned maintenance portion sits in contract liabilities (deferred revenue).
:::

### Incoterms, delivery and revenue timing

For goods sales, the point at which control transfers is often linked to the delivery terms, although Incoterms deal with risk and costs, not ownership, and the full contract must be considered.

| Delivery term | Typical indication of control transfer | Revenue timing (typical) |
|---|---|---|
| EXW | Goods placed at buyer’s disposal at seller’s premises | When made available and collected/at buyer’s disposal |
| FOB / FCA | Delivery at origin (on board / to carrier) | At shipment |
| CIF / CFR / CIP / CPT | Risk passes at origin, seller arranges carriage | Often at shipment; freight/insurance may be a separate service obligation recognised over the voyage under IFRS 15 analysis |
| DAP / DPU / DDP | Delivery at destination | On arrival/delivery at destination |

:::warn Revenue-recognition red flags
- Large sales booked in the last days of a quarter (“channel stuffing”), followed by returns.
- Bill-and-hold arrangements without substantive reasons.
- Revenue recognised for goods shipped on consignment (the distributor has not bought them).
- Gross revenue reported where the company is only an agent.
- Percentage-of-completion estimates that keep rising while cash lags.
:::

## Principal vs agent

If a company controls the goods or services before transfer to the customer, it is a **principal** and reports revenue **gross**; if it arranges for another party to provide them, it is an **agent** and reports only its commission or margin **net**. Indicators of principal status: primary responsibility for fulfilment, inventory risk, discretion in setting price.

:::example Marketplace vs reseller
A platform sells a $1,000 holiday package supplied by a hotel, keeping $150. If the platform only arranges the booking (agent), revenue = **$150**. If it buys room allotments in advance at its own risk and resells them (principal), revenue = **$1,000** and cost of sales = $850. The profit is identical; revenue differs by a factor of almost 7 — another reason to compare companies on gross profit or margins rather than headline revenue.
:::

## Expense recognition and matching

Expenses are recognised when the related economic benefits are consumed: cost of goods sold when the revenue is recognised; depreciation over useful life; salaries in the period worked; marketing generally when incurred (even if benefits are long-term — advertising is not capitalised under IFRS); research costs expensed; development costs capitalised under IAS 38 only when strict criteria are met (US GAAP generally expenses R&D, with specific exceptions such as certain software costs).

### Accruals, prepayments and cut-off

| Situation | Adjustment | Entry |
|---|---|---|
| Expense incurred, invoice not yet received | Accrued expense | Dr Expense / Cr Accrued expenses |
| Expense paid in advance | Prepayment | Dr Prepayments / Cr Cash; then release monthly |
| Revenue earned, not yet invoiced | Accrued income / contract asset | Dr Accrued income / Cr Revenue |
| Cash received before earning | Deferred revenue / contract liability | Dr Cash / Cr Contract liability |

**Cut-off** testing verifies that transactions are recorded in the correct period — auditors examine deliveries and invoices just before and after the year-end.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A company sells a machine for $200,000 on 20 December under DAP terms; it arrives at the customer on 8 January. Year-end is 31 December. When is revenue recognised?
2. A software company sells a 3-year subscription for $36,000 paid upfront on 1 July. Year-end 31 December. Compute revenue for the year and deferred revenue at year-end, splitting current and non-current.
3. A travel agency sells $2m of flights as agent, earning 6% commission. What revenue does it report?
4. A distributor gives a customer a 3% rebate if annual purchases exceed $1m. By September purchases are $900k and the customer is very likely to exceed $1m. How should revenue be measured?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Under DAP the seller delivers at destination; control typically transfers on arrival → revenue in **January** (next year), not December. In December the machine remains in the seller’s inventory (in transit).
2. Monthly revenue = 1,000. Revenue this year (July–December) = **$6,000**. Deferred revenue at 31 Dec = **$30,000**: current (next 12 months) $12,000; non-current $18,000.
3. Net commission: 6% × 2m = **$120,000**.
4. Variable consideration: since the rebate is highly likely, revenue on each sale should be recognised net of the expected 3% rebate (with a refund liability/accrued rebate), not gross with a lump-sum rebate later.
:::
