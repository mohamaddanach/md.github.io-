Part II looks at the firm as a working system. Part I gave you the words; this part shows the machinery those words describe: how an idea becomes a company, how companies decide what to sell and at what price, how the departments depend on each other, and how a decision in one department ripples through inventory, cash, financing and profit.

The part ends with an industry tour — manufacturing, retail, logistics, software, banking, construction, energy and international trading — because each industry has a characteristic way of making and losing money. Recognising these patterns is one of the fastest ways to sound (and think) like an experienced professional.

# The Business Lifecycle: From Idea to Exit

:::obj In this chapter
The stages every business passes through — and the questions, documents, risks and financing that dominate each stage.
:::

## The stages at a glance

| Stage | Central question | Main activities | Typical financing | Typical failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Opportunity | Is there a real problem worth solving? | Observation, research, customer interviews | Founder’s own time and savings | Solving a non-problem |
| 2. Validation | Will customers pay, and enough? | MVP, pilots, pre-sales | Savings, friends and family, grants, angels | Building before validating |
| 3. Launch | Can we deliver reliably? | Legal set-up, first hires, operations, first sales | Angels, seed funds, small bank loans with guarantees | Operational chaos, underpricing |
| 4. Growth | Can we scale without breaking? | Systems, management layers, new markets | VC, bank facilities, trade finance, private equity | Running out of working capital |
| 5. Maturity | How do we defend and extract value? | Efficiency, cost control, adjacent products | Bank debt, bonds, retained earnings | Complacency, disruption |
| 6. Renewal or decline | Reinvent, sell or wind down? | Restructuring, M&A, divestment | Restructuring finance, new investors | Too late to act |
| 7. Exit or closure | How is value realised? | Sale, IPO, management buyout, liquidation | Buyer’s funding / insolvency process | Value destroyed by delay |

:::ar دورة حياة المنشأة
تمر المنشأة عادةً بسبع مراحل: اكتشاف الفرصة، ثم التحقق من وجود طلب حقيقي، ثم الإطلاق، ثم النمو، ثم النضج، ثم التجدد أو التراجع، وأخيراً التخارج أو الإغلاق. لكل مرحلة سؤال محوري ومصادر تمويل مناسبة ومخاطر نموذجية. فالشركة الناشئة تموَّل غالباً من المؤسسين والمستثمرين الملائكة، بينما تعتمد الشركة الناضجة على القروض المصرفية والسندات والأرباح المحتجزة.
:::

## Opportunity identification and market research

An **opportunity** exists when a group of customers has a problem or desire that is not served well, they are willing and able to pay for a better solution, and you can deliver it at a cost below what they will pay, in a way competitors cannot immediately copy.

Sources of opportunities:

- **Pain points observed at work.** Many strong B2B companies start when an employee sees a recurring problem (e.g., manual customs paperwork, slow supplier payments).
- **Changes in the environment.** New regulation (e-invoicing mandates), new technology (APIs, AI), demographic shifts, infrastructure (a new port or free zone), trade-policy changes.
- **Inefficient markets.** Fragmented supply, opaque prices, slow intermediaries.
- **Imitation with adaptation.** Bringing a proven model to a new geography with local adaptation — common and legitimate, but competition follows quickly.

### Market research methods

| Method | What it tells you | Cost | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Desk research (reports, statistics, trade data) | Market size, growth, players, prices | Low | Often outdated or too aggregated |
| Customer interviews (15–30) | Problems, current solutions, decision process, willingness to pay | Low | Small sample; people say what they think you want to hear |
| Surveys | Quantified preferences | Medium | Stated vs actual behaviour |
| Competitor analysis | Prices, positioning, weaknesses | Low | Hard to see their costs |
| Pilot / pre-sale | Real willingness to pay | Medium | Requires a product or credible promise |
| Trade data (import statistics by HS code) | Volumes and origins of goods | Low | Requires interpretation |

### Sizing the market: TAM, SAM, SOM

- **TAM (total addressable market):** total demand for the category if you had 100% share.
- **SAM (serviceable available market):** the part you can realistically serve with your product, channel and geography.
- **SOM (serviceable obtainable market):** the share you can plausibly win in 3–5 years.

:::example Sizing a B2B market (fictional)
A founder plans software that automates customs declarations for freight forwarders in three Gulf countries.
- Licensed forwarders in the three countries: ~2,400 (assumption from trade-directory research).
- Target: forwarders filing more than 100 declarations a month: ~900 (SAM in accounts).
- Expected price: $500 per month → SAM = 900 × $6,000 = **$5.4m per year**.
- Realistic 5-year share: 15% → SOM ≈ 135 customers ≈ **$0.8m ARR**.

Insight: the market is real but small. A venture-capital investor will ask how the business expands (more countries, more products such as payments or trade finance) — otherwise this is a good lifestyle or bootstrapped business, not a VC case.
:::

## Customer identification

A professional describes the customer precisely. In B2B, define the **ideal customer profile (ICP)**: industry, size, geography, technology used, trigger events (new regulation, expansion), and the **buying centre** — the people involved in a purchase.

| Role in the buying centre | Typical title | What they care about |
|---|---|---|
| Economic buyer | CFO, CEO, owner | ROI, budget, risk |
| User | Operations staff | Ease, time saved |
| Technical evaluator | IT, engineering | Integration, security |
| Procurement | Purchasing manager | Price, terms, compliance |
| Champion | Often a manager who feels the pain | Personal success, recognition |
| Blocker | Anyone threatened by change | Status quo |

In B2C, define segments by needs and behaviour, not only demographics. “Women 25–40” is a demographic; “working parents who order groceries online weekly and value delivery slots” is a usable segment.

## From launch to scale: what changes

| Dimension | Launch | Growth | Maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder role | Does everything | Hires managers, sets direction | Governs, allocates capital |
| Decisions | Fast, intuitive | Need data and processes | Formal approvals (DoA) |
| Systems | Spreadsheets, basic accounting | ERP, CRM, BI | Integrated systems, automation |
| Finance | Cash survival | Working-capital funding, controls | Capital structure, dividends |
| Risk | Existential | Operational and credit | Strategic and disruption |
| Culture | Informal | Strained by new hires | Bureaucracy risk |

:::tip What experienced founders know
The most dangerous period is often not the start but the transition to growth. Volumes rise faster than systems, controls and working-capital funding. Typical symptoms: invoices not issued on time, stock counts that do not match the system, customers paying late because nobody chases, and the bank overdraft maxed out despite record sales.
:::

## Exit or closure

Owners realise value through: trade sale (to a strategic buyer), sale to private equity, management buyout (MBO), initial public offering (IPO), dividends over time, or orderly closure. Closure can be **solvent** (members’ voluntary liquidation: assets cover all debts) or **insolvent** (creditors’ processes under insolvency law). Chapter 19 and Part XII cover restructuring and insolvency.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. For a planned business importing solar water pumps to farmers in a North African country, list five research questions and the method you would use for each.
2. A founder estimates TAM as “the $40bn agricultural equipment market”. Critique this and propose a better approach.
3. Identify the buying centre for a hospital purchasing a new inventory-management system.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Examples: (a) How many farms use diesel pumps, and of what size? — government agricultural census, interviews with dealers. (b) Diesel cost per farm per year? — farmer interviews, fuel price data. (c) Willingness and ability to pay; need for financing? — interviews, microfinance institutions. (d) Import duties, standards and subsidies? — customs tariff, ministry regulations, interviews with customs brokers. (e) Competing suppliers and prices? — dealer visits, online catalogues, trade data by HS code.
2. That number is irrelevant: it includes tractors and harvesters in all countries. Build bottom-up: number of target farms × share likely to adopt × average spend. Then SAM by reachable regions and channels; SOM by realistic share.
3. Economic buyer: hospital director/CFO; users: pharmacy and stores staff, nurses; technical evaluator: IT and information security; procurement: purchasing department; clinical stakeholders (patient-safety lead); possible blockers: staff comfortable with the old system, IT if integration load is heavy.
:::

# Business Models, Value Propositions, Revenue Models and Pricing

:::obj In this chapter
How businesses decide what to offer, to whom, how to charge, and at what price — and how these choices determine margins, cash flow and risk.
:::

## The Business Model Canvas

The Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder and Pigneur) is a one-page description of a business in nine blocks. It is widely used in startups and corporate innovation.

| Block | Question | Example: fictional B2B spare-parts distributor |
|---|---|---|
| Customer segments | Who do we serve? | Oil-field service contractors; industrial plants |
| Value proposition | What problem do we solve? | Genuine parts within 24 hours; technical advice |
| Channels | How do we reach and deliver? | Key account managers; e-catalogue; own vans |
| Customer relationships | What relationship do we build? | Dedicated account manager; framework agreements |
| Revenue streams | How do we earn money? | Margin on parts; emergency delivery fee; service contracts |
| Key resources | What assets are essential? | Inventory; exclusive brand agreements; warehouse; technical staff |
| Key activities | What must we do well? | Procurement, inventory planning, fast logistics |
| Key partners | Who helps us? | Manufacturers (principals), freight forwarders, banks |
| Cost structure | What are the main costs? | Inventory carrying cost, salaries, warehouse, freight |

## Value propositions that win

A strong value proposition is specific, quantified and relevant to the buyer’s economics. Compare:

- Weak: “We provide high-quality logistics solutions.”
- Strong: “We clear your containers within 48 hours of arrival or we pay the demurrage — saving you on average $1,100 per container.”

Common sources of B2B value: lower total cost (not just lower price), reduced risk, faster delivery, reliability, compliance, cash-flow benefits (credit terms, consignment stock), revenue growth for the customer, convenience and integration.

:::tip Total cost, not price
Experienced buyers compare **total cost of ownership**: price + freight + duties + inventory holding + failures + downtime + administration. A seller with a higher price but lower total cost can win if it proves the numbers. This is how premium suppliers compete against cheap imports.
:::

## Revenue models

| Revenue model | How money is earned | Examples | Cash-flow profile | Key metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product sale (transaction) | Price × units | Distributors, manufacturers | Depends on credit terms | Gross margin, volume |
| Subscription | Recurring fee per period | SaaS, maintenance contracts | Predictable, often prepaid | ARR, churn |
| Usage-based | Fee per unit of use | Cloud computing, payments, telecoms | Variable, recurring | Volume, take rate |
| Commission / take rate | % of transaction value | Marketplaces, brokers, agents | Asset-light | GMV × take rate |
| Licensing / royalty | Fee for using IP | Brands, patents, franchises | High margin | Licensee sales |
| Spread / interest | Difference between buy and sell rates | Banks, traders, FX dealers | Balance-sheet heavy | Net interest margin, spread |
| Advertising | Payment for audience attention | Media, platforms | Volume-driven | Users, CPM |
| Project / contract | Milestone payments | Construction, consulting | Lumpy; advance payments critical | Backlog, margin |
| Freemium | Free base, paid premium | Software, apps | Conversion-dependent | Free-to-paid conversion |
| Razor and blades | Cheap base product, profitable consumables | Printers, coffee machines | Long-tail recurring | Installed base |

## Pricing strategies

Pricing is the most powerful profit lever. For a business with a 10% operating margin, a 1% price increase with no volume loss raises operating profit by about 10%.

| Strategy | Logic | When it works | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost-plus | Cost × (1 + markup) | Commodity-like goods, government contracts | Ignores customer value and competition |
| Competitive (market) pricing | Match or position against competitors | Transparent markets | Price wars |
| Value-based | Price based on value to customer | Differentiated B2B products | Requires quantified value |
| Penetration | Low price to win share | Network effects, scale economies | May never raise prices |
| Skimming | High price at launch, then lower | Innovative products | Invites competitors |
| Tiered / good-better-best | Versions at different prices | Software, services | Complexity |
| Dynamic | Prices change with demand | Airlines, hotels, freight | Customer resentment |
| Bundling | Package priced below sum | Services + products | Hides product profitability |
| Price discrimination | Different prices for segments | Students, regions, volume tiers | Legal limits; arbitrage |

:::example Price increase vs volume loss
A product sells 10,000 units at $100; variable cost $70; fixed costs $200,000.
- Current profit = 10,000 × (100 − 70) − 200,000 = **$100,000**.
- Increase price 5% to $105: contribution per unit rises to $35. Break-even volume to keep the same profit: (100,000 + 200,000) ÷ 35 = **8,571 units** — the business can lose up to 14.3% of volume and still be as profitable.
- Cut price 5% to $95: contribution falls to $25. Required volume = 300,000 ÷ 25 = **12,000 units** (+20%).

Rule: with thin contribution margins, discounts require very large volume increases to pay off.
:::

:::formula Volume change needed to offset a price change
Required volume change % = −ΔP ÷ (CM + ΔP)

where ΔP = price change as % of price, CM = contribution margin % before the change.

Example: CM = 30%, ΔP = −5% → −(−5) ÷ (30 − 5) = +20% volume needed.
:::

## Discounts, terms and the hidden price

The **pocket price** is what the seller actually keeps after all deductions: invoice discounts, volume rebates, free freight, extended payment terms (financing cost), marketing allowances, returns. In many B2B businesses the pocket price is 10–20% below list price, and different customers have very different pocket prices without management noticing.

:::example Pocket price waterfall (fictional)
| Item | $/unit |
|---|---|
| List price | 100.00 |
| Invoice discount 8% | (8.00) |
| Volume rebate 3% | (2.76) |
| Free delivery | (2.10) |
| Payment terms 90 days vs standard 30 (60 extra days at 9% p.a. on $89.24) | (1.32) |
| Promotional allowance | (1.50) |
| **Pocket price** | **84.32** |
:::

## Cost structures

| Cost type | Definition | Examples | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Variable | Change with volume | Materials, freight, commissions | Determine contribution margin |
| Fixed | Do not change with volume in the short term | Rent, salaries, depreciation, insurance | Create operating leverage |
| Semi-variable | Fixed base + variable element | Utilities, overtime | Split for analysis |
| Step | Fixed within a range, then jump | Adding a shift, a second warehouse | Capacity planning |
| Direct | Traceable to a product | Raw materials, direct labour | Product costing |
| Indirect | Shared across products | Management, IT | Allocation choices matter |
| Sunk | Already spent, unrecoverable | Past R&D | Ignore in decisions |
| Opportunity | Value of best alternative forgone | Using warehouse space for product A instead of B | Include in decisions |

**Operating leverage** measures how much operating profit changes for a change in revenue. Businesses with high fixed costs (airlines, software, telecoms, shipping) see profits swing sharply with volume.

:::formula Degree of operating leverage (DOL)
DOL = Contribution margin ÷ Operating profit = %Δ Operating profit ÷ %Δ Revenue

Example: contribution $3m, operating profit $1m → DOL = 3. A 10% revenue fall reduces operating profit by about 30%.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Describe the revenue model and the key metric for: (a) a container shipping line; (b) a B2B payments platform; (c) a construction contractor; (d) a franchise brand owner.
2. A distributor has a 22% contribution margin. A customer asks for an 8% discount, promising more volume. How much more volume is needed to keep contribution unchanged?
3. A company’s list price is $50. Typical deductions: 10% invoice discount, 2% rebate on net invoice, free shipping costing $1.20, 120-day terms instead of 30 (cost of capital 10%). Compute the pocket price.
4. Company X has fixed costs of $6m and variable costs of 40% of revenue; revenue is $12m. Compute operating profit and DOL. What happens to profit if revenue falls 15%?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (a) Freight per container/TEU (spot and contract); metric: rate per TEU, utilisation, unit cost. (b) Take rate on payment volume, sometimes subscription; metric: total payment volume × take rate, transaction margin. (c) Project/contract revenue recognised over time; metrics: backlog, gross margin per project, cash (advance payments vs work-in-progress). (d) Royalties (% of franchisee sales) + initial fees; metric: system-wide sales, number of units, royalty rate.
2. Required volume change = 8 ÷ (22 − 8) = **57.1%**. Very unlikely — the discount should be refused or exchanged for other value (longer contract, prepayment, exclusivity).
3. After 10%: 45.00. Rebate 2% of 45: −0.90 → 44.10. Shipping −1.20 → 42.90. Extra 90 days financing: 42.90 × 10% × 90/365 = 1.06 → pocket price ≈ **$41.84** (16.3% below list).
4. Contribution = 60% × 12 = $7.2m; operating profit = 7.2 − 6 = **$1.2m**; DOL = 7.2 ÷ 1.2 = **6**. Revenue −15% → contribution falls to 6.12 → profit **$0.12m** (−90%, consistent with DOL 6 × 15%).
:::

# Cost Structures, Operations and the Functional Departments

:::obj In this chapter

## The value chain of a typical company

Michael Porter’s value chain divides activities into **primary activities** (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service) and **support activities** (firm infrastructure — finance, legal, management; human resources; technology; procurement). Profit is the difference between the value customers pay for and the total cost of these activities.

## The departments

| Department | Core responsibilities | Key metrics | Key documents / systems | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | Win and grow customers; negotiate prices and terms | Revenue, margin, win rate, pipeline | Quotations, contracts, CRM | Operations (delivery), finance (credit approval), marketing (leads) |
| Marketing | Demand generation, brand, pricing research | Leads, CAC, brand awareness | Campaign plans, analytics | Sales feedback, product |
| Procurement | Source suppliers, negotiate, buy | Purchase price variance, supplier OTIF, savings | RFQs, POs, supplier contracts | Demand forecasts (sales), specs (operations), payment terms (finance) |
| Operations / production | Make or deliver the product or service | Unit cost, capacity utilisation, quality, OTIF | Production plans, work orders | Materials (procurement), forecasts (sales) |
| Logistics / supply chain | Move and store goods | Lead time, inventory turnover, freight cost, fill rate | Shipping documents, WMS/TMS | Forwarders, customs, procurement |
| Customer service | Support, complaints, returns | Response time, NPS, resolution rate | Tickets, CRM | Operations, sales |
| Finance & accounting | Record, report, plan, fund, control | Cash, profit, DSO, budget variance | Ledgers, statements, budgets, ERP | Everyone’s data |
| Treasury | Liquidity, banking, FX, funding | Cash position, funding cost | Cash forecasts, facility agreements | Finance, sales/procurement (timing) |
| Credit control | Customer credit limits and collections | DSO, overdue %, bad debt | Credit applications, ageing reports | Sales (customers), finance |
| Human resources | Hire, pay, develop, retain people | Turnover, time to hire, engagement | Contracts, payroll, policies | Budgets (finance), managers |
| Legal & compliance | Contracts, disputes, regulatory compliance | Disputes, compliance incidents | Contract templates, policies | Sales, procurement, management |
| IT | Systems, data, security | Uptime, incidents, project delivery | Architecture, SLAs | Business requirements |
| Risk / internal audit | Identify risks; independent assurance | Audit findings, risk indicators | Risk registers, audit reports | All departments |

كل إدارة في الشركة تعتمد على غيرها: المبيعات تحتاج من العمليات أن تسلّم في الموعد، ومن المالية أن توافق على الحد الائتماني للعميل. والمشتريات تحتاج إلى توقعات المبيعات لتحديد الكميات، وإلى المالية لتحديد شروط الدفع. والمالية تعتمد على بيانات جميع الإدارات لإعداد التقارير والموازنات. فهم هذه العلاقات هو مفتاح فهم كيف تعمل الشركة فعلياً.

## The order-to-cash and procure-to-pay cycles

Two end-to-end processes link most departments and drive working capital.

### Order-to-cash (O2C)

1. Customer enquiry → quotation (sales).
2. Credit check and credit limit (credit control).
3. Order acceptance; sales order created in ERP.
4. Stock allocation or production order (operations).
5. Picking, packing, shipping; delivery note / proof of delivery (logistics).
6. Invoice issued (finance) — revenue recognised when control transfers.
7. Collection: reminders, dunning, cash application (credit control, treasury).
8. Reconciliation: payment matched to invoice; disputes resolved.

### Procure-to-pay (P2P)

1. Need identified → purchase requisition.
2. Sourcing: RFQ/RFP, supplier selection (procurement).
3. Purchase order approved under the DoA.
4. Goods received / service confirmed: goods receipt note (GRN).
5. Supplier invoice received.
6. **Three-way match**: PO vs GRN vs invoice (quantity, price).
7. Payment scheduled per terms; payment executed (treasury) with approvals.
8. Accounting entries; supplier statement reconciliation.

:::warn Common control failures in these cycles
- Invoices issued late or not at all after delivery (revenue leakage, longer DSO).
- Orders accepted for customers over their credit limit by sales under pressure.
- Payments made without three-way match — duplicate or fraudulent invoices paid.
- Supplier bank details changed on the strength of an email.
- Goods released before payment where terms required prepayment.
:::

## Operations: capacity, utilisation and quality

Operations converts inputs to outputs. The key concepts:

- **Capacity:** maximum output in a period. **Utilisation** = actual ÷ capacity. High utilisation lowers unit cost but increases waiting times and the risk of failure; most service operations become unstable above ~85–90% utilisation.
- **Throughput:** output per unit time; limited by the **bottleneck** (Theory of Constraints).
- **Quality:** cost of quality = prevention + appraisal + internal failure + external failure costs. Preventing defects is cheaper than fixing returns.
- **Make or buy:** produce internally or outsource, considering cost, control, quality, capacity and strategic importance.

:::example Unit cost and utilisation (fictional bottling plant)
Fixed cost $1.2m per month; variable cost $0.10 per bottle; capacity 20m bottles/month.
- At 60% utilisation (12m bottles): unit cost = 0.10 + 1.2 ÷ 12 = **$0.200**.
- At 90% (18m): unit cost = 0.10 + 1.2 ÷ 18 = **$0.167** (−17%).
A competitor running at 90% can undercut by 15% and still earn the same margin — this is why capacity utilisation drives pricing in manufacturing, shipping and airlines.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Map the departments involved when a customer requests 60-day payment terms on a large order.
2. Explain the three-way match and the fraud it prevents.
3. A logistics warehouse runs at 95% utilisation. The CEO wants to accept a new customer that would add 10% volume. What would you advise?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Sales (negotiates), credit control (assesses limit and risk), finance/treasury (cost of funding the receivable, facility headroom), operations/logistics (capacity and delivery), legal (contract terms), possibly management (DoA approval for non-standard terms), and possibly credit insurance or the bank (receivables finance).
2. Matching PO (what was ordered, at what price), GRN (what was received) and invoice (what is billed) before payment. It prevents paying for goods not ordered or not received, duplicate invoices, price inflation and fictitious suppliers (when combined with vendor master controls).
3. 105% utilisation is impossible without extra capacity; even approaching 100% causes delays and errors for all customers. Options: add shifts or temporary space (step cost), price the new business to cover the incremental capacity, phase onboarding, or decline. Calculate the incremental contribution versus the cost of extra capacity and the risk to existing clients.
:::
