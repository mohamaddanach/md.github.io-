# Assets and Liabilities in Detail

:::obj In this chapter
The accounting for the balance-sheet items business people deal with most: inventory, receivables and payables, property and equipment, intangible assets and goodwill, impairment, provisions and contingencies, leases, and deferred tax — each with the commercial consequences.
:::

## Inventory (IAS 2)

**Cost** of inventory includes purchase price, import duties and non-recoverable taxes, transport and handling, and other costs directly attributable to bringing goods to their present location and condition; trade discounts and rebates are deducted. For manufacturers, cost includes conversion costs (direct labour and a systematic allocation of production overheads based on normal capacity). Excluded: abnormal waste, storage (unless necessary in production), administrative overheads, selling costs.

**Cost formulas:** IFRS permits FIFO and weighted average (and specific identification for non-interchangeable items). LIFO is prohibited under IFRS but permitted under US GAAP.

**Measurement:** lower of cost and **net realisable value (NRV)** = estimated selling price − costs to complete − costs to sell. Write-downs to NRV are expensed; IFRS allows reversal if NRV recovers (US GAAP does not reverse write-downs for most inventory).

:::example FIFO vs weighted average (fictional)
| Date | Event | Units | Unit cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Mar | Opening stock | 1,000 | $10.00 |
| 10 Mar | Purchase | 2,000 | $11.50 |
| 20 Mar | Sale | 2,200 | — |

**FIFO:** COGS = 1,000 × 10.00 + 1,200 × 11.50 = **$23,800**; closing stock 800 × 11.50 = **$9,200**.

**Weighted average:** average cost = (10,000 + 23,000) ÷ 3,000 = $11.00; COGS = 2,200 × 11.00 = **$24,200**; closing stock 800 × 11.00 = **$8,800**.

With rising prices, FIFO gives lower COGS and higher profit (and closing stock closer to current cost). The cash flows are identical; only the allocation between periods changes (and possibly tax).
:::

:::example NRV write-down
800 units carried at $11.50 ($9,200). A competitor’s launch drops the selling price to $12.00; selling costs are $1.20 per unit → NRV = $10.80. Write-down = 800 × 0.70 = **$560**: Dr Cost of sales (inventory write-down) 560 / Cr Inventory 560.
:::

**Commercial point:** slow-moving and obsolete stock is one of the most common sources of overstated assets in trading companies. Lenders and buyers ask for an **inventory ageing** and the policy for provisions on old stock (e.g., 50% for items older than 12 months, 100% beyond 24).

## Trade receivables and expected credit losses

Receivables are measured at amortised cost less a **loss allowance**. Under IFRS 9, trade receivables without a significant financing component use the **simplified approach**: lifetime expected credit losses (ECL), often estimated with a **provision matrix** based on historical loss rates adjusted for forward-looking information.

:::example Provision matrix (fictional distributor)
| Ageing bucket | Balance ($) | Expected loss rate | Allowance ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Not yet due | 1,400,000 | 0.5% | 7,000 |
| 1–30 days overdue | 520,000 | 2% | 10,400 |
| 31–60 days | 260,000 | 6% | 15,600 |
| 61–90 days | 140,000 | 15% | 21,000 |
| Over 90 days | 180,000 | 45% | 81,000 |
| **Total** | **2,500,000** | | **135,000** |

If the opening allowance was $95,000, the period charge is $40,000: Dr Impairment loss on receivables 40,000 / Cr Loss allowance 40,000. When a specific debt is confirmed uncollectable ($22,000): Dr Loss allowance 22,000 / Cr Trade receivables 22,000 (no further P&L effect).
:::

## Trade payables, accruals and supplier finance

Payables are generally measured at the invoice amount. Watch for:

- **Accruals** for goods received not invoiced (GRNI) and services consumed.
- **Supplier finance arrangements** (reverse factoring): a bank pays suppliers early and the buyer pays the bank later. IFRS and US GAAP now require specific disclosures because such arrangements can make debt-like funding look like ordinary payables; analysts may reclassify them as debt.
- **Customer advances** are contract liabilities, not payables.

## Property, plant and equipment (IAS 16)

**Recognition:** an item is capitalised if it is probable that future economic benefits will flow and cost can be measured reliably. Cost includes purchase price, duties, directly attributable costs (delivery, installation, professional fees, testing) and estimated dismantling/restoration costs. Day-to-day servicing is expensed.

**Depreciation methods:**

:::formula Depreciation
Straight-line: annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life

Declining balance: depreciation = Rate × Opening net book value (rate e.g. 2 ÷ useful life for double-declining)

Units of production: depreciation = (Cost − Residual) × Units this period ÷ Total expected units
:::

:::example Three methods for a $100,000 truck (residual $10,000, 5 years or 500,000 km)
| Year | Straight-line | Double-declining (40%) | Units (km driven) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18,000 | 40,000 | 120,000 km → 21,600 |
| 2 | 18,000 | 24,000 | 110,000 km → 19,800 |
| 3 | 18,000 | 14,400 | 100,000 km → 18,000 |
| 4 | 18,000 | 8,640 | 90,000 km → 16,200 |
| 5 | 18,000 | 2,960 (to reach residual) | 80,000 km → 14,400 |
| Total | 90,000 | 90,000 | 90,000 |

Same total; different timing. Management’s choice of useful life and method changes reported profit — another place analysts look for aggressive accounting (e.g., suddenly extending useful lives).
:::

**Component accounting:** significant parts with different lives are depreciated separately (aircraft engines vs airframe; ship dry-docking costs).

**Disposal:** gain or loss = proceeds − net book value. Example: truck with NBV $46,000 sold for $50,000 → gain $4,000 (Dr Cash 50,000; Dr Accumulated depreciation 54,000; Cr Truck at cost 100,000; Cr Gain on disposal 4,000).

**Revaluation model:** IFRS permits revaluing classes of PP&E to fair value (common for land and buildings in some regions), with gains to a revaluation reserve in equity. US GAAP does not permit upward revaluation. Lenders often exclude revaluation reserves when computing tangible net worth for covenants.

## Intangible assets and goodwill (IAS 38, IFRS 3)

Intangibles: software, licences, patents, trademarks, customer relationships (when acquired), development costs (when IAS 38 criteria are met: technical feasibility, intention and ability to complete and use or sell, probable future benefits, resources available, reliable measurement). Research is always expensed. Internally generated brands, customer lists and similar items are not recognised.

Finite-life intangibles are amortised; indefinite-life intangibles and **goodwill** are not amortised under IFRS but tested for impairment at least annually.

## Impairment (IAS 36)

An asset (or cash-generating unit, CGU) is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds its **recoverable amount** = higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use (present value of future cash flows). Impairment losses are expensed; goodwill impairments can never be reversed; other impairments may be reversed under IFRS (not under US GAAP for assets held and used).

**Commercial meaning:** large impairments tell you past investment decisions have failed. They are non-cash in the year recognised, but the cash was lost earlier when the asset was bought.

## Provisions, contingent liabilities and contingent assets (IAS 37)

:::def Recognition test for a provision
Recognise a provision when (1) there is a present obligation (legal or constructive) from a past event, (2) an outflow of resources is probable (more likely than not), and (3) a reliable estimate can be made. Otherwise, disclose a **contingent liability** unless the possibility of outflow is remote. **Contingent assets** (e.g., a probable insurance recovery) are not recognised until virtually certain; they are disclosed when an inflow is probable.
:::

| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Warranties on products sold (historical claims 1.5% of sales) | Provision = expected cost of claims |
| Lawsuit: lawyers estimate 70% probability of losing $400k | Provision (best estimate, ~$400k) |
| Lawsuit: 30% probability of losing | Contingent liability — disclose |
| Lawsuit: remote probability | No disclosure required |
| Restructuring announced with a detailed formal plan | Provision for direct costs |
| Future operating losses | No provision |
| Bank guarantee issued for a subsidiary | Contingent liability (or financial guarantee accounting) |
| Onerous contract (unavoidable costs exceed benefits) | Provision for the unavoidable loss |
| End-of-service benefits for employees (common in GCC labour laws) | Employee benefit obligation (IAS 19), not IAS 37 |

## Leases (IFRS 16)

For lessees, IFRS 16 (effective 2019) brings almost all leases onto the balance sheet: a **right-of-use (ROU) asset** and a **lease liability** equal to the present value of lease payments. Exemptions: short-term leases (≤ 12 months) and low-value assets. The lessee then recognises depreciation of the ROU asset and interest on the liability instead of a rent expense. US GAAP (ASC 842) also puts leases on the balance sheet but keeps a straight-line single lease cost for operating leases in the income statement.

:::example Warehouse lease under IFRS 16
5-year lease, $100,000 paid at the end of each year, incremental borrowing rate 7%.
- Lease liability at start = 100,000 × annuity factor (5 years, 7%) = 100,000 × 4.1002 = **$410,020**; ROU asset the same.
- Year 1: interest = 7% × 410,020 = **$28,701**; depreciation = 410,020 ÷ 5 = **$82,004**; total expense $110,705 (vs $100,000 rent previously); liability after payment = 410,020 + 28,701 − 100,000 = $338,721.
- **EBITDA rises by $100,000** (rent removed; depreciation and interest are below EBITDA), net debt rises by $410,020, and operating cash flow rises because principal repayments are financing cash flows.

That is why bank covenants and analysts specify whether figures are “pre-IFRS 16” or “post-IFRS 16”.
:::

## Deferred tax (IAS 12) — the essentials

Accounting profit and taxable profit differ. **Temporary differences** (e.g., tax depreciation faster than accounting depreciation) create deferred tax liabilities or assets that reverse later. Deferred tax assets from tax losses are recognised only if future taxable profits are probable. Business implication: a company may show a tax expense but pay little cash tax (or vice versa); analysts look at **cash tax paid** in the cash flow statement.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A company bought 500 units at $20 and later 500 at $26, then sold 600. Compute COGS under FIFO and weighted average.
2. A $60,000 machine, residual $6,000, life 6 years, is sold at the end of year 3 for $28,000 (straight-line). Compute the gain or loss.
3. Classify: (a) a probable $2m environmental clean-up obligation for contamination caused last year; (b) a possible patent infringement claim; (c) expected losses next year from a weak product line.
4. A retailer signs a 10-year store lease at $200,000 per year (paid in arrears) with an incremental borrowing rate of 8%. Compute the initial lease liability (annuity factor 6.7101) and the year-1 interest and depreciation. How does EBITDA change versus the old treatment?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. FIFO: 500 × 20 + 100 × 26 = **$12,600**. Weighted average: (10,000 + 13,000) ÷ 1,000 = 23 → 600 × 23 = **$13,800**.
2. Annual depreciation = (60,000 − 6,000) ÷ 6 = 9,000; NBV after 3 years = 60,000 − 27,000 = 33,000; loss = 28,000 − 33,000 = **−$5,000**.
3. (a) Provision (present obligation, probable, estimable). (b) Contingent liability — disclose if not remote. (c) No provision — future operating losses are not provided for (unless an onerous contract exists).
4. Liability = 200,000 × 6.7101 = **$1,342,020**. Year-1 interest = 8% × 1,342,020 = **$107,362**; depreciation = 1,342,020 ÷ 10 = **$134,202**. EBITDA **increases by $200,000** because rent is no longer an operating expense.
:::

# The Three Financial Statements and a Complete Worked Example

:::obj In this chapter
The income statement, balance sheet (statement of financial position) and cash flow statement in depth; how they link; how single transactions move all three; and a complete worked example from opening balances through journal entries to final statements.
:::

## The income statement (statement of profit or loss)

Reports performance over a period. IFRS allows presentation of expenses **by function** (cost of sales, distribution, administrative) or **by nature** (materials, staff costs, depreciation). IFRS 18 *Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements*, issued in 2024 and effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, will replace IAS 1 and introduce defined subtotals (operating profit, profit before financing and income taxes) and disclosure of management-defined performance measures; verify adoption status for any company you analyse.

| Line | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Growth, segment and geographic split, recognition policy |
| Cost of sales / gross profit | Gross margin trend; input-cost pressures |
| Distribution and administrative expenses | Operating leverage; one-offs |
| Other operating income/expense | Gains on disposals, FX, impairments — sustainable? |
| Operating profit | Core profitability |
| Finance income/costs | Debt burden, interest rate exposure, FX on borrowings |
| Share of profit of associates | Non-controlled investments |
| Income tax | Effective tax rate vs statutory |
| Profit for the year; attributable to owners vs NCI | What belongs to the parent’s shareholders |
| Other comprehensive income | Revaluations, FX translation, hedge reserves — not in profit |

## The balance sheet (statement of financial position)

A snapshot at a date: what the entity controls (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the residual (equity), split into current and non-current.

| Section | Typical lines | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Non-current assets | PP&E, ROU assets, intangibles, goodwill, investments, deferred tax assets | Age of assets? Goodwill size relative to equity? |
| Current assets | Inventory, trade receivables, prepayments, contract assets, cash | Quality and ageing; restricted cash? |
| Current liabilities | Trade payables, accruals, short-term borrowings, current portion of long-term debt, lease liabilities, contract liabilities, tax | Refinancing needs within 12 months? |
| Non-current liabilities | Long-term borrowings, lease liabilities, provisions, employee benefits, deferred tax | Maturity profile; covenants |
| Equity | Share capital, reserves, retained earnings, NCI | Tangible net worth after goodwill |

## The cash flow statement

Reports cash movements in three categories (IAS 7):

- **Operating activities:** cash from trading. Usually presented with the **indirect method**: start from profit, add back non-cash items, adjust for working-capital changes. (The direct method shows receipts and payments; encouraged by IAS 7 but less common.)
- **Investing activities:** capex, acquisitions, disposals, investments.
- **Financing activities:** borrowings and repayments, share issues, dividends, lease principal.

Interest and dividends paid/received may be classified in operating or financing/investing under current IAS 7 (choice applied consistently); US GAAP generally puts interest paid in operating. IFRS 18 introduces consequential amendments that reduce these choices for most companies from 2027.

## How the statements link

1. **Net profit** from the income statement flows into **retained earnings** (balance sheet) and is the starting point of the indirect cash flow statement.
2. **Changes in balance-sheet items** (receivables, inventory, payables, fixed assets, debt, equity) explain the cash flows.
3. **Ending cash** in the cash flow statement equals cash on the balance sheet.
4. **Depreciation** reduces PP&E (balance sheet), is an expense (income statement) and is added back (cash flow).

## One transaction, three statements

Assume a 25% tax rate where relevant and that tax is paid in cash at once (to keep the logic visible).

| Transaction | Income statement | Balance sheet | Cash flow statement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sell goods on credit for $100 (cost $60) | Revenue +100, COGS −60, tax −10 → NI +30 | Receivables +100, inventory −60, cash −10 (tax), RE +30 | CFO: NI +30, Δreceivables −100, Δinventory +60 → −10 |
| Customer pays the $100 | No effect | Cash +100, receivables −100 | CFO +100 (receivables decrease) |
| Buy equipment for $1,000 cash | No immediate effect | PP&E +1,000, cash −1,000 | CFI −1,000 |
| Depreciation of $200 | Expense −200, tax saving +50 → NI −150 | PP&E −200, cash +50, RE −150 | CFO: NI −150 + D&A 200 = +50 |
| Borrow $500 | No effect | Cash +500, debt +500 | CFF +500 |
| Pay interest of $40 | Expense −40, tax +10 → NI −30 | Cash −30, RE −30 | CFO −30 (if interest in operating) |
| Repay $100 of loan principal | No effect | Cash −100, debt −100 | CFF −100 |
| Inventory write-down of $30 | Expense −30, tax +7.5 → NI −22.5 | Inventory −30, cash +7.5, RE −22.5 | CFO: NI −22.5 + non-cash 30 = +7.5 |
| Pay dividend of $50 | No effect (not an expense) | Cash −50, RE −50 | CFF −50 |
| Issue shares for $300 | No effect | Cash +300, share capital +300 | CFF +300 |
| Prepay a year’s insurance $120 | No effect | Prepayments +120, cash −120 | CFO −120 (Δprepayments) |

:::tip The classic interview question
“Depreciation increases by $10. Walk me through the three statements” (25% tax). Income statement: pre-tax profit −10, tax −2.5, net income −7.5. Cash flow: net income −7.5, add back depreciation +10 → cash +2.5 (lower tax paid). Balance sheet: PP&E −10, cash +2.5 → assets −7.5; retained earnings −7.5 → balanced. A finance graduate who can do this for any item in seconds has internalised accounting.
:::

Levant Office Solutions distributes office furniture and equipment. All amounts in USD. Financial year 1 January – 31 December.

### Interpretation

- **Profit to cash conversion:** operating cash flow 168k vs net income 153k — healthy, helped by unpaid tax (27k) that will be paid next year. Excluding that timing benefit, conversion is 141k ÷ 153k ≈ 92%.
- **Working capital** absorbed 46k (receivables +30k, inventory +30k, prepayments +4k, payables −20k, accruals +2k), consistent with growth.
- **Free cash flow** (CFO − capex) = 88k; it covered dividends (20k) and debt repayment (30k), and cash rose 38k.
- **Ratios:** current ratio = (88 + 110 + 150 + 4) ÷ (90 + 8 + 27 + current portion of loan, say 30) = 352 ÷ 155 = 2.3×; net debt = 120 − 88 = 32k; net debt/EBITDA ≈ 0.14×; ROE = 153 ÷ average equity ((260 + 393) ÷ 2 = 326.5) ≈ 47%.
- **Questions an analyst would ask:** Is the 39% gross margin sustainable? Is the inventory count reliable? What are receivables’ ageing and concentration? Was the tax computation correct (any non-deductible expenses)?

## Accounting profit vs cash generated: a summary

| Reason profit ≠ cash | Direction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Non-cash expenses (depreciation, amortisation, impairments, provisions) | Cash > profit | Depreciation 34k |
| Working-capital investment | Cash < profit when receivables/inventory rise | +30k receivables |
| Capex | Cash < profit (capex not expensed) | 80k equipment |
| Timing of tax payments | Either | Tax payable 27k |
| Debt principal flows | Do not affect profit | 30k repayment |
| Dividends | Do not affect profit | 20k |
| Non-cash revenue (e.g., fair-value gains) | Profit > cash | Revaluation gains |
| Customer prepayments | Cash > profit | Subscriptions |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Using the Levant Office Solutions example, suppose credit customers had paid only $1,100,000 (instead of $1,170,000). What changes in each statement?
2. Walk through the three statements for: a $50,000 inventory purchase on credit; then the sale of half of it for $40,000 cash (tax 15%, paid immediately).
3. A company reports net income of $5m and CFO of $1m. List the balance-sheet lines you would examine first.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Income statement: no change (revenue already recognised). Balance sheet: receivables $180,000 (+70k), cash $18,000 (−70k). Cash flow: increase in receivables (100,000) instead of (30,000); CFO $98,000; net increase in cash −$32,000 (from 50k to 18k). The company would need to use its overdraft — profit unchanged, cash much weaker.
2. Purchase: inventory +50k, payables +50k; no IS or cash effect (CFO: Δinventory −50k, Δpayables +50k = 0). Sale: revenue +40k, COGS −25k, pre-tax +15k, tax −2.25k → NI +12.75k; cash +40k − 2.25k = +37.75k; inventory −25k; RE +12.75k. Cash flow: NI 12.75 + Δinventory +25 = 37.75 ✓.
3. Trade receivables and contract assets (rising? overdue?), inventory (build-up, obsolescence), payables (lower — paying suppliers faster?), prepayments and advances to suppliers, capitalised costs and other receivables, and non-cash gains in profit (fair-value gains, disposals).
:::
