# How Departments Interact — and Why Profitable Businesses Fail

:::obj In this chapter
A single commercial decision traced through every department and all three financial statements; the mechanics of the working-capital trap; and the recurring reasons profitable or high-revenue companies get into financial distress.
:::

## The ripple effect of one sales decision

Gulf Kitchen Supplies LLC (fictional) distributes commercial kitchen equipment. The sales director wins a contract with a hotel group: **$6m of extra annual sales**, at a 10% price discount compared with list, with **120-day payment terms** instead of the standard 45. The board must decide whether to accept.

### Baseline (before the contract)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual revenue | $20m |
| Gross margin | 28% |
| Operating expenses (mostly fixed) | $3.6m |
| Operating profit | $2.0m |
| DSO | 50 days |
| DIO (inventory days on COGS) | 90 days |
| DPO | 45 days |
| Overdraft limit / drawn | $4.0m / $2.5m |
| Interest rate on overdraft | 9% |

### Effects across the company

| Department / area | Effect of the new contract |
|---|---|
| Sales | +$6m revenue; commission paid on signing; account management workload. |
| Pricing / margin | List gross margin 28%; the 10% discount reduces gross margin on this contract to 20% (price 90, cost 72). |
| Procurement | Must buy ~$4.8m more stock per year; supplier wants 30% advance on larger orders. |
| Inventory / warehouse | Hotel requires 60 days of buffer stock dedicated to it; extra racking and a part-time storekeeper. |
| Logistics | Deliveries to 9 sites; installation teams scheduled. |
| Credit control | Single customer exposure rises to $2.0m at peak — above the credit-insurance limit of $1.2m. |
| Finance / working capital | Receivables, inventory and supplier advances all rise. |
| Treasury / banking | Overdraft headroom ($1.5m) is not enough; a facility increase or receivables finance is needed. |
| Legal | Hotel contract has liquidated damages of 1% per week of late delivery, capped at 10%. |
| HR | Two installers to hire. |
| Management | Concentration: hotel group would be 23% of revenue. |

### The numbers

Contribution from the contract:

- Revenue: $6.0m. COGS at 80% of discounted price: $4.8m. **Gross profit: $1.2m.**
- Incremental operating costs: installers $120k, storekeeper and racking $60k, extra delivery $90k, commissions 2% = $120k → **$390k**.
- Incremental operating profit **before financing: $810k**.

Working capital needed:

- Receivables: 6.0m × 120/365 = **$1.97m**.
- Dedicated inventory: 4.8m × 60/365 = **$0.79m** (plus normal pipeline stock 4.8 × 30/365 = $0.39m) → **$1.18m**.
- Supplier advance: 30% on, say, quarterly orders of $1.2m = **$0.36m** outstanding at peak.
- Less payables: 4.8m × 45/365 on the 70% not prepaid ≈ **($0.41m)**.
- **Net working capital required ≈ $3.1m.**

Financing cost at 9%: about **$280k per year**, and the existing overdraft headroom is only $1.5m.

Incremental profit after financing ≈ 810 − 280 = **$530k** before tax. Return on the incremental working capital: 810 ÷ 3,100 ≈ **26%** pre-financing — attractive *if* it can be funded and the customer pays.

:::tip How an experienced CFO would respond
“Commercially attractive, but not on these terms. We need (1) a facility increase or a receivables-finance line of at least $2m before signing; (2) credit insurance or a parent-company guarantee covering the exposure above $1.2m; (3) payment terms of 90 days rather than 120, or a 20% advance; (4) liquidated damages capped at 5%; (5) a price-review clause if supplier prices rise more than 5%.”

The graduate looks at the $6m revenue; the CFO looks at the $3.1m of cash it consumes and the $2m concentrated credit exposure.
:::

### Effect on the three statements (first year, simplified)

| Statement | Line | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Income statement | Revenue | +6.0m |
| | COGS | −4.8m |
| | Operating expenses | −0.39m |
| | Interest | −0.28m |
| | Profit before tax | +0.53m |
| Balance sheet | Receivables | +1.97m |
| | Inventory | +1.18m |
| | Supplier advances | +0.36m |
| | Payables | +0.41m (liability) |
| | Borrowings | +2.6m (approx., after retained profit) |
| Cash flow | Operating cash flow | Profit 0.53m − ΔWC 3.1m ≈ **−2.57m** |
| | Financing | +2.6m borrowing |

The contract makes the company **more profitable and more indebted** in its first year. If the hotel group pays late or disputes deliveries, profit evaporates and the debt remains.

## The working-capital trap

The **cash conversion cycle** (CCC) measures how long cash is tied up in operations:

:::formula Cash conversion cycle
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO

DIO = inventory ÷ COGS × 365 · DSO = receivables ÷ revenue × 365 · DPO = payables ÷ COGS × 365 (purchases can be used instead of COGS where available)

Working-capital need ≈ daily COGS × DIO + daily revenue × DSO − daily COGS × DPO
:::

Every additional day of CCC on $20m revenue costs about $55k of cash (20m ÷ 365). Fast growth multiplies this. A company growing revenue at 40% a year with a 100-day CCC needs to finance roughly 0.4 × revenue × 100/365 ≈ 11% of current revenue in new working capital every year — usually more than its net margin can fund.

:::formula Self-funded growth rate (approximation)
Maximum growth funded by internal cash ≈ Net margin (after dividends) ÷ (working capital as % of sales + net fixed assets as % of sales − debt capacity created)

A simplified version for working-capital-heavy traders: g ≈ retained net margin ÷ (NWC ÷ Sales). Example: retained margin 4%, NWC/Sales 25% → g ≈ 16% per year without new debt or equity.
:::

## Why profitable businesses fail

| Cause | Mechanism | Warning signs | Typical remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overtrading | Growth outruns working-capital funding | Rising DSO/DIO, overdraft at limit, late supplier payments | Slow growth, raise funding, tighten terms |
| Customer concentration | One customer delays or fails | Top customer > 20–25% of revenue | Credit insurance, diversification |
| Short-term funding of long-term assets | Capex funded with overdraft; bank withdraws line | Current ratio < 1, overdraft hard-core | Term loan, equity |
| Debt maturity wall | Loan matures when markets are closed | Large bullet repayments | Refinance early, stagger maturities |
| Covenant breach | Bank can accelerate | Shrinking headroom | Early negotiation, waivers |
| FX mismatch | Revenue in local currency, debt in USD; devaluation | Unhedged foreign debt | Hedging, matching |
| Fixed-price contracts in inflation | Costs rise, prices fixed | Long contracts without indexation | Escalation clauses |
| Fraud or control failure | Cash leaks, false profits | Unreconciled accounts, dominant individual | Controls, audit |
| Tax or legal shock | Large unexpected liability | Aggressive tax positions, disputes | Provisions, settlements |
| Supplier failure / disruption | Cannot deliver; penalties | Single-source supply | Dual sourcing, inventory buffers |
| Accounting profit not real | Revenue recognised too early; capitalised costs | Profit rising, cash falling | Quality-of-earnings review |

## High revenue, financial distress: three patterns

1. **Low-margin trading with high volume.** A commodity trader with $500m revenue and a 1% net margin earns $5m. One defaulted cargo of $15m wipes out three years of profit. Revenue says nothing about resilience; equity and risk controls do.
2. **Project businesses with negative cash flows.** A contractor with $300m of revenue recognised over time may have $90m of unbilled work-in-progress and retentions, while paying subcontractors monthly. A client’s payment delay pushes it into distress.
3. **Subsidised growth.** A delivery platform with $200m of gross merchandise value and negative unit economics loses more as it grows; it survives only while investors fund it.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A company has revenue $36.5m, COGS $29.2m, receivables $6.0m, inventory $4.8m and payables $3.2m. Compute DSO, DIO, DPO and CCC. What is the cash impact of reducing DSO by 10 days?
2. Revisit the Gulf Kitchen case. The hotel offers 90-day terms if you remove the discount entirely. Recompute the gross profit and receivables. Which deal is better?
3. List five questions a bank credit officer would ask Gulf Kitchen when it requests a $2m facility increase.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. DSO = 6.0 ÷ 36.5 × 365 = **60 days**; DIO = 4.8 ÷ 29.2 × 365 = **60 days**; DPO = 3.2 ÷ 29.2 × 365 = **40 days**; CCC = **80 days**. Reducing DSO by 10 days releases 36.5m ÷ 365 × 10 = **$1.0m** of cash.
2. Without discount the price is list: revenue for the same volume = 6.0 ÷ 0.9 = $6.67m; COGS $4.8m; gross profit **$1.87m** (vs $1.2m). Receivables = 6.67 × 90/365 = **$1.64m** (vs $1.97m). Clearly better: +$0.67m gross profit and $0.33m less receivables — if the hotel accepts it.
3. What is the hotel group’s credit quality and payment history? How concentrated will exposure be, and is it insured? What is the working-capital forecast month by month? What are the contract’s penalty and termination terms? What security and covenants can the company offer, and what is current leverage and headroom? (Also: management capacity to deliver, supplier terms.)
:::

# Industry Economics: How Eight Industries Make Money

:::obj In this chapter
For each industry: the revenue model, cost structure, capital intensity, working-capital profile, key metrics, typical risks and how banks finance it. Figures are illustrative ranges for teaching and vary widely by company, country and period.
:::

## Comparative overview

| Industry | Revenue model | Cost structure | Working capital | Key metrics | Typical financing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing | Product sales | High fixed (plant), material-heavy | Inventory-heavy (raw, WIP, finished) | Utilisation, unit cost, yield, OEE | Term loans for plant; working-capital lines |
| Retail | Product sales to consumers | Rent, staff, inventory | Cash sales; supplier credit can make CCC negative | Same-store sales, sales per m², GMROI, shrinkage | Supplier credit, RCF, leases |
| Logistics | Freight, handling, storage fees | Fleet/warehouse fixed costs or asset-light | Receivables 45–90 days; pays carriers faster | Revenue per shipment, yield, utilisation | Asset finance, invoice finance |
| Software (SaaS) | Subscriptions | High R&D and sales; low cost to serve | Often negative (annual prepayments) | ARR, NRR, CAC payback, Rule of 40 | Equity (VC), recurring-revenue loans |
| Banking | Interest spread + fees | Funding cost, staff, credit losses, capital | Balance sheet is the product | NIM, cost-to-income, NPL ratio, CET1, ROE | Deposits, wholesale funding, capital |
| Construction | Contracts recognised over time | Subcontractors, materials, plant | Retentions, WIP, advance payments | Backlog, margin per project, cash curve | Advance-payment and performance guarantees, project lines |
| Energy | Commodity sales, regulated tariffs, PPAs | Very capital-intensive | Varies | Production cost/barrel, lifting cost, capacity factor | Project finance, reserve-based lending, bonds |
| International trading | Margin on buy-sell | Freight, insurance, finance cost | Transaction-by-transaction | Margin per tonne, turnover of capital, VaR | Trade finance (L/Cs, borrowing base) |

## Manufacturing

A manufacturer buys raw materials, converts them with labour and machines, and sells finished goods. Profitability depends on **capacity utilisation**, **yield** (good output ÷ input), **material price** management and **product mix**. Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE = availability × performance × quality) is a standard operational KPI.

Cash cycle: raw materials (30–90 days) + production (days to months) + finished goods (30–60 days) + receivables (45–90 days) − payables (30–60 days). CCC of 90–150 days is common.

:::example Fictional plastic-packaging plant
Revenue $40m; materials 55% of revenue (polymer resin priced off oil derivatives); direct labour 8%; energy 6%; plant overheads and depreciation $6m; SG&A $3m. EBITDA margin ≈ 18%, EBIT ≈ 13%. A 20% rise in resin prices not passed through for one quarter cuts quarterly EBIT by about 85% (55% × 20% = 11% of revenue). Mitigation: price-adjustment formulas linked to resin indices.
:::

## Retail

Retailers earn a gross margin (typically higher in fashion, lower in grocery) and must cover rent, staff and logistics. Strong grocers can have a **negative cash conversion cycle**: they sell stock for cash in days but pay suppliers in 45–60 days, so suppliers finance their growth. Key metrics: like-for-like (same-store) sales growth, sales per square metre, basket size, footfall, conversion, inventory turnover, shrinkage (losses from theft and damage), GMROI (gross margin ÷ average inventory at cost). E-commerce adds fulfilment and return costs and customer-acquisition spending.

## Logistics

Two models: **asset-heavy** (own trucks, warehouses, vessels — high fixed costs, utilisation-driven) and **asset-light** (freight forwarders, brokers, 3PL management — buy capacity from carriers, earn a margin or fee). Forwarders report **gross profit per shipment/TEU** rather than revenue because pass-through freight inflates revenue when freight rates spike. A forwarder’s revenue can double in a freight-rate boom with almost no change in gross profit — another reason not to judge businesses by revenue.

Working capital risk: forwarders often pay carriers within 7–30 days but collect from customers in 45–90 days.

## Software (SaaS)

Gross margins of 70–85% because serving an extra customer costs little; heavy upfront spending on development and customer acquisition. Revenue is recognised monthly while customers often pay annually in advance, creating deferred revenue and favourable cash flow. Metrics: ARR, net revenue retention, gross churn, CAC payback months, LTV/CAC, and the “Rule of 40” (growth % + EBITDA or FCF margin % ≥ 40 as a heuristic for balance between growth and profitability).

## Banking

A bank’s products are its balance sheet. It borrows (deposits, wholesale funding) and lends at a higher rate, earning **net interest income**; it also earns fees (payments, trade finance, advisory). Costs: staff, technology, credit losses and the cost of holding regulatory capital. Key ratios: net interest margin (NIM), cost-to-income ratio, NPL ratio and coverage, capital ratios (CET1), liquidity ratios (LCR, NSFR), return on equity. Banks are highly leveraged (assets often 10–12× equity), so small losses on assets matter. Part VI explains banking in depth.

## Construction

Contractors win projects through tenders, often providing **bid bonds**, then **performance guarantees** and **advance-payment guarantees**. Revenue is recognised over time as work progresses (IFRS 15 input or output methods). Clients often hold back **retentions** (5–10%) until completion and the defects liability period ends. Typical risks: underpriced bids, scope changes not approved (variations), delays with liquidated damages, subcontractor failure, material price inflation, and client payment delays. Cash curve: positive if advance payments and timely certifications exceed spending; negative if certifications lag.

## Energy

Upstream oil and gas: exploration and production with very high capex, long lead times, commodity price exposure; financed by equity, bonds, reserve-based lending. Midstream: pipelines and storage, often contracted fee income. Downstream: refining (earns the **crack spread**) and marketing. Power: generation under long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) — typical for renewable project finance — or merchant exposure. Utilities: regulated returns on a regulated asset base. Part XI covers commodity trading.

## International trading

Traders buy from producers and sell to buyers across borders, earning a margin from price differences across locations, times, qualities and from logistics and credit services. Margins are thin (often 1–5% gross on commodities, more on specialised goods); return on capital depends on **turning capital quickly** and leveraging trade finance. Risks: counterparty default, price moves between purchase and sale, quality/quantity disputes, shipping problems, documentary discrepancies, sanctions and fraud.

:::example Return on capital for a fictional trader
A trader buys $2m of goods and sells for $2.06m (3% gross margin) every 60 days, six times a year. Costs per deal: freight/insurance included in margin; finance cost (80% L/C-financed at 8% for 60 days) ≈ $21k; other costs $9k. Net per deal ≈ $30k; per year ≈ $180k. Equity employed: 20% × $2m = $400k → return on equity ≈ **45%**. One deal lost through default (−$2m) would wipe out five equity bases — so credit risk controls dominate trading.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Why might a freight forwarder’s revenue fall 40% while its gross profit stays stable?
2. Explain how a supermarket can have negative working capital, and why that is valuable.
3. A SaaS company grows 35% with an EBITDA margin of −10%. Does it pass the Rule of 40? What would you ask?
4. A contractor recognises $50m of revenue but has negative operating cash flow. Give three causes.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Revenue includes pass-through freight; when market freight rates fall, revenue falls while the forwarder’s margin per shipment (gross profit) can remain stable.
2. It collects cash from consumers immediately (DSO ≈ 0), turns inventory quickly (e.g., 20 days) and pays suppliers in 45–60 days, so CCC is negative: suppliers finance its operations and growth generates cash.
3. 35 + (−10) = 25 < 40 → fails the heuristic. Ask about CAC payback, net revenue retention, gross margin, cash runway, and when operating leverage will lift margins.
4. Retentions withheld by the client; certifications/billing lagging work done (contract assets/WIP); client payment delays; heavy upfront procurement; unapproved variations.
:::

# Growth, Foreign Expansion, M&A, Restructuring and Closure

:::obj In this chapter
How companies grow and how they shrink: organic and inorganic growth, entering foreign markets, mergers and acquisitions (overview), restructuring, insolvency and closure. Corporate-finance aspects of M&A are in {{ch:Mergers, Acquisitions and Corporate Restructuring}}; legal aspects in Part XII; integration in Part XIV.

## Growth strategies

The Ansoff matrix organises growth options:

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing markets** | Market penetration (sell more to current customers/segments) — lowest risk | Product development |
| **New markets** | Market development (new regions, segments) | Diversification — highest risk |

**Organic growth** (building yourself) is slower but cheaper and easier to control. **Inorganic growth** (acquisitions, joint ventures) is faster but riskier: overpayment, integration failure, culture clash.

## Expansion into foreign markets

| Entry mode | Control | Investment | Risk | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exporting via agents/distributors | Low | Low | Low | Testing a market |
| Licensing / franchising | Low-medium | Low | IP and brand risk | Consumer brands, technology |
| Representative office | Low | Low | Limited activities permitted | Market intelligence, relationship building |
| Branch | High | Medium | Parent liable for branch | Banks, contractors (where permitted) |
| Joint venture | Shared | Medium | Partner disputes | Markets requiring local partners or knowledge |
| Wholly owned subsidiary | Full | High | Full exposure | Long-term commitment |
| Acquisition | Full | High | Integration, valuation | Fast access to customers and licences |

Questions to answer before entering a country: market size and growth; regulatory restrictions on foreign ownership; licences; tax (corporate tax, withholding tax on dividends and royalties, double tax treaties, transfer pricing); currency convertibility and repatriation of profits; labour law; contract enforcement and dispute resolution; political and sanctions risk; payment practices; local partners’ reputation (due diligence).

:::warn Common mistakes in foreign expansion
- Assuming the home-country business model works unchanged.
- Choosing a local partner on personal trust without due diligence; giving exclusivity without performance targets.
- Ignoring how profits will be repatriated (withholding taxes, FX controls).
- Underestimating time to obtain licences and hire staff.
- Pricing in local currency without hedging or cost matching.
:::

## Mergers and acquisitions: an overview

Motives: market share, scale economies, new capabilities or technology, new geographies, vertical integration, tax or financial engineering (less defensible), and sometimes managerial empire-building. The typical process:

1. Strategy and target screening.
2. Approach; NDA; preliminary information (teaser, information memorandum).
3. Non-binding offer / letter of intent; valuation range.
4. Due diligence (financial, tax, legal, commercial, operational, IT, HR, ESG).
5. Binding offer; negotiation of the sale and purchase agreement (SPA).
6. Signing; conditions precedent (regulatory approvals, financing).
7. Closing; payment; completion accounts or locked box.
8. Post-merger integration.

Studies and practitioner experience consistently show that many acquisitions fail to create value for the acquirer, mainly through overpayment and integration problems.

## Restructuring

Restructuring changes a company’s operations, capital structure or ownership to restore viability or increase value.

| Type | What changes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Operational | Costs, footprint, processes | Closing branches, outsourcing, headcount reduction |
| Financial | Debt and equity | Maturity extension, covenant reset, debt-for-equity swap, new money |
| Portfolio | Businesses owned | Divestments, spin-offs |
| Legal/organisational | Group structure | Merging entities, holding company changes |

A financial restructuring usually involves negotiations with lenders under a **standstill** (lenders agree not to enforce while a plan is prepared), an **independent business review**, a **13-week cash-flow forecast**, and a plan that shares the losses among shareholders and creditors according to their priority.

## Bankruptcy, insolvency and closure

Insolvency is generally tested in two ways: **cash-flow insolvency** (unable to pay debts as they fall due) and **balance-sheet insolvency** (liabilities exceed assets). Each jurisdiction has its own procedures — for example reorganisation procedures that allow the business to continue under court protection while a plan is agreed (US Chapter 11 is the best-known example), administration-type procedures, preventive composition or restructuring procedures, and liquidation. Several Middle Eastern countries have modernised their bankruptcy laws in recent years; the details must be verified locally with a qualified lawyer.

The **priority of claims** (waterfall) in liquidation typically runs: secured creditors (from their collateral) → insolvency costs → preferential creditors (often certain employee claims and taxes, depending on the law) → unsecured creditors → subordinated creditors → shareholders. Shareholders usually receive nothing in an insolvent liquidation.

## Directors’ responsibilities near insolvency

In many jurisdictions, when a company is insolvent or close to it, directors must consider creditors’ interests and may face personal liability if they continue trading irresponsibly, prefer certain creditors, or dispose of assets at undervalue. Practical steps: take professional advice early, keep accurate records, minute decisions, avoid new debts the company cannot pay, and communicate with lenders.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A Lebanese software company wants to sell into Saudi Arabia. Compare exporting through a reseller with establishing a subsidiary.
2. A company has assets that would realise $30m in liquidation: $20m from a mortgaged warehouse (bank owed $25m) and $10m from other assets. Unsecured creditors are owed $15m; liquidation costs $1m; preferential claims $2m. Estimate recoveries for the bank and unsecured creditors.
3. Why do lenders usually prefer a consensual restructuring to liquidation?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Reseller: fast, low cost, low control, reseller margin 20–40%, dependent on reseller’s motivation, limited customer data; check exclusivity and termination terms. Subsidiary: higher cost and time (licensing, local hiring, possible localisation requirements), more control and credibility with large/government clients, local tax obligations, repatriation planning. Many firms start with a reseller and move to a subsidiary once revenue justifies it.
2. Bank recovers $20m from its collateral; its $5m shortfall becomes an unsecured claim. Remaining $10m: costs $1m and preferential $2m → $7m for unsecured creditors totalling 15 + 5 = $20m → **35 cents per dollar**. Bank total ≈ 20 + 1.75 = **$21.75m (87%)**; other unsecured creditors receive **$5.25m (35%)**. (Order of costs vs secured realisations varies by jurisdiction.)
3. Liquidation destroys going-concern value (customers, staff, contracts), sells assets at distressed prices and incurs heavy costs; a viable business restructured usually returns more to creditors, faster.
:::
