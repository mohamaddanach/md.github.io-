If sales is how a business earns money, procurement and supply chain are how it spends most of it. For a distributor, purchases can be 70–85% of revenue; a 2% saving in landed cost can be worth more than a 20% increase in sales. Supply chains also determine service levels, working capital and resilience to disruption.

This part covers the full chain: procurement strategy and supplier management, inventory and warehousing, transport modes and freight economics, customs and disruption management, and a complete simulation of importing goods from China into the Middle East with every cost from supplier quotation to the buyer’s warehouse.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part IX
- Design a procurement strategy and run RFI, RFQ and RFP processes with objective evaluation.
- Qualify suppliers, negotiate and contract, and choose payment terms and supply-chain finance.
- Manage inventory with forecasts, reorder points, safety stock and turnover targets.
- Compare transport modes and price freight correctly (chargeable weight, W/M, surcharges).
- Understand ports, container and bulk shipping, multimodal transport and last-mile delivery.
- Manage customs, demurrage and detention, and supply-chain disruption.
- Calculate a full landed cost and allocate costs across products.
:::

# Procurement Strategy and Supplier Management

:::obj In this chapter
Strategic sourcing, category segmentation, supplier identification and qualification, RFI/RFQ/RFP processes, evaluation, negotiation, contracts, payment terms and supplier performance management.
:::

## From purchasing to strategic procurement

| Level | Focus | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Transactional purchasing | Processing orders | “Did we get it on time?” |
| Tactical procurement | Price negotiation, supplier choice | “Did we get a good price?” |
| Strategic sourcing | Total cost, supply risk, innovation, category strategies | “Are we buying the right things from the right suppliers on the right terms, with acceptable risk?” |

## Category segmentation: the Kraljic matrix

| | Low supply risk | High supply risk |
|---|---|---|
| **High profit impact** | **Leverage items** — many suppliers, high spend (e.g., packaging, standard steel). Strategy: competitive tendering, volume consolidation, negotiate hard. | **Strategic items** — few suppliers, critical (e.g., key components, specialised chemicals). Strategy: partnerships, long-term contracts, joint development, risk mitigation. |
| **Low profit impact** | **Non-critical items** — office supplies, MRO. Strategy: simplify, catalogues, e-procurement, reduce transaction cost. | **Bottleneck items** — low spend but scarce (e.g., a proprietary spare part). Strategy: secure supply, safety stock, find alternatives, redesign. |

## Supplier identification and qualification

Sources: trade fairs, industry associations, B2B platforms, trade data (importers’ records in some countries), embassies and trade-promotion agencies, referrals, existing suppliers’ suppliers.

Qualification checks:

| Area | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Legal existence and ownership | Registration documents, UBO, sanctions and adverse-media screening |
| Financial strength | Financial statements, credit reports, bank references |
| Capability and capacity | Factory audit, production lines, capacity utilisation, lead times |
| Quality | ISO 9001 or sector standards, quality-control process, defect rates, samples, test reports |
| Compliance | Product certifications, export licences, labour and environmental standards, anti-bribery |
| Logistics | Proximity to ports, packaging, export experience, Incoterms capability |
| References | Other customers, ideally in your region |
| Risk | Single-site dependency, country risk, concentration of their revenue on you |

## RFI, RFQ and RFP

| Document | Purpose | When | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| RFI — request for information | Learn about suppliers’ capabilities | Early, market scanning | Shortlist |
| RFQ — request for quotation | Get prices for well-specified items | Standard goods/services | Price comparison |
| RFP — request for proposal | Get solutions and prices for complex needs | Services, systems, projects | Evaluated proposals |
| Tender (often public) | Formal competitive process with strict rules | Government, large corporates | Contract award |

## Evaluating offers: the procurement comparison sheet

:::example Comparison of three supplier offers (fictional, 120 t steel pipe, CFR Dammam)
| Criterion | Supplier A (China) | Supplier B (Turkey) | Supplier C (India) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price CFR (USD/t) | 820 | 905 | 845 |
| Duty and charges (assume 5% duty + $35/t) | 76 | 80 | 77 |
| Payment terms | 30% advance T/T, 70% at sight L/C | 10% advance, 90% L/C 60 days | 100% L/C at sight |
| Financing cost/benefit vs base (8% p.a.) | Advance 30% held ~75 days: +$4/t | 60-day credit on 90%: −$11/t | 0 |
| Expected quality cost (rejects, rework) | $30/t (history 3.5% rejects incl. replacement costs) | $5/t | $12/t |
| Lead time | 60 days | 35 days | 50 days |
| Safety stock implication | +$9/t holding cost | 0 | +$4/t |
| **Total landed cost (USD/t)** | **939** | **979** | **938** |
| Quality/certification score (0–10) | 6 | 9 | 7 |
| Delivery reliability score | 6 | 9 | 7 |

Weighted scoring (landed cost 50% scored relative to lowest; others as weights): C ranks first narrowly, B second (premium for quality and speed), A third despite the lowest price. Decision: award 70% to C, 30% to B (dual sourcing reduces risk and keeps competition).
:::

## Supplier negotiations and contracts

Negotiate the **total cost**: price, payment terms, Incoterms, packaging, lead time, quality guarantees, warranties and remedies, price-adjustment formulas, minimum order quantities, consignment or VMI stock, rebates, and exclusivity.

Key supply-contract clauses: specifications and acceptance tests; delivery terms and liquidated damages for delay; warranties and remedies; inspection rights; price-adjustment mechanisms (e.g., linked to steel or resin indices); force majeure; intellectual property; confidentiality; compliance (anti-bribery, sanctions, labour standards); limitation of liability; termination; governing law and dispute resolution.

## Supplier payment terms and supply-chain finance

| Term | Buyer cash impact | Supplier risk | When used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advance payment | Worst | None | New supplier relationships, custom goods |
| Cash against documents | Neutral | Low | Trade with moderate trust |
| L/C at sight / usance | Neutral / financing | Low | International trade |
| Open account 30–120 days | Best | High | Established relationships |
| Consignment | Best (pay when used) | Highest | Strategic partnerships |

**Supply-chain finance** (payables finance) allows suppliers to be paid early by a bank at the buyer’s credit cost while the buyer keeps or extends its terms (see {{ch:Trade, Working-Capital, Project}}).

## Supplier performance management

KPIs: on-time-in-full (OTIF), quality (defect rate, PPM), lead time and variability, responsiveness, price competitiveness, compliance, innovation. Supplier scorecards reviewed quarterly; development plans for strategic suppliers; exit plans for chronic underperformers.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Classify in the Kraljic matrix for a dairy processor: (a) milk cartons; (b) office stationery; (c) a specialised cultures supplier with a patent; (d) electricity.
2. A supplier offers a 2% discount if you pay 30% in advance 60 days before shipment and the 70% balance on delivery (delivery is 30 days after shipment), instead of open account 60 days after delivery with no discount. Purchases $1.2m; your cost of capital 9%. Compare.
3. Write four qualification questions for a new overseas supplier of electrical cables.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (a) Leverage (high spend, many suppliers); (b) non-critical; (c) strategic (high impact, limited supply — or bottleneck if spend is small); (d) leverage or strategic depending on market — often strategic with limited supplier choice and high impact.
2. Discount saves **$24,000**. Cost of paying earlier: the 30% advance ($360k) is paid 150 days earlier than under open account (60 + 30 + 60): 360,000 × 9% × 150/365 = $13,315; the 70% balance ($840k) is paid 60 days earlier: 840,000 × 9% × 60/365 = $12,427. Total **$25,742** > $24,000 → slightly unfavourable even before the counterparty risk of the advance. Decline, or accept only with a higher discount or an advance-payment guarantee.
3. Do you hold certifications to IEC/local standards for these cables, and can you provide test reports from accredited labs? What is your annual capacity and current utilisation? Who are three customers in our region we can contact? Can you provide audited financial statements and a bank reference? (Also: copper price adjustment policy, export experience, ownership.)
:::

# Inventory Management and Warehousing

:::obj In this chapter
Demand forecasting, inventory policies, reorder points and safety stock, EOQ, ABC/XYZ analysis, inventory KPIs, and warehouse operations and costs.
:::

## Why inventory exists

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Cycle stock | Ordering in batches (economies of ordering/shipping) |
| Safety stock | Protection against demand and supply variability |
| Pipeline (in-transit) stock | Goods moving through the chain — owned by whoever bears it under the Incoterms/contract |
| Anticipation / seasonal stock | Build-up before peaks (Ramadan, summer, back-to-school) |
| Speculative stock | Buying ahead of expected price rises (a commercial bet — label it as such) |
| Decoupling stock | Buffers between production stages |

## Demand forecasting

Methods range from moving averages and exponential smoothing to regression and machine learning (Part XVI). Forecasts should be made at the level decisions are taken (SKU × location × week), reconciled with sales and marketing plans in a **Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP)** process, and measured for accuracy (MAPE) and bias.

## Reorder points, safety stock and service levels

:::formula Reorder point and safety stock
ROP = average daily demand × average lead time (days) + safety stock
Safety stock (demand variability only) = z × σ_d × √L
Safety stock (demand and lead-time variability) = z × √(L × σ_d² + d² × σ_L²)
z = service-level factor (90% → 1.28; 95% → 1.65; 98% → 2.05; 99% → 2.33); σ_d = std dev of daily demand; L = lead time in days; σ_L = std dev of lead time
:::

:::example Air-conditioner spare parts (fictional)
Average demand d = 40 units/day; σ_d = 12; lead time L = 30 days; σ_L = 6 days; target service level 95% (z = 1.65).
- SS = 1.65 × √(30 × 144 + 1,600 × 36) = 1.65 × √(4,320 + 57,600) = 1.65 × 248.8 ≈ **411 units**.
- ROP = 40 × 30 + 411 = **1,611 units**.
- Lead-time variability (σ_L) dominates: reducing it from 6 to 3 days cuts SS to about 1.65 × √(4,320 + 14,400) = **226 units** — supplier reliability is worth money.
:::

## Order quantity

EOQ (see Part IV) balances ordering and holding costs; in international trade, **container economics** often determine quantities (ordering a full 40ft container may be cheaper per unit than LCL), along with supplier MOQs and price breaks.

## ABC and XYZ analysis

- **ABC** by value: A items (~10–20% of SKUs, ~70–80% of value) — tight control, frequent review; C items (~50% of SKUs, ~5% of value) — simple rules.
- **XYZ** by demand variability: X stable, Y variable, Z erratic. AX items suit automated replenishment; CZ items may be made-to-order or dropped.

## Inventory KPIs

| KPI | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory turnover | COGS ÷ average inventory | Efficiency |
| Days inventory outstanding | 365 ÷ turnover | Working capital |
| Fill rate / service level | Orders or lines fully served ÷ total | Customer service |
| Stock-out rate | Stock-out occurrences ÷ SKUs or orders | Lost sales risk |
| Obsolete / slow-moving stock % | Value aged > x months ÷ total | Write-off risk |
| GMROI | Gross margin ÷ average inventory at cost | Retail profitability of stock |
| Inventory accuracy | Matching system vs physical counts | Data quality |

## Warehousing

Functions: receiving, inspection, put-away, storage, picking, packing, dispatch, returns, value-added services (labelling, kitting). Cost drivers: space (per pallet position or m²), labour (per pick, per hour), equipment, systems (WMS), utilities (especially cold storage), insurance. Options: own warehouse (control, fixed cost), leased, public/shared warehouse, 3PL contract (variable cost, flexibility), bonded/free-zone warehouses (duty deferral).

:::example Cost per pallet in a 3PL warehouse (fictional monthly)
| Activity | Rate | Volume | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storage per pallet per week | $3.50 | 800 pallets × 4.3 weeks | $12,040 |
| Inbound handling per pallet | $4.00 | 300 | $1,200 |
| Outbound picking per case | $0.45 | 18,000 | $8,100 |
| Value-added labelling per unit | $0.08 | 25,000 | $2,000 |
| WMS fee | — | — | $500 |
| **Total** | | | **$23,840** |
Cost per case shipped ≈ $1.32; as a % of $1.5m monthly COGS shipped ≈ 1.6%.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Demand 120/day, σ_d 30, lead time 20 days (fixed). Compute safety stock and ROP at 98% service.
2. Inventory: average $4.5m; COGS $27m; gross margin $9m. Compute turnover, DIO and GMROI.
3. Why might a distributor deliberately hold more inventory than EOQ suggests?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. SS = 2.05 × 30 × √20 = 2.05 × 134.2 ≈ **275 units**; ROP = 120 × 20 + 275 = **2,675 units**.
2. Turnover = 27 ÷ 4.5 = **6.0×**; DIO ≈ **61 days**; GMROI = 9 ÷ 4.5 = **2.0** ($2 of gross margin per $1 of inventory per year).
3. Supplier MOQs or full-container economics, expected price increases, supply risk (disruptions), seasonal peaks, customer service commitments with penalties, or to secure availability during shortages — each with an explicit cost-benefit justification.
:::

# Transport Modes, Freight and Logistics Networks

:::obj In this chapter
Road, rail, air and sea transport; container and bulk shipping; multimodal transport; ports and terminals; last-mile delivery; freight forwarders; and how freight is priced.
:::

## Comparing modes

| Mode | Cost per tonne-km | Speed | Reliability | Suited to | Key documents |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sea (container) | Very low | Slow (2–6 weeks intercontinental) | Moderate (schedule reliability varies) | Most manufactured goods | B/L, sea waybill |
| Sea (bulk/tanker) | Lowest | Slow | Depends on charter | Commodities: grain, ore, coal, oil | B/L, charter party |
| Air | Highest | Fastest (days) | High | High value, urgent, perishable, small | Air waybill |
| Road | Moderate | Door-to-door flexibility | Border delays possible | Regional trade (e.g., GCC, Levant), last mile | CMR note, TIR carnet |
| Rail | Low–moderate | Medium | Good on corridors | Bulk and containers on long land corridors | CIM/SMGS consignment notes |
| Pipeline | Very low (after construction) | Continuous | High | Oil, gas, water | Contracts |
| Multimodal | Combination | — | One contract for the whole route | Door-to-door | Multimodal/combined transport B/L |

## Container shipping

- **Equipment:** 20ft (1 TEU, ~33 m³), 40ft (2 TEU, ~67 m³), 40ft high cube (~76 m³), reefers (refrigerated), open-top, flat-rack, tank containers.
- **FCL vs LCL:** full container load (one shipper) vs less-than-container load (consolidated by a forwarder/NVOCC at a container freight station).
- **Liner services:** scheduled routes; carriers often operate in alliances sharing vessel space.
- **Freight components:** base ocean freight + surcharges: bunker (fuel) adjustment factor (BAF), currency adjustment factor (CAF), peak-season surcharge (PSS), emergency or war-risk surcharges, low-sulphur fuel surcharges, carbon-related surcharges (e.g., EU ETS costs passed through), terminal handling charges (THC) at origin and destination, documentation fees, equipment imbalance charges.
- **Free time:** days without demurrage/detention at origin and destination — negotiable.
- **Verified gross mass (VGM):** shippers must declare container weight before loading (SOLAS requirement since 2016).

## Bulk shipping

Dry bulk (grain, ore, coal, fertiliser) and liquid bulk (crude, products, chemicals, LNG) move under **charter parties**: voyage charter (pay per tonne for a voyage), time charter (hire the vessel per day), bareboat charter (lease the vessel without crew). Vessel sizes (e.g., Handysize, Supramax, Panamax, Capesize for dry bulk; VLCC, Suezmax, Aframax for tankers) affect freight per tonne and port access. Laytime, demurrage and despatch are key commercial terms (Part XI).

## Air cargo

Priced per chargeable kg (higher of actual and volumetric weight at 6,000 cm³/kg), with fuel and security surcharges, airline and handling charges. Air cargo moves in passenger aircraft bellies and freighters. Dangerous goods (including lithium batteries) face strict IATA rules.

## Road and rail

Road freight is priced per trip, per kg or per pallet; cross-border road transport depends on border-crossing times, permits and transit regimes (e.g., TIR carnets). Rail is efficient for long corridors and bulk; new rail links in the Gulf region (e.g., the Etihad Rail network in the UAE) are changing regional logistics options.

## Ports and terminals

A port call involves pilotage, berthing, stevedoring (loading/unloading), terminal storage, customs inspection, and gate-out. Port performance indicators: vessel waiting time, crane productivity, truck turnaround time, dwell time of containers. Major regional hubs include Jebel Ali, King Abdullah Port, Jeddah Islamic Port, Dammam, Salalah, Hamad Port, Port Said and others. Port congestion and dwell times directly affect demurrage and inventory planning.

## Last-mile delivery

The final delivery to the customer is often the most expensive part per unit (failed deliveries, address quality, cash on delivery in some markets, returns). Solutions: route optimisation software, pickup points/lockers, delivery windows, crowdsourced fleets, address standardisation (e.g., national addressing systems).

## Freight forwarders and NVOCCs

Forwarders organise transport, consolidate cargo, handle documents and customs, and buy capacity from carriers. An **NVOCC** (non-vessel operating common carrier) issues its own house B/Ls and acts as carrier towards the shipper. Choosing a forwarder: network coverage, carrier contracts, customs capability, technology (tracking, documents), financial stability, insurance and liability terms (standard trading conditions often limit liability heavily).

## Freight pricing: worked examples

:::example Container quote (fictional, Shanghai → Jebel Ali, 40ft HC)
| Charge | USD |
|---|---|
| Ocean freight (base) | 1,450 |
| Bunker surcharge | 320 |
| Origin THC | 160 |
| Documentation | 60 |
| Seal and VGM | 25 |
| Destination THC | 250 |
| Delivery order fee | 90 |
| **Total port-to-port + terminals** | **2,355** |
Under FOB Shanghai the buyer pays everything except origin THC (usually the seller’s); under CFR Jebel Ali the seller pays freight and surcharges, and the buyer pays destination THC and DO fees (check the carriage contract).
:::

:::example FCL vs LCL break-even
LCL rate $70 per W/M plus $180 fixed origin/destination CFS charges; FCL 20ft all-in $1,300. LCL cost for x m³ = 70x + 180. Break-even: 70x + 180 = 1,300 → x = **16 m³**. Above ~16 m³, a 20ft container is cheaper (and handling risk is lower).
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. An air shipment: 8 cartons of 80 × 60 × 50 cm, 22 kg each, rate $3.80/kg. Compute the freight.
2. Why would a shipper of 30 m³ choose a 40ft container over a 20ft?
3. List five surcharges that can appear on a container freight invoice and explain one.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Volume per carton 240,000 cm³ ÷ 6,000 = 40 kg volumetric > 22 kg actual → chargeable 8 × 40 = 320 kg → **$1,216**.
2. A 20ft holds ~33 m³ nominal but ~25–28 m³ usable depending on cargo shape; 30 m³ will not fit comfortably. Also 40ft rates are often well below twice the 20ft rate.
3. BAF (fuel), CAF (currency), PSS (peak season), THC (terminal handling), war-risk/emergency surcharges, low-sulphur surcharge, EU ETS surcharge, equipment imbalance. Example: BAF adjusts freight for changes in bunker fuel prices, usually recalculated monthly or quarterly.
:::
