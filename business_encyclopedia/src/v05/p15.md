Every business decision carries risk. Insurance is one way to deal with it — by transferring certain losses to an insurer for a premium — but it is only one of four basic responses: avoid, reduce, transfer, or retain. This part explains how insurance works, the commercial lines most businesses buy, how enterprise risk management organises all risks, and how four different businesses — an importer, a logistics company, a manufacturer and a commodity trader — make concrete coverage decisions.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part XV
- Explain the mechanics of insurance: premiums, deductibles, limits, exclusions, underwriting, claims and reinsurance.
- Describe the main commercial insurance lines and what each does and does not cover.
- Apply enterprise risk management: identification, assessment, treatment, registers, controls and business continuity.
- Decide which risks to insure, reduce, transfer contractually or retain, for different business models.
:::

# Insurance Fundamentals

:::obj In this chapter
The principles of insurance contracts, how premiums are set, the structure of a policy, the claims process, and the roles of brokers and reinsurers.
:::

## How insurance works

Insurance pools risks: many policyholders pay premiums; the few who suffer insured losses are compensated. It works best for risks that are **numerous, independent, measurable, accidental and not catastrophic for the insurer**. Uninsurable or hard-to-insure risks include ordinary business risks (losing customers, bad strategy), deliberate acts, and risks that are certain to happen.

## Core principles (common-law tradition; local laws vary)

| Principle | Meaning | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Insurable interest | The insured must suffer a financial loss if the event occurs | You cannot insure a competitor’s warehouse |
| Utmost good faith / duty of disclosure | Material facts must be disclosed to the insurer | Non-disclosure can void cover; some jurisdictions now apply a “fair presentation” duty |
| Indemnity | Compensation restores the insured’s position, no profit | Payment limited to actual loss (except agreed-value policies) |
| Contribution | Several policies covering the same loss share it | No double recovery |
| Subrogation | Insurer takes over the insured’s rights against third parties | Don’t waive rights against carriers or contractors without insurer consent |
| Proximate cause | The dominant cause of loss determines cover | Disputes when several causes combine |

## Anatomy of a policy

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Schedule / declarations | Insured, period, sum insured, limits, premium, deductible |
| Insuring clause | What is covered (perils, property, liabilities) |
| Definitions | Meaning of key terms |
| Exclusions | What is not covered |
| Conditions | Obligations of the insured (maintenance, security, notification, cooperation) |
| Warranties (insurance sense) | Promises that must be strictly complied with (e.g., sprinklers maintained); breach consequences vary by law |
| Endorsements | Modifications to the standard wording |
| Limits and sub-limits | Maximum payable; specific caps for certain losses |
| Deductible / excess / self-insured retention | Insured’s share of each loss |

## Pricing: premiums

Premium = expected losses + expenses + cost of capital + profit, adjusted for market conditions (insurance markets alternate between **soft** markets — ample capacity, falling prices — and **hard** markets — scarce capacity, rising prices and stricter terms).

:::example Premium logic (fictional property risk)
Warehouse and stock value $10m. Insurer’s data: annual probability of a fire loss 0.4%, average severity 40% of value → expected loss = 0.4% × 40% × 10m = **$16,000**. Adding expenses (30%), capital and profit loading → premium ≈ $25,000–30,000 (rate ≈ 0.25–0.30%). Sprinklers and good housekeeping may halve the probability, reducing the premium; a deductible of $50,000 removes frequent small losses and lowers it further.
:::

## Underwriting

Underwriters evaluate: nature of the business, loss history (usually 5 years), risk surveys (fire protection, security), financial strength, contracts (liabilities assumed), geography (natural catastrophe exposure), management quality. Better information and risk management lead to better terms.

## Claims

1. **Notify** promptly per policy conditions.
2. **Mitigate** further loss (the insured must act as if uninsured).
3. **Preserve evidence**; do not admit liability to third parties without insurer consent.
4. **Loss adjuster** investigates cause and quantum.
5. **Document**: invoices, inventory records, accounts, photos, surveys, police reports.
6. **Settlement**: indemnity minus deductible, subject to limits, average (underinsurance) and exclusions.
7. **Subrogation**: insurer pursues responsible parties.

:::warn Underinsurance and the average clause
If a property is insured for less than its value, many policies reduce claims proportionally. Stock worth $2m insured for $1.5m; loss $400,000 → payment = 400,000 × 1.5/2.0 = $300,000 (before deductible). Keep sums insured updated, especially in inflation or with seasonal stock peaks (use declaration or seasonal-increase clauses).
:::

## Intermediaries and reinsurance

- **Brokers** represent the insured, design programmes, place risks with insurers, and assist with claims; paid by commission or fees.
- **Agents** represent insurers.
- **Reinsurers** insure insurers (treaty or facultative), enabling local insurers to write large risks.
- **Captive insurers** are insurance companies owned by a corporate group to insure its own risks — used by large groups to retain predictable risks efficiently and access reinsurance.
- **Takaful** is a Sharia-compliant cooperative alternative: participants contribute to a fund managed by an operator under wakala or mudaraba models.

:::ar مبادئ التأمين
يقوم التأمين على تجميع المخاطر: يدفع كثيرون أقساطاً صغيرة لتعويض القلة الذين يتعرضون لخسائر مؤمن عليها. ومن مبادئه: المصلحة التأمينية، ومنتهى حسن النية والإفصاح عن الوقائع الجوهرية، والتعويض بقدر الخسارة دون ربح، والحلول (حق شركة التأمين في الرجوع على المتسبب). وتتكون الوثيقة من نطاق التغطية والاستثناءات والشروط والتحمّل وحدود التعويض. ويجب الانتباه إلى قاعدة النسبية عند نقص مبلغ التأمين عن القيمة الحقيقية.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Stock valued $5m insured for $4m; deductible $25,000; fire loss $800,000. Estimate the claim payment if average applies.
2. Why might a company choose a higher deductible?
3. A company signs a contract with a logistics provider waiving all claims against it. What insurance problem may arise?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Proportion 4/5 = 80% → 800,000 × 0.8 = 640,000 − 25,000 = **$615,000** (order of applying deductible and average depends on wording).
2. Lower premium; small losses are predictable and cheaper to self-fund; aligns incentives for loss prevention; may access better terms in a hard market.
3. The insurer loses subrogation rights; if the policy prohibits such waivers or requires consent, the company may prejudice its cover. Check policies before signing waiver clauses, or obtain a waiver-of-subrogation endorsement.
:::

# Commercial Insurance Lines

:::obj In this chapter
The main commercial insurance products, what they cover, key exclusions, and who needs them.
:::

| Line | Covers | Key exclusions / issues | Typical buyers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial property (all risks or named perils) | Damage to buildings, machinery, stock from fire, flood, storm, theft, etc. | Wear and tear, gradual deterioration, war; natural catastrophe sub-limits | Everyone with physical assets |
| Business interruption (BI) | Lost gross profit and increased cost of working after insured property damage | Usually requires a covered physical loss; indemnity period must be long enough; supplier/customer extensions needed for dependency | Manufacturers, retailers, warehouses |
| Engineering / machinery breakdown | Sudden breakdown of machinery; construction all risks (CAR) and erection all risks (EAR) | Wear and tear, maintenance failures | Manufacturers, contractors |
| General / public liability | Third-party bodily injury and property damage | Contractual liabilities beyond law, professional errors, pollution (often) | All businesses |
| Product liability | Injury or damage caused by products | Product recall costs (separate cover), faulty workmanship itself | Manufacturers, importers, distributors |
| Professional indemnity (E&O) | Financial loss to clients from professional negligence | Fraud; contractual guarantees | Consultants, brokers, IT firms, forwarders (as intermediaries) |
| Directors and officers (D&O) | Personal liability of directors/officers for wrongful acts; defence costs | Fraud, intentional illegal acts; prior known claims | Companies of all sizes, especially with investors or lenders |
| Employers’ liability / workers’ compensation | Employee injuries (often mandatory) | Varies by law | Employers |
| Motor fleet | Vehicles: third-party (often mandatory) and own damage | Unlicensed drivers, misuse | Logistics, sales fleets |
| Marine cargo | Goods in transit by sea, air, road; Institute Cargo Clauses (A) all risks, (B), (C) named perils; war and strikes clauses | Insufficient packing, inherent vice, delay, wilful misconduct, sanctions | Importers, exporters, traders |
| Marine hull and machinery | Physical damage to vessels | Wear and tear, unseaworthiness with privity of owner | Shipowners |
| Protection & indemnity (P&I) | Shipowners’ third-party liabilities (crew, pollution, cargo, wreck removal) | Provided mainly by mutual P&I clubs | Shipowners, charterers (charterers’ liability) |
| Freight forwarders’ / carriers’ liability | Liability as forwarder or carrier under trading conditions | Contracted liabilities above standard terms | Forwarders, NVOCCs, truckers |
| Aviation | Hull, liability, cargo for aircraft operators; airport and aviation products liability | War (separate), wear and tear | Airlines, operators |
| Trade credit insurance | Non-payment by buyers (commercial and political) | Disputed debts, sales beyond limits, late reporting | Exporters, distributors, traders |
| Political risk insurance | Expropriation, currency inconvertibility and transfer restriction, political violence, contract frustration | Devaluation (not covered — only inconvertibility), pre-existing issues | Investors, contractors, traders in emerging markets |
| Cyber | First-party costs (incident response, data restoration, BI, ransom where lawful) and third-party liability | War exclusions, poor security warranties, unpatched systems | All digital businesses |
| Crime / fidelity | Employee theft, fraud, social engineering (often sub-limited) | Losses discovered after policy, poor controls | Companies with payment flows |
| Environmental impairment | Pollution liabilities and clean-up | Known conditions | Industrial sites |
| Key person / life | Loss of key individuals | — | Owner-managed businesses, startups |
| Warranty and indemnity (W&I) | Breaches of warranties in M&A | Known issues, forward-looking statements | Buyers/sellers in acquisitions |

:::tip Business interruption — the cover that saves companies
After a fire, the property policy rebuilds the warehouse; the BI policy pays the profits lost and the extra costs (temporary premises, overtime) during the **indemnity period**. Choose the indemnity period realistically (12 months is often too short for rebuilding and recovering customers — 18–24 months is common for manufacturers) and calculate gross profit as the insurer’s wording defines it (often revenue minus variable costs, not accounting gross profit).
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Which policies respond: (a) a customer is injured by a faulty kettle your company imported; (b) your server is encrypted by ransomware; (c) your buyer in another country becomes insolvent owing $300,000; (d) your factory is flooded and closed for 5 months?
2. A forwarder’s standard trading conditions limit liability to 2 SDR per kg. A customer asks the forwarder to accept full value liability. What insurance does the forwarder need?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (a) Product liability; (b) cyber insurance (first-party costs and BI) and possibly crime cover for any fraud; (c) trade credit insurance; (d) property (damage) and business interruption (lost gross profit, extra expense).
2. Forwarder’s liability insurance extended to the higher contractual liability (or the customer should buy cargo insurance — usually more efficient); the forwarder should charge for the extra liability or decline.
:::

# Enterprise Risk Management, Risk Registers, Controls and Business Continuity

:::obj In this chapter
A structured approach to all risks: frameworks, risk identification and assessment, treatment options, risk appetite, registers and heat maps, key risk indicators, internal controls, and business continuity planning.
:::

## Frameworks

**ISO 31000:2018** (risk management guidelines) and **COSO Enterprise Risk Management — Integrating with Strategy and Performance (2017)** are the most widely used frameworks. Both emphasise integrating risk into strategy and decisions rather than maintaining a separate compliance exercise.

## The risk management process

1. **Establish context:** objectives, stakeholders, risk appetite.
2. **Identify risks:** workshops, interviews, process walkthroughs, historical losses, industry studies, PESTLE, supplier and customer mapping.
3. **Analyse:** causes, consequences, likelihood, impact, existing controls.
4. **Evaluate:** compare with appetite; prioritise.
5. **Treat:** choose responses and actions.
6. **Monitor and review:** KRIs, incidents, audits.
7. **Communicate and report:** to management, board, risk committees.

## Risk treatment options

| Response | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Avoid | Stop the activity | Exit a sanctioned market; refuse a high-risk customer |
| Reduce (mitigate) | Lower likelihood or impact | Sprinklers, dual sourcing, controls, training, hedging |
| Transfer | Shift financial consequences to others | Insurance, contracts (indemnities, Incoterms, liability caps), L/Cs, credit insurance, outsourcing |
| Retain (accept) | Bear the risk consciously | Deductibles, self-insurance, minor risks |
| Exploit (for upside risks) | Take advantage of opportunities | Expanding where competitors retreat |

## Assessment: likelihood × impact

| Likelihood | Description | Indicative probability (per year) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Rare | Exceptional | < 2% |
| 2 Unlikely | Could occur | 2–10% |
| 3 Possible | Might occur | 10–30% |
| 4 Likely | Will probably occur | 30–70% |
| 5 Almost certain | Expected | > 70% |

| Impact | Financial (example for a $50m revenue company) | Other dimensions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Minor | < $50k | Minor customer complaints |
| 2 Moderate | $50–250k | Short disruption |
| 3 Major | $250k–1m | Loss of a key customer, regulatory warning |
| 4 Severe | $1–5m | Prolonged disruption, fines |
| 5 Critical | > $5m | Threat to survival, licence loss |

Score = likelihood × impact (1–25); heat map colours guide prioritisation. Note the weakness: high-impact, low-likelihood risks can be under-prioritised by multiplication — boards should review all risks with critical impact regardless of score.

## Internal controls and the three lines

Controls are the day-to-day mechanisms that reduce risk (see {{ch:Internal Controls and Audit}}). The **three lines model** assigns roles: management owns risks and controls; risk and compliance functions set frameworks and challenge; internal audit provides independent assurance to the board.

## Key risk indicators (KRIs)

KRIs are metrics that signal rising risk early: supplier OTIF trend, overdue receivables %, unhedged FX exposure, staff turnover in critical roles, number of system outages, customs holds per month, inventory aged > 180 days. Each KRI has thresholds (green/amber/red) and escalation rules.

## Business continuity management

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Business impact analysis (BIA) | Critical processes, maximum tolerable downtime, recovery time objectives (RTO), recovery point objectives (RPO) for data |
| Continuity strategies | Alternate sites, backup suppliers, remote work, manual workarounds, IT disaster recovery |
| Plans | Crisis management team, communication plan, recovery procedures |
| Testing | Tabletop exercises, failover tests; updates after changes |
| Standards | ISO 22301 (business continuity management systems) |

:::ar إدارة المخاطر المؤسسية
تهدف إدارة المخاطر المؤسسية إلى التعامل المنظم مع جميع المخاطر: تحديدها وتحليلها وتقييمها ومعالجتها ومتابعتها. وخيارات المعالجة أربعة رئيسية: التجنب، والتخفيف، والنقل (بالتأمين أو العقود)، والقبول. ويُوثّق ذلك في سجل المخاطر مع تحديد المسؤول عن كل خطر ومؤشرات المخاطر الرئيسية التي تنذر مبكراً بارتفاعه. أما خطة استمرارية الأعمال فتحدد كيف تستمر العمليات الحرجة عند وقوع أزمة كبيرة.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Score and propose treatments for: “Main IT system (ERP) unavailable for more than 3 days due to ransomware” for a distributor processing 500 orders per day.
2. Give two KRIs for credit risk and two for supply-chain risk.
3. Why can a risk heat map mislead a board?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Likelihood 3 (possible, given ransomware prevalence), impact 4 (lost sales, delayed deliveries, recovery cost, reputation) → score 12. Treatments: reduce (MFA, patching, EDR, offline/immutable backups, segmentation, staff training), prepare (incident response plan, tested restore with RTO < 24 h, manual order process), transfer (cyber insurance with BI), and monitor KRIs (patching backlog, phishing test failure rate).
2. Credit: % of receivables overdue > 60 days; concentration of top-5 customers; credit-limit excesses. Supply chain: supplier OTIF; days of safety stock for critical items; % of spend single-sourced.
3. Scores compress complex risks into one number; multiplication underweights rare catastrophic risks; subjective ratings vary; correlations between risks (one event triggering several) are ignored; it can create a false sense of precision.
:::

# Insurance and Risk Decisions for Four Businesses

:::obj In this chapter
Four fictional case studies showing how risk profiles drive insurance programmes and other risk treatments: an importer-distributor, a logistics company, a manufacturer and an international commodity trader.
:::

## Case 1 — Importer-distributor of consumer electronics

**Profile:** Revenue $60m; imports from Asia (FOB terms); one central warehouse (stock up to $12m before peak season); 400 retail customers on 45-day terms; 120 employees.

| Risk | Decision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Goods in transit (buyer’s risk from loading under FOB) | **Marine cargo open cover**, ICC (A) + war/strikes, all shipments declared monthly; deductible $5,000 | High value, theft-prone goods; frequent shipments |
| Warehouse fire/flood/theft | **Property all risks** with stock declaration clause (peak values), **BI** 18-month indemnity | Single site concentration |
| Customer insolvency | **Trade credit insurance** on top 80 customers; smaller accounts self-insured with tight limits | Receivables ~$7m; concentration in chains |
| Product liability (imported electricals) | **Product liability** $5m limit; supplier contracts with indemnities and supplier insurance certificates | Importer often treated as producer under consumer-protection laws |
| FX (USD purchases, local sales) | Not insurance — **treasury hedging policy** | Market risk |
| Payment fraud | **Crime policy** with social-engineering extension; call-back controls | BEC is frequent |
| Customs penalties | **Retain**; reduce through compliance controls | Generally uninsurable fines |

## Case 2 — Logistics company (trucking, warehousing, forwarding)

**Profile:** 80 trucks, two 3PL warehouses holding customers’ goods, freight forwarding as NVOCC.

| Risk | Decision |
|---|---|
| Liability for customers’ goods in its care | **Carrier’s and warehouse keeper’s liability** insurance aligned with trading conditions (e.g., CMR limits on road; contractual limits in warehousing) — avoid accepting unlimited liability in contracts |
| NVOCC liability under house B/Ls | **Freight forwarders’ liability** / NVOCC E&O, including errors in documents and customs |
| Trucks | **Motor fleet** third-party (mandatory) and own damage on newer vehicles; older trucks self-insured for own damage |
| Warehouses | Property (own buildings/equipment) + **customers’ goods** covered by the customers’ own property insurance as agreed in contracts |
| Employees | Workers’ compensation/employer’s liability per law; medical as required |
| Cyber | **Cyber** cover: tracking systems and customs interfaces are critical |
| Customs bonds | **Customs guarantees** from banks (not insurance) for bonded operations |

Key lesson: the logistics company’s exposure is mostly **liability for others’ goods** — controlled by contract terms (limits, exclusions, time bars) and matched by liability insurance.

## Case 3 — Manufacturer (plastics packaging)

**Profile:** One plant with 12 extrusion lines; revenue $45m; customers in food industry with strict quality requirements.

| Risk | Decision |
|---|---|
| Fire/explosion at plant | Property all risks (reinstatement value), **BI 24 months** (lead time for replacement machines ~10 months + customer recovery) |
| Machinery breakdown | **Machinery breakdown** cover + BI extension for breakdown; critical spare parts stock |
| Product contamination/defect affecting customers’ food | **Product liability** and **product recall** insurance; quality certifications (e.g., food-safety standards) |
| Supplier (resin) disruption | Not insurable cost-effectively → dual sourcing, 4 weeks’ stock, **contingent BI** for named key supplier if available |
| Environmental (spills) | Environmental impairment liability (often excluded from general liability) |
| Directors’ liability (bank-financed, private equity minority) | **D&O** |

## Case 4 — International commodity trader (grains)

**Profile:** Trades 1.2m tonnes per year of wheat and corn between the Black Sea, South America and North Africa/Middle East; uses L/Cs and open account; finances via transactional trade-finance lines.

| Risk | Decision |
|---|---|
| Cargo loss/damage at sea | **Marine cargo** (ICC A or grain-specific trade clauses), including war risk for certain routes; often required by financing banks (assigned as loss payee) |
| Quality/quantity disputes | Contract terms (final at loading per independent inspection), inspection companies, not insurance |
| Charterer’s liabilities (damage to vessel, pollution, cargo claims) | **Charterer’s liability** insurance (P&I-type cover for charterers) |
| Buyer default on open account sales | **Trade credit insurance** and/or confirmed L/Cs; bank requires credit-insured receivables for financing |
| Political risk (export bans, contract frustration, transfer restrictions) | **Political risk insurance** for exposures in high-risk countries |
| Price risk | **Not insurance** — hedging with futures/swaps (Part XI) |
| Fraud (fake warehouse receipts, double financing) | Collateral managers with their own liability insurance; KYC; **crime** cover has limits — mainly controls |
| Sanctions | Compliance controls; insurance will not pay for sanctioned voyages |

:::tip The general pattern
Insurance is efficient for **accidental, measurable and independent** losses (fire, cargo damage, liability claims, buyer insolvency). Market risks (price, FX, interest rates) are managed with **hedging**; strategic and operational risks with **management and controls**; contractual risks with **contract terms**. A professional builds a combined programme and makes sure the pieces fit — for example, that Incoterms, cargo insurance, carrier liability and sales-contract claims procedures align.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. For the importer in Case 1, estimate a suitable BI indemnity period and justify it.
2. The commodity trader is asked by its bank to assign cargo insurance and credit insurance policies. Why?
3. Name three risks that insurance generally cannot cover and the alternative treatment for each.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. 12–18 months: finding and fitting out a new warehouse, restocking (2–3 months’ lead time from Asia), and regaining customers who switched suppliers during disruption; peak-season dependence argues for 18 months.
2. The goods and receivables financed are the bank’s collateral; assignment (or loss-payee clauses) ensures insurance proceeds go to the bank if cargo is lost or a buyer defaults, protecting repayment.
3. Price/FX movements → hedging; loss of market share due to competition → strategy and product development; fines for deliberate regulatory breaches → compliance controls; poor business decisions → governance and analysis.
:::

:::summary Part XV summary
- Insurance transfers accidental, measurable losses for a premium; read limits, deductibles, exclusions and conditions.
- Business interruption, product liability, cargo, credit and cyber covers are often the most important for trading and manufacturing companies.
- ERM organises all risks — avoid, reduce, transfer, retain — with registers, KRIs and clear ownership.
- Different business models need different programmes; align insurance with contracts, Incoterms and financing requirements.
:::
