# Customs, Demurrage, Supply-Chain Finance and Disruption Management

:::obj In this chapter
The operational side of customs clearance, how Incoterms allocate costs and risks in supply-chain planning, managing demurrage and detention, using payment terms and supply-chain finance, and building resilience against disruption.
:::

## Customs clearance in operations

The legal and valuation principles of customs are in {{ch:Customs Clearance, Valuation}}. Operationally, clearance succeeds when:

1. **Documents arrive before the goods** (commercial invoice, packing list, B/L or delivery order, certificate of origin, conformity certificates, permits).
2. **Data are consistent** across documents and with the declaration.
3. **HS classification** has been agreed in advance (ideally with a binding ruling for recurring items).
4. **Duties and VAT are funded** (deposit accounts or guarantees with customs, broker advances).
5. **Product compliance** is completed before shipment (e.g., product registration and conformity certificates in the destination country).
6. The **broker** has authorisation (power of attorney / delegation in the customs system).

Many countries use **single-window** systems and risk-based inspection channels (green: release; yellow: document check; red: physical inspection). Trusted-trader (AEO) programmes reduce inspections.

## Incoterms and cost allocation in supply-chain planning

The procurement team must translate the agreed Incoterms rule into a **cost and responsibility plan**:

| Responsibility | FOB origin port | CFR destination port | DAP buyer warehouse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Book main carriage | Buyer (via its forwarder) | Seller | Seller |
| Insurance | Buyer | Buyer (not required of seller) | Seller (bears risk) |
| Destination THC and DO | Buyer | Buyer (unless included) | Seller |
| Import clearance and duties | Buyer | Buyer | Buyer |
| Inland delivery | Buyer | Buyer | Seller |
| Who controls timing and carrier choice | Buyer | Seller | Seller |
| Who bears freight-rate changes after contract | Buyer | Seller | Seller |

Many experienced importers prefer **FOB/FCA** so that they control carriers, negotiate freight with their volume, and see costs transparently; small importers without logistics capability may prefer **CFR/CIF** or **DAP** from reliable suppliers.

## Managing demurrage and detention (D&D)

| Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|
| Late original documents | Use electronic B/L or telex release once payment is secured; courier documents promptly; present L/C documents early |
| Customs holds | Pre-clearance checks, correct classification, certificates ready |
| Warehouse not ready / no trucks | Plan receiving capacity; book trucks in advance |
| Carrier free time too short | Negotiate extended free time in the service contract (e.g., 14 days combined) |
| Port congestion | Monitor; route via alternative ports where possible |
| Unclear responsibility | Contract clauses allocating D&D caused by each party |

## Supplier payment terms and supply-chain finance

Payment terms shift working capital between buyers and suppliers. Large buyers extending payment terms can push weak suppliers into distress — which then becomes the buyer’s supply risk. **Supply-chain finance** lets suppliers get paid early at a cost based on the buyer’s credit, balancing the buyer’s working capital with suppliers’ liquidity. Other tools: inventory financing, consignment stock, vendor-managed inventory, and dynamic discounting.

## Disruption management and resilience

| Disruption type | Examples | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier failure | Bankruptcy, fire, quality failure | Dual sourcing, supplier financial monitoring, safety stock |
| Logistics disruption | Port congestion, canal or strait closures, carrier failures | Alternative routes and ports, buffer lead times, multiple carriers |
| Geopolitical | Sanctions, conflict, export bans | Screening, diversification of origins, contract clauses |
| Natural events and pandemics | Floods, earthquakes, epidemics | BCP, geographic diversification |
| Demand shocks | Sudden spikes or collapses | Flexible contracts, S&OP, scenario planning |
| Cyber attacks | Carrier or port systems down | Manual fallbacks, cyber requirements for partners |
| Price shocks | Freight rate spikes, commodity spikes | Indexed contracts, hedging, long-term freight contracts |

:::tip Resilience costs money — decide deliberately
Each mitigation has a cost: dual sourcing loses volume discounts; safety stock ties up cash; long-term freight contracts may be above spot in soft markets. A structured approach — map critical items and their single points of failure, estimate impact (lost margin per week of disruption), and buy resilience where the expected loss exceeds the cost — avoids both fragility and waste.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Your main supplier of a critical component is in one city and supplies 90% of your needs. Lost margin if supply stops is $200,000 per week; probability of a 4-week disruption in a year estimated at 5%. Qualifying a second supplier costs $60,000 a year in lost discounts and admin. Should you do it?
2. Containers repeatedly incur demurrage because B/Ls arrive late under L/C payment. Propose two solutions.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Expected annual loss ≈ 5% × 4 × 200,000 = **$40,000** — below $60,000 on expected value. But the impact (a $800,000 loss) may be unacceptable for the company’s liquidity and customer relationships; consider partial measures (qualify a backup supplier without regular volume, hold 3–4 weeks of safety stock), or proceed if risk appetite is low.
2. Ask the supplier to present documents faster and courier originals to the bank by express; request an L/C allowing presentation of one original B/L to the buyer directly (with the rest through the bank) — riskier for the bank; use electronic B/Ls where the banks and carrier support them; or obtain a bank guarantee to the carrier for release before original B/Ls arrive (letter of indemnity backed by a bank — only with careful risk assessment).
:::

# Simulation: Importing from China into the Middle East

:::obj In this chapter
A complete training simulation: supplier quotations and selection, purchase order, payment, freight, insurance, Saudi customs duties and VAT, conformity certification, port charges, inland transport, financing costs, landed cost by product with freight allocation, pricing and sensitivity. **All companies, quotations, rates and regulatory fees are fictional assumptions for training; verify current Saudi customs tariffs, VAT and product-conformity requirements before real use.**
:::

## Background

Najd Home Appliances Trading (fictional), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, wants to import three small-appliance products for its wholesale customers (retail chains and hypermarkets). Functional currency SAR (pegged at 3.75 per USD). Purchases are quoted in USD.

| SKU | Product | Quantity | Carton volume | Carton weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Microwave oven 20 L | 900 | 0.060 m³ | 12.0 kg |
| B | Electric kettle 1.7 L | 2,400 | 0.012 m³ | 1.3 kg |
| C | Air fryer 4 L | 1,000 | 0.045 m³ | 5.0 kg |

Total volume = 54.0 + 28.8 + 45.0 = **127.8 m³** → **two 40ft high-cube containers** (usable ~68 m³ each). Total gross weight = 10,800 + 3,120 + 5,000 = **18,920 kg**.

## Step 1 — Supplier quotations (microwave example)

| | Supplier X (Ningbo) | Supplier Y (Foshan) | Supplier Z (Ningbo) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quoted price | $38.50 FOB Ningbo | $36.90 EXW factory | $41.00 CFR Dammam |
| Add to reach CFR Dammam (per unit) | Freight $1.97 | Inland + export clearance $1.10 + freight $1.97 | — |
| **Comparable CFR Dammam** | **$40.47** | **$39.97** | **$41.00** |
| Payment terms | 30% deposit, 70% against copy B/L | 50% deposit, 50% before shipment | 30% deposit, 70% L/C at sight |
| Lead time | 40 days | 50 days | 45 days |
| Certifications for Saudi market | Has prior conformity registrations | Not yet | Has |
| Factory audit result | Good | Acceptable | Good |

**Decision:** Supplier X — price within 1.3% of the cheapest, better payment terms than Y, shorter lead time, existing conformity experience, and the buyer keeps control of freight under FOB. Suppliers for B and C were selected similarly; for simplicity, assume all three SKUs are bought from Supplier X (a consolidated order).

## Step 2 — Purchase order and payment

| Item | Value (USD) |
|---|---|
| A: 900 × $38.50 | 34,650.00 |
| B: 2,400 × $6.20 | 14,880.00 |
| C: 1,000 × $24.00 | 24,000.00 |

| 30% deposit (day 0) | 22,059.00 |

Controls: verified supplier bank details by phone; PO states specifications, plug type and voltage (Saudi standards), Arabic/English labelling, packing and marking, conformity responsibilities, inspection right, warranty (2% free spare parts), delay penalty (0.5% per week, max 5%), and arbitration clause. Optional third-party pre-shipment inspection: $320 (included below in local costs as “inspection”).

## Step 3 — Freight and insurance

- Ocean freight Ningbo → Dammam (King Abdulaziz Port), all-in incl. bunker: **2 × $2,100 = $4,200** (booked by the buyer’s forwarder; origin THC paid by the seller under FOB).
- Cargo insurance ICC (A) incl. war and strikes: 0.15% × 110% × (FOB + freight) = 0.0015 × 1.1 × 77,730 = **$128.25**.
- **CIF value = 73,530 + 4,200 + 128.25 = $77,858.25** (Saudi customs value basis).

## Step 8 — Pricing

Target wholesale gross margin 30% on landed cost: Price = landed ÷ 0.70.

| | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wholesale price (USD) | 68.26 | 11.37 | 41.61 |
| Wholesale price (SAR, excl. VAT) | 255.98 | 42.64 | 156.04 |

:::warn What happens with naive pricing
If the sales team priced at FOB × 1.30 (a common shortcut), the microwave would sell at $50.05: margin on landed cost = (50.05 − 47.78) ÷ 50.05 = **4.5%**, not 30%. For the kettle, $8.06 → margin 1.2%. Landed-cost discipline is the difference between a profitable import business and a busy one that loses money.
:::

## Step 10 — Sensitivity and risks

| Scenario | Effect on total landed cost | Effect on blended margin at planned prices |
|---|---|---|
| Freight rate doubles to $4,200/container | +$4,200 freight, +$7 insurance, +~$347 duty → **+$4,554 (+5.0%)** | Gross margin falls from 30.0% to ~26.5% |
| 7-day customs hold (demurrage $120/container/day after free time) | +$1,680 | −1.3 pts |
| Supplier delivers 3 weeks late | Missed Ramadan season → potential discounting of 10% on 50% of stock | −~5 pts |
| HS classification of air fryer at 10% instead of 5% | +$1,276 | −1.0 pt |
| 2% of microwaves defective | Supplier provides spares; repair cost ~$600 | −0.5 pt |

Controls: freight contract with fixed rates for the season; documents checklist and pre-clearance; delay penalties and early production slots; advance HS ruling; inspection; warranty clause.

:::summary Lessons from the simulation
- Compare suppliers on a landed basis, not on headline price; payment terms, lead time and compliance capability matter.
- Allocate freight by the driver that consumes it (volume for containers), insurance by value; fixed per-SKU costs hit small shipments hard.
- Duty and VAT must be funded at clearance; VAT recovery affects cash, not cost.
- Price from landed cost; shortcuts based on FOB destroy margins.
- Build the timeline around regulatory steps (conformity certification) and seasonal demand.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. New volumes: A 54, B 57.6, C 45 → 156.6 m³ (would actually require checking container capacity — 136 m³ usable — so a third container would be needed; the simplification shows the method). Using the simplification: B’s volume share = 57.6 ÷ 156.6 = 36.78% → freight 1,544.83; FOB B = 29,760 (value share 33.67% of 88,410) → insurance ≈ 128.25 × (FOB+freight share) ≈ 51.6 (approx.); CIF ≈ 31,356; duty 10% ≈ 3,136; certificates 500; volume-based local costs 36.78% × (910 + 400 + 1,940) = 1,195; value-based ~33.67% × (90 + financing ~2,400) ≈ 838 → landed ≈ 37,025 → **≈ $7.71 per unit** (vs $7.96): fixed costs spread over more units. In reality, check whether a third container is needed — if so, the cost per unit could rise.
2. (a) Volume (or per container); (b) value or per transfer; (c) value; (d) number of declaration lines/SKUs, or value if line counts are similar.
3. Buyer’s own costs for those elements: freight 4,200 + insurance 128 + DO/port/THC 910 + trucking/unloading 1,940 = **$7,178**. DAP at +$6,800 is cheaper by ~$378 and shifts transit risk to the seller (the seller bears risk to Riyadh). However, the buyer loses control of carrier choice and timing, must still clear customs and pay duties (on a CIF value that customs will reconstruct), and DAP pricing may hide destination charges. Check the supplier’s forwarder capability and whether destination THC and DO fees are truly included before accepting.
:::
