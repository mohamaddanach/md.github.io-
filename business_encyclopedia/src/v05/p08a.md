Revenue does not happen by itself. Someone must understand which customers to serve, what they value, how to reach them, how to convince them, at what price, on what terms, and how to keep them. This part covers the commercial engine of a business — marketing, sales, pricing, negotiation, account management and channels — with an emphasis on B2B practice, where most finance and technology graduates will work.

The chapters include practical tools: segmentation grids, qualification frameworks, discovery-meeting guides, sales scripts, emails, a full commercial proposal, negotiation preparation sheets and exercises.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part VIII
- Segment a market, choose targets and define a positioning and value proposition.
- Measure and manage acquisition, conversion, retention and customer economics.
- Run a B2B sales process from prospecting to signature, including discovery, proposals and closing.
- Price, discount and structure commercial proposals that protect margin.
- Prepare and conduct commercial negotiations.
- Manage key accounts, use CRM data, forecast sales and understand revenue operations.
- Choose and manage channels: direct sales, distributors, agents, resellers and partners.
:::

# Markets, Customers and Positioning

:::obj In this chapter
Segmentation, targeting, positioning, branding and value propositions — the strategic choices that precede any sales activity.
:::

## Segmentation

Segmentation divides a market into groups whose needs and buying behaviour differ enough to justify different offers, messages or channels. A good segment is **measurable**, **substantial**, **accessible**, **differentiable** and **actionable**.

| Basis | B2C examples | B2B examples |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic / firmographic | Age, income, household | Industry, company size, ownership (government, private, multinational) |
| Geographic | Region, urban/rural | Country, cluster, free zone |
| Behavioural | Usage frequency, loyalty, channel | Purchase volume, buying process (tender vs direct), switching history |
| Needs-based | Convenience, status, price | Speed, reliability, technical support, credit terms |
| Value | Lifetime value | Profit potential, strategic value |

:::example Needs-based segmentation for a logistics company (fictional)
| Segment | Need | Price sensitivity | Service model | Profit potential |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Large retailers | Reliability, integration, scale | High (tender-driven) | Dedicated key-account teams, EDI/API | Medium (volume, thin margins) |
| Industrial importers | Customs expertise, project cargo handling | Medium | Technical sales, account managers | High |
| E-commerce SMEs | Simplicity, speed, digital booking | High | Online platform, self-service | Medium if automated |
| Pharma | Temperature control, compliance | Low | Specialist team, certifications | Very high |
:::

## Targeting

Choose segments based on **attractiveness** (size, growth, margins, competition) and **ability to win** (fit with capabilities, reputation, cost position). Strategies: **undifferentiated** (one offer for all), **differentiated** (different offers per segment), **concentrated/niche** (focus on one or few segments). Small firms usually win with focus.

## Positioning

Positioning is the place an offer occupies in the target customer’s mind relative to competitors. A positioning statement:

> For **[target segment]** who **[need]**, **[brand]** is the **[category]** that **[key benefit]**, because **[reason to believe]**, unlike **[main alternative]**.

Example: “For industrial importers in Saudi Arabia who lose money on customs delays, Delta Clear is the customs and logistics partner that clears containers in 48 hours, because our licensed brokers and digital pre-clearance work with every port, unlike general forwarders who subcontract clearance.”

## Branding

A brand is the set of associations and expectations attached to a name. In B2B, brand reduces perceived risk (“nobody gets fired for choosing a known supplier”), shortens sales cycles and supports price premiums. Brand assets include name, logo, reputation, certifications, references, thought leadership and the behaviour of every employee customers meet.

## Value propositions

Use the customer’s economics. A value proposition is credible when it is **specific, quantified, relevant to the buyer’s role and provable**.

:::example From features to value (fictional software for freight forwarders)
| Feature | Benefit | Quantified value for a forwarder filing 1,500 declarations a month |
|---|---|---|
| Automatic data extraction from invoices | Less manual typing | 1,500 × 12 minutes saved = 300 hours/month ≈ 2 FTEs ≈ $5,000/month |
| HS-code suggestions with audit trail | Fewer classification errors | Avoided penalties and delays, e.g., 3 fewer incidents × $2,000 = $6,000/month |
| Integration with customs single window | Faster submission | Containers released one day earlier → lower demurrage |
| **Total value** | | **> $11,000 per month vs price $1,500 per month** |
:::

:::ar التجزئة والاستهداف والتموضع
تبدأ الاستراتيجية التسويقية بتقسيم السوق إلى شرائح ذات احتياجات مختلفة، ثم اختيار الشرائح التي تستطيع الشركة خدمتها بربحية وتميز، ثم تحديد المكانة التي تريد أن يحتلها المنتج في ذهن العميل مقارنة بالمنافسين. وعرض القيمة القوي يُعبَّر عنه بأرقام تخص اقتصاديات العميل نفسه: كم يوفر عليه، وكم يقلل من مخاطره، وكم يزيد من إيراداته.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Segment the market for a B2B cleaning-services company in a Gulf city and choose one target segment with justification.
2. Write a positioning statement for a Lebanese olive-oil exporter targeting premium European retailers.
3. Convert two features of a cloud accounting system into quantified value for a 30-person trading company.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Segments: offices (multi-tenant towers), hospitals/clinics, hotels, schools, industrial sites, residential compounds. A plausible target: healthcare facilities — higher willingness to pay for compliance and infection control, recurring contracts, fewer low-cost competitors, provided the company can obtain certifications and trained staff.
2. “For premium European grocers whose customers seek authentic single-origin products, [brand] is the extra-virgin olive oil from century-old Lebanese groves that offers traceable harvest-to-bottle quality, certified by independent labs, unlike blended oils of unclear origin.”
3. Bank-feed automation: reconciliation time cut from 3 days to half a day per month → ~20 hours saved × $25 = $500/month. Real-time multi-currency reporting: faster month-end close (5 days earlier) → management decisions on pricing and stock earlier; avoided FX errors. Quantify against subscription cost.
:::

# Customer Acquisition, Retention and Commercial Metrics

:::obj In this chapter
How customers are acquired and retained, the funnel and its metrics, customer economics (CAC, LTV, payback), retention and churn analysis.
:::

## Acquisition channels

| Channel | B2B examples | B2C examples | Typical economics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outbound sales | Prospecting calls, emails, LinkedIn, events | Door-to-door, telesales | High cost per customer; suits high-value deals |
| Inbound marketing | Content, SEO, webinars, case studies | Search, social content | Lower cost at scale; slower to build |
| Paid acquisition | Search ads, LinkedIn ads | Search, social, display, influencers | Scalable; CAC can rise quickly |
| Referrals and partnerships | Introductions by banks, accountants, forwarders | Referral programmes | Low CAC, high trust |
| Tenders and marketplaces | Government and corporate tenders, procurement platforms | Online marketplaces | Price-competitive; fees |
| Trade shows | Industry exhibitions | Consumer fairs | Expensive; good for relationships in some industries |
| Distributors and resellers | Channel partners | Retail chains | Partner margin; reach |

## The funnel and its metrics

:::example A B2B funnel (fictional SaaS for distributors, quarterly)
| Stage | Volume | Conversion to next |
|---|---|---|
| Website visitors from target accounts | 6,000 | 2.0% |
| Leads (demo requests, downloads) | 120 | 50% |
| Sales-qualified leads (SQL) | 60 | 40% |
| Opportunities (discovery done, need confirmed) | 24 | 50% |
| Proposals sent | 12 | 42% |
| Closed-won | 5 | — |

To win 10 customers next quarter at the same rates, the company needs ~240 leads. Improving the proposal win rate from 42% to 55% would deliver the same result with ~180 leads — often a cheaper lever than buying more traffic.
:::

## Customer economics

:::formula Core customer metrics
CAC = Sales and marketing cost for new customers ÷ New customers acquired
LTV (simple) = Average gross profit per customer per period ÷ Churn rate per period
LTV/CAC — commonly targeted at ≥ 3 in subscription businesses (heuristic)
CAC payback (months) = CAC ÷ Monthly gross profit per customer
Net revenue retention (NRR) = (Starting recurring revenue + expansion − contraction − churn) ÷ Starting recurring revenue
:::

:::example Unit economics (fictional)
Monthly subscription $400; gross margin 80% → $320 gross profit per month. Monthly churn 2%. Sales and marketing spend $240,000 per quarter; 60 new customers.
- CAC = 240,000 ÷ 60 = **$4,000**.
- LTV = 320 ÷ 0.02 = **$16,000**; LTV/CAC = **4.0**.
- Payback = 4,000 ÷ 320 = **12.5 months**.
If churn rises to 3%, LTV falls to $10,667 and LTV/CAC to 2.7 — retention is as important as acquisition.
:::

## Retention

Retention is usually cheaper than acquisition and drives long-term value. Tools:

- **Onboarding:** fast time-to-value; clear success criteria.
- **Customer success / account management:** proactive reviews, usage monitoring, health scores.
- **Service quality:** OTIF, response times, complaint resolution.
- **Commercial structures:** multi-year contracts, volume tiers, loyalty rebates, bundles, switching costs (integration).
- **Voice of the customer:** NPS, satisfaction surveys, churn interviews.

:::example Cohort retention analysis
| Cohort (month acquired) | Customers | Active after 3 months | After 6 | After 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| January | 100 | 91% | 85% | 76% |
| April | 120 | 88% | 80% | — |
| July (discount campaign) | 200 | 72% | 58% | — |

The July cohort, acquired with deep discounts, churns much faster: the campaign bought low-quality customers. Total customer count hides this; cohort analysis reveals it.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Marketing spend $90,000 and sales salaries/commissions for new business $150,000 bring 80 customers. Average gross profit per customer is $250 per month; monthly churn 2.5%. Compute CAC, LTV, LTV/CAC and payback.
2. Starting ARR $2.0m; expansion $0.35m; contraction $0.10m; churned $0.15m. Compute NRR.
3. A funnel converts leads → opportunities at 20% and opportunities → won at 25%. Average deal $40,000. How many leads are needed for $2m of new business?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. CAC = 240,000 ÷ 80 = **$3,000**; LTV = 250 ÷ 0.025 = **$10,000**; LTV/CAC = **3.3**; payback = **12 months**.
2. (2.0 + 0.35 − 0.10 − 0.15) ÷ 2.0 = **105%**.
3. Deals needed = 50; opportunities = 200; leads = **1,000**.
:::

# The B2B Sales Process: From Prospect to Signed Agreement

:::obj In this chapter
A complete B2B sales process — prospecting, qualification, discovery, solution design, proposal, negotiation, closing and handover — with scripts, emails and meeting scenarios.
:::

## The stages

| Stage | Goal | Exit criteria | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Research and targeting | Identify accounts fitting the ICP | Account list with triggers | CRM, industry databases, LinkedIn, trade data |
| 2. Prospecting | Start a conversation | Meeting booked | Calls, emails, referrals, events |
| 3. Qualification | Decide whether to invest effort | Need, budget, authority, timing confirmed | BANT, MEDDICC |
| 4. Discovery | Understand problems, impact, decision process | Documented pains and success criteria | Discovery guide |
| 5. Solution and validation | Design and prove the solution | Demo, pilot, references accepted | Demos, pilots, site visits |
| 6. Proposal | Present offer and business case | Proposal delivered and reviewed | Proposal template, ROI model |
| 7. Negotiation | Agree commercial and legal terms | Agreed term sheet / contract draft | Concession plan, deal desk |
| 8. Closing | Signature | Signed contract, PO | E-signature, approvals |
| 9. Handover and onboarding | Deliver value | Kick-off, implementation plan | Handover document |

## Prospecting

:::dialog Cold call opening (fictional; seller of customs-automation software)
**Seller:** Good morning, Mr. Saleh. This is Rami from ClearPath. I’ll be brief — is this a reasonable moment for two minutes?
**Prospect:** Two minutes.
**Seller:** Thank you. We work with forwarders in Jeddah and Dammam that file more than a thousand declarations a month. Most of them tell us their teams spend hours retyping invoice data and fixing HS-code errors. I’m calling to ask how your team handles that today.
**Prospect:** We have six people doing declarations. It’s manual but it works.
**Seller:** Understood. When volumes peak — say in Ramadan or before year-end — what happens to clearance times?
**Prospect:** They slip. We pay overtime and sometimes demurrage.
**Seller:** That’s exactly what our clients wanted to fix. Would it be worth a 30-minute meeting next week to see whether we could reduce that peak workload? If it isn’t relevant, I’ll tell you so.
**Prospect:** Tuesday morning, then.
:::

Principles: research before contact; respect time; lead with a relevant problem, not product features; ask a question; ask for a small next step.

:::doc Prospecting email (training template)
**Subject:** Clearance peaks at Dammam — quick question

Dear Mr. Saleh,

Forwarders filing over 1,000 declarations a month in the Eastern Province tell us that peak periods create overtime costs and occasional demurrage, mainly because invoice data is re-keyed manually.

At Gulf Freight (fictional), automating data extraction reduced average preparation time per declaration from 14 to 4 minutes and avoided demurrage on most peak-period containers.

Would a 30-minute call next Tuesday or Wednesday be useful to see whether similar results are realistic for your team? If not a priority now, a short reply would help me not to follow up unnecessarily.

Kind regards,
Rami Khoury · ClearPath (fictional) · +966 5X XXX XXXX
:::

## Qualification

| Framework | Elements |
|---|---|
| BANT | Budget, Authority, Need, Timing |
| MEDDICC | Metrics (quantified value), Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion, Competition |

Qualification is about **not wasting resources**: a good salesperson disqualifies early. Signals of poor qualification: no access to decision-makers, no quantified problem, “just collecting quotes”, no timeline, procurement-only contact.

## Discovery meetings

:::tip A discovery meeting guide
1. **Agenda and permission:** “May I ask a few questions about how you handle X today, then show only what is relevant?”
2. **Situation:** current process, volumes, systems, team.
3. **Problems:** what goes wrong, how often, who is affected.
4. **Impact:** cost, time, risk, revenue lost — quantify together.
5. **Desired outcome:** what success looks like in 12 months; how measured.
6. **Decision process:** who else is involved, approvals, budget cycle, procurement steps, timing.
7. **Alternatives:** what else they are considering (including doing nothing).
8. **Next step:** agree a concrete action with a date.
Listen more than you talk (a common guideline is 70/30). Summarise back what you heard.
:::

:::dialog Discovery: quantifying impact (extract)
**Seller:** You mentioned demurrage during peaks. Roughly how many containers were delayed last quarter?
**Buyer:** Maybe 40.
**Seller:** And the average delay and charge?
**Buyer:** Two or three days; around 300 riyals a day per container, sometimes more.
**Seller:** So approximately 40 × 2.5 days × SAR 300 — about SAR 30,000 last quarter, plus overtime. Is that a fair estimate?
**Buyer:** Probably low, if I include customer complaints.
**Seller:** Then the problem is worth at least SAR 120,000 a year in direct costs. If we could cut that by two-thirds, would that justify a project this year?
**Buyer:** If the numbers hold, yes — but the general manager decides.
**Seller:** Could we include him in the next meeting where we validate these numbers with your data?
:::

## Proposals

A strong proposal is a decision document for the buyer’s internal approval, not a brochure. Structure:

1. Executive summary — the client’s objective, recommended solution, value, investment, next steps (one page).
2. Understanding of the situation and needs (in the client’s words).
3. Proposed solution and scope (what is included and excluded).
4. Implementation plan, timeline and responsibilities.
5. Business case — quantified benefits, costs, payback.
6. Commercial terms — prices, payment terms, validity, assumptions.
7. Why us — relevant references, team, guarantees.
8. Terms and conditions (or reference to master agreement).

A full sample proposal appears in {{ch:Pricing, Discounting and Commercial Proposals}}.

## Closing

Closing is the natural outcome of a well-managed process, not a trick. Practical closing techniques:

| Technique | Example |
|---|---|
| Summary close | “We agreed on scope, timeline and price; the remaining step is your signature by the 20th so we can start on 1 March.” |
| Next-step close | “Shall we schedule the kick-off for the week of 4 March?” |
| Option close | “Would you prefer the 12-month or 24-month agreement?” |
| Deadline linked to real events | “The installation team is available in March; after that the next slot is May.” (Only if true.) |
| Trial close (checking readiness) | “If we resolve the data-migration question, is there anything else preventing a decision?” |

:::warn Unethical or counterproductive tactics
Fake deadlines, false scarcity, misrepresenting competitors, or promising what operations cannot deliver may win one deal but destroy trust, create disputes and, in some cases, breach consumer-protection or competition law.
:::

## Handover to delivery

Before signature, sales should confirm with operations that the promised scope, timeline and service levels are deliverable. After signature, a handover document records what was sold, promises made, key contacts, risks and success criteria — preventing the classic “sales sold something we cannot deliver”.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Write three discovery questions to quantify the impact of late deliveries for a construction-materials buyer.
2. A prospect says: “Send me your price list, we’ll compare.” How do you respond?
3. Draft a follow-up email after a discovery meeting summarising pains, value and next steps.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. “How many deliveries arrived late last quarter, and by how long on average?” “What does a one-day delay cost you on site — idle crews, equipment rental, penalty clauses?” “Have late deliveries led to liquidated damages or disputes with your clients? What was the amount?”
2. Acknowledge and redirect: “Happy to. Prices depend on volumes and service levels, so a list may not compare like with like. Could we spend 20 minutes understanding your requirements — then I can send a quotation you can compare accurately with others?” If they refuse, qualify them as low priority.
3. Example: “Thank you for your time today. My summary: your team files ~1,500 declarations monthly; peak periods cause 2–3 day delays on ~40 containers per quarter (est. SAR 120,000/year in demurrage and overtime); success means peak-period clearance within 48 hours. Next steps: (1) you share last quarter’s demurrage report by Thursday; (2) we prepare a validation with your data; (3) meeting with Mr. Saleh and your GM on the 12th. Please correct anything I misunderstood.”
:::
