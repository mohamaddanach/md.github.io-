# Pricing, Discounting and Commercial Proposals

:::obj In this chapter
How professional sellers set prices, govern discounts, structure terms, and write commercial proposals — with a complete annotated proposal.
:::

## Price setting in practice

Prices are set within three boundaries:

- **Floor:** cost (variable cost for incremental deals; full cost for long-run viability).
- **Ceiling:** value to the customer (the next-best alternative’s total cost plus the differentiated value you provide).
- **Reference:** competitors’ prices and the customer’s perception.

**Value-based pricing** captures part of the value created; **cost-plus** ignores value; **competitor-based** ignores both value and costs unless used carefully.

:::example Economic value to the customer (EVC)
A new pump costs a farm $4,000 vs a standard pump at $3,000. It saves $600 a year in diesel over a 5-year life (discount rate 12% → PV ≈ $2,163) and needs $200 less maintenance per year (PV ≈ $721).
EVC = reference price $3,000 + differentiation value $2,884 = **$5,884**. Pricing at $4,000 leaves the farmer $1,884 of value — a compelling offer if the savings are credible and the farmer can finance the higher upfront cost.
:::

## Discount governance

Uncontrolled discounting is one of the largest sources of profit leakage. Good practice:

| Control | Description |
|---|---|
| Price list and floor prices | Approved list; floors by product/segment |
| Discount authority matrix | Salesperson up to 5%; sales manager 10%; commercial director 15%; CFO beyond |
| Deal desk | Reviews non-standard deals: price, terms, credit, legal |
| Give–get rule | Every concession is traded for something: volume, term, prepayment, faster payment, reference, exclusivity |
| Pocket-price reporting | Measures all deductions (rebates, free freight, terms) by customer |
| Commission on margin | Aligns salesperson incentives |

## Payment terms as part of the price

Extending terms from 30 to 90 days on a $1m order at a 10% cost of capital costs about $16,400 — equivalent to a 1.6% discount. Treat terms requests as price requests.

## Anatomy of a commercial proposal

:::doc Commercial Proposal (training template, abridged)
**From:** Delta Clear Logistics (fictional), Dammam · **To:** Al-Waha Industrial Supplies (fictional), Riyadh · **Ref.:** DCL-P-2026-044 · **Valid until:** 30 days from issue

**1. Executive summary.** Al-Waha imports ~1,100 containers a year through Dammam and experiences average clearance of 5.2 days, causing ~SAR 480,000 of annual demurrage, storage and overtime. Delta Clear proposes an integrated customs-clearance and port-trucking service with a 48-hour clearance commitment for compliant shipments. Expected net saving: **SAR 310,000 per year** after our fees, payback immediate. We propose a 2-year agreement starting 1 April.

**2. Understanding of your situation.** [Volumes, ports, product categories, current providers, issues identified in discovery, success criteria agreed with your logistics manager.]

**3. Scope of services.** Customs clearance (classification review, declarations, duty payment facilitation); delivery-order collection; port trucking to your Riyadh dry port facility; pre-clearance document checks; monthly KPI report. **Excluded:** customs duties and VAT (paid on your behalf at cost against funding), inspection-authority fees, storage caused by documents not received 72 hours before arrival.

**4. Service levels.**

| KPI | Commitment | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Clearance within 48 hours of complete documents | 95% of containers monthly | Service credit 5% of monthly clearance fees per percentage point below target (max 25%) |
| Trucking pickup within 24 hours of release | 97% | Delta Clear bears demurrage caused by its delay |
| KPI report | By 5th working day | — |

**5. Implementation.** Weeks 1–2: data mapping and authorisation letters; weeks 3–4: pilot on 50 containers; week 5: full go-live.

**6. Pricing (SAR, excluding VAT).**

| Item | Unit | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Customs clearance per declaration | per declaration | 450 |
| Port trucking Dammam → Riyadh dry port (40ft) | per container | 1,950 |
| Delivery-order handling | per B/L | 150 |
| Duty-payment facilitation | % of amount advanced | 1.0% |

Volume rebate: 3% on clearance fees above 1,000 declarations per year.

**7. Business case.** Current annual cost SAR 3.35m (clearance, trucking, demurrage, overtime) → projected SAR 3.04m; saving SAR 310,000 (9%).

**8. Commercial terms.** Payment 30 days from invoice; duty advances reimbursed within 7 days; prices fixed for 12 months, then adjusted by the change in diesel price index for the trucking component; agreement subject to Delta Clear’s standard terms of service (attached) and limitation of liability per those terms.

**9. Why Delta Clear.** Licensed brokerage at all Eastern Province ports; 28 trucks owned; references available from two industrial importers.

**10. Next steps.** Confirm acceptance by signing the attached order form; kick-off meeting week of 18 March.

*Training simulation — fictional companies and figures.*
:::

Annotations: the executive summary states the client’s objective and the numbers first; the scope lists **exclusions** (where disputes arise); service levels include **remedies**; price adjustment clauses protect against fuel inflation; payment terms and duty advances are separated (advancing duties is financing and carries credit risk).

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A customer requests 120-day terms instead of 30 on $600,000 of annual purchases (evenly spread). Your cost of capital is 9%. What is the annual cost of the request, and what would you ask in return?
2. Your salesperson can approve 5% discounts. A customer asks for 12% for a 3-year contract. Who approves, and what “gets” might justify it?
3. Rewrite a weak proposal opening — “We are a leading provider of innovative solutions” — into a client-focused executive-summary sentence.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Extra receivables ≈ 600,000 × 90/365 = $147,945 → cost ≈ 9% × 147,945 = **$13,315 a year** (≈ 2.2% of sales). Ask for a price increase, a volume commitment, prepayment of part, credit insurance cost-sharing, or supply-chain finance through their bank.
2. Beyond 10% → commercial director (or CFO per the matrix). Gets: 3-year commitment with minimum volumes, prepayment or shorter payment terms, exclusivity in a category, case-study reference, index-linked price review after year 1.
3. “Al-Waha loses an estimated SAR 480,000 a year to clearance delays at Dammam; our proposal reduces clearance to 48 hours and saves about SAR 310,000 a year from the first year.”
:::

# Negotiation in Practice

:::obj In this chapter
Principles of commercial negotiation, preparation, tactics and counter-tactics, cultural considerations, and negotiation exercises with model answers.
:::

## Principles

1. **Prepare more than the other side.** Most negotiations are won before the meeting.
2. **Know your BATNA and walk-away point,** and estimate theirs.
3. **Separate interests from positions.** A position is what someone says they want (“10% discount”); an interest is why (“my budget was cut”, “my boss needs to show savings”).
4. **Expand the pie before dividing it:** trade variables valued differently by each side (price, volume, term, payment, delivery, specification, risk allocation, exclusivity).
5. **Make every concession conditional** (“if you…, then we…”), and make concessions smaller as you approach your limit.
6. **Anchor thoughtfully:** a credible first offer influences the outcome.
7. **Keep relationships intact,** especially in repeated business.

## Preparation sheet

| Item | Our side | Their side (estimated) |
|---|---|---|
| Objectives | Price ≥ $X; 2-year term; 30-day payment | Lower cost; flexibility; less risk |
| Interests | Margin; volume to fill capacity; reference client | Budget pressure; supply security; quality |
| BATNA | Sell capacity to segment B at lower margin | Current supplier at +4% price |
| Walk-away point | $X − 6% with 2-year term | Current supplier’s price |
| Variables and their value to each side | Term (high for us), prepayment (medium), delivery schedule (low cost to us, high value to them) | |
| Concession plan | Order, size, conditions | |
| Questions to ask | What drives your timeline? How do you evaluate suppliers? | |
| Team roles | Lead, analyst, note-taker; who decides | |

## Common tactics and responses

| Tactic | What it looks like | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring | “Our budget is 30% below your price.” | Don’t accept the frame; re-anchor with value and your own reference |
| Nibbling | Small extra demands after agreement | “Happy to look at it as part of the whole package” — trade it |
| Good cop / bad cop | One friendly, one aggressive | Address the real decision-maker; stay on substance |
| Deadline pressure | “Decide today or we go elsewhere.” | Test it; know your BATNA; don’t concede for time alone |
| Higher authority | “My board won’t accept this.” | Ask what the board needs; ask to meet the decision-maker; reserve your own authority too |
| Silence | Waiting for you to fill the gap | Stay silent; summarise |
| Salami (splitting the difference repeatedly) | Small cuts | Concede in decreasing steps; link to conditions |
| Competitor bluff | “Supplier X offered 15% less.” | Ask about scope and terms; compare like for like; hold if confident in value |

## Cultural and regional considerations

Negotiation styles vary by culture and individual. In much of the Middle East, relationships and trust built over several meetings, respect for hierarchy, hospitality, and indirect communication are often important; decisions may be made by owners or senior family members rather than the person at the table. Avoid stereotypes — read the individuals, ask about decision processes, and allow time. In tenders and with government entities, formal procedures and compliance rules override relationship-based flexibility.

## Negotiation exercises

:::exercise Scenario 1 — supplier price increase
You are the procurement manager of a food manufacturer. Your packaging supplier announces a 12% price increase citing resin costs. Annual spend $2.4m. Resin indices rose 15% over 6 months; resin is ~55% of the supplier’s cost. You have one alternative supplier, 6% more expensive than current prices, needing 3 months to qualify. Prepare your position.
:::

:::answer Model approach for Scenario 1
- Justified increase ≈ 55% × 15% ≈ **8.3%**, not 12%.
- BATNA: alternative at +6% after qualification (plus switching costs) — so a settlement between 6% and 8% is realistic.
- Proposal: accept an **index-linked formula** (55% of price moves with a published resin index, quarterly, with a band), apply it retroactively from now (≈ +8%), in exchange for a 2-year agreement, 60-day payment terms and priority supply. Start qualifying the alternative anyway to strengthen your BATNA.
:::

:::exercise Scenario 2 — distributor asks for exclusivity
A European equipment maker (you) is approached by a Gulf distributor asking for 5-year exclusivity in all GCC countries. The distributor has strong relationships in Saudi Arabia but none in Qatar or Oman. What do you offer?
:::

:::answer Model approach for Scenario 2
Offer exclusivity in Saudi Arabia (where it can perform) for an initial 2–3 years, renewable, conditional on **minimum purchase targets**, marketing and service commitments, stock levels, trained technicians, and reporting; non-exclusive rights elsewhere until performance is proven. Include termination rights for missed targets, clear post-termination stock buy-back terms, and check local agency/distribution laws (some jurisdictions give registered agents protection on termination — legal advice needed).
:::

:::exercise Scenario 3 — customer demands 5% discount at renewal
A logistics client representing 8% of your revenue says a competitor offers 5% lower rates. Your gross margin on the account is 14%. What do you do?
:::

:::answer Model approach for Scenario 3
A 5% price cut would reduce gross profit on the account by about a third (5 ÷ 14 ≈ 36%). Ask for the competitor’s offer scope (service levels, free time, liability, payment terms). Present your performance data (OTIF, claims ratio, avoided demurrage). Offer alternatives: a 2% rebate for a 2-year extension and volume commitment, efficiency changes that lower your cost (consolidated pickups, digital documents), or adjust service levels. Decide in advance your walk-away: losing the account may be better than serving it at near-zero margin.
:::

# Account Management, CRM, Sales Forecasting and Revenue Operations

:::obj In this chapter
Managing and growing existing customers, key account management, CRM systems and data, sales forecasting methods, and the revenue operations function.
:::

## Key account management (KAM)

Key accounts are selected for strategic importance, not just size: revenue and profit potential, growth, reference value, strategic fit. KAM practices:

- **Account plan:** customer’s strategy and priorities, our share of wallet, growth opportunities, risks, relationship map, actions, targets.
- **Relationship mapping:** multiple contacts at multiple levels (“multi-threading”), not a single point of contact.
- **Joint business reviews (QBRs):** performance against KPIs, issues, improvement plans, upcoming needs.
- **Value tracking:** document savings or benefits delivered — used at renewal.
- **Risk monitoring:** credit exposure, concentration, competitor activity, contract expiry.

## CRM systems

A CRM records accounts, contacts, activities, opportunities, quotes, contracts and service cases. Value comes from **data discipline**: opportunities with correct stages, values and close dates; activities logged; one record per account. CRM data feeds pipeline reports, forecasts and incentive calculations; poor data produces poor forecasts.

## Sales forecasting

| Method | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weighted pipeline | Σ opportunity value × stage probability | Simple, systematic | Probabilities generic; stale deals inflate |
| Commit / best case / pipeline | Salespeople categorise deals | Uses judgement | Optimism or sandbagging |
| Historical run-rate / time series | Extrapolate past patterns | Good for transactional, recurring sales | Misses structural change |
| Driver-based | Leads × conversion × deal size; customers × usage | Links to activity | Needs reliable conversion data |
| Cohort-based (recurring revenue) | Retained revenue + new bookings | Fits subscriptions | Needs cohort data |

:::example Weighted pipeline forecast (fictional quarter)
| Stage | Probability | Pipeline value | Weighted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qualified | 10% | $2.0m | $0.20m |
| Discovery complete | 25% | $1.6m | $0.40m |
| Proposal | 50% | $1.0m | $0.50m |
| Negotiation | 75% | $0.8m | $0.60m |
| Verbal commit | 90% | $0.4m | $0.36m |
| **Total** | | **$5.8m** | **$2.06m** |
Target $2.4m → gap $0.34m. Actions: accelerate the negotiation-stage deals, add pipeline, or adjust the plan. Historical accuracy should be checked: if the team usually closes only 80% of weighted pipeline, forecast ≈ $1.65m.
:::

## Revenue operations (RevOps)

RevOps aligns marketing, sales and customer success operations: shared definitions (what is an SQL?), systems (CRM, marketing automation, CPQ — configure, price, quote), data quality, territory and quota design, incentive compensation administration, forecasting process and analytics. For a finance–CS professional, RevOps is a natural bridge role.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Build a weighted forecast: opportunities of $300k (proposal, 50%), $500k (discovery, 25%), $200k (negotiation, 75%), $150k (verbal, 90%). Compute the total.
2. List five elements of a key account plan.
3. Why do salespeople sometimes “sandbag” forecasts, and how can management reduce it?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. 150 + 125 + 150 + 135 = **$560k**.
2. Customer objectives and strategy; current revenue, margin and share of wallet; relationship map; growth opportunities with values and timing; risks and mitigation; action plan with owners and dates; KPIs and review schedule.
3. To beat targets and protect bonuses (low forecasts are easier to exceed). Reduce it with forecast-accuracy metrics, incentive designs that reward accurate forecasting, deal inspection based on evidence (buyer actions, not seller feelings), and data-driven stage probabilities.
:::

# Channels and Go-to-Market: B2B, B2C, Enterprise, Distributors, Agents, Resellers and Partners

:::obj In this chapter
Differences between B2B, B2C and enterprise selling; indirect channels and their agreements; how to choose and manage channel partners.
:::

## B2B vs B2C vs enterprise

| Dimension | B2C | SMB B2B | Enterprise B2B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision-makers | Individual/household | Owner or small team | Buying committee (5–15 people), procurement, legal |
| Sales cycle | Minutes to days | Weeks | Months to over a year |
| Deal size | Small | Small–medium | Large |
| Channel | Retail, e-commerce, apps | Inside sales, partners, online | Field sales, key account teams, partners |
| Pricing | List prices, promotions | Standard tiers | Negotiated, custom contracts |
| Contract | Terms of sale (consumer law) | Standard terms | Master agreements, SLAs, security reviews |
| Key metrics | Conversion, basket, retention | CAC, churn | Pipeline coverage, win rate, NRR |

## Indirect channels

| Channel partner | Role | Owns goods? | Earns | Key agreement terms |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agent (commercial agent) | Finds customers; may negotiate/contract for principal | No | Commission | Territory, commission, exclusivity, termination compensation (some laws) |
| Distributor | Buys, stocks, resells, often provides service | Yes | Margin | Territory, exclusivity, targets, pricing freedom (competition law), stock, service, IP use, termination and buy-back |
| Reseller / VAR | Resells (often software/IT), may add services | Usually | Margin or discount | Partner tier, certifications, deal registration |
| Wholesaler | Buys in bulk, sells to retailers | Yes | Margin | Volumes, terms |
| Retailer | Sells to consumers | Yes (or consignment) | Margin | Listing fees, promotions, payment terms |
| Marketplace / platform | Connects buyers and sellers | Usually not | Take rate | Fees, rules, data |
| Referral partner / alliance | Introduces opportunities | No | Referral fee or reciprocity | Fee terms, confidentiality |
| Franchisee | Operates under brand and system | Owns its business | Profit minus royalties | Franchise agreement (Part XIII) |

:::warn Legal points to check for agents and distributors
Many countries have specific laws on commercial agency and distribution — registration requirements, restrictions on termination, compensation for agents on termination (e.g., under EU-derived laws), exclusive registered agent regimes in some Middle Eastern jurisdictions, and competition-law limits on resale price maintenance and territorial restrictions. Use local legal advice before appointing or terminating.
:::

## Choosing direct or indirect

| Factor | Favours direct sales | Favours partners |
|---|---|---|
| Deal size | Large, complex | Small, standard |
| Need for local presence/service | Can afford own team | Partner already present |
| Customer relationship and data | Critical to own | Less critical |
| Speed of market entry | Slower | Faster |
| Control of pricing and brand | High | Lower |
| Cost structure | Fixed (salaries) | Variable (margin/commission) |

## Managing channel partners

Partner selection (market coverage, capabilities, financial strength, reputation, compliance — anti-bribery due diligence is essential where partners deal with government buyers), onboarding (training, materials), joint business plans with targets, incentives (rebates, MDF — market development funds), performance reviews, channel conflict management (deal registration, territory rules), and exit planning.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A software company sells a $3,000/year product to SMEs in 12 Arab countries. Direct or partner model? Explain.
2. A distributor buys at 30% below list and sells at list. An agent alternative would earn 8% commission and the company would sell directly at list. Compare the company’s revenue per $100 of list-price sales and the risks it carries under each.
3. Name three clauses you would insist on in a distribution agreement as the supplier.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Partners (resellers/MSPs) or a digital self-serve model: deal size too small to justify field sales in 12 markets; partners provide local language support, invoicing and relationships. Possibly direct digital sales with local partners for implementation.
2. Distributor: company revenue $70; distributor holds stock, credit risk on end customers and local marketing. Agent: company revenue $100 minus $8 commission = $92 net, but the company bears credit risk, stock, logistics, invoicing and possibly local tax registration. The extra $22 must cover these costs and risks.
3. Minimum purchase/performance targets with termination right; territory and exclusivity scope (and limits consistent with competition law); IP/trademark use and quality standards; confidentiality; payment terms and security (e.g., SBLC); stock buy-back and transition on termination; anti-bribery and sanctions compliance; governing law and arbitration.
:::

:::summary Part VIII summary
- Commercial success starts with choosing the right customers and a quantified value proposition.
- Funnel metrics and customer economics (CAC, LTV, retention) show whether growth creates value.
- B2B selling is a disciplined process: qualify hard, discover deeply, propose for the buyer’s internal decision, and close on clear next steps.
- Price is the strongest profit lever; govern discounts and treat payment terms as price.
- Negotiation is preparation: BATNA, interests, variables and conditional concessions.
- Key accounts need plans and multi-level relationships; forecasts need clean CRM data.
- Channels extend reach at the cost of margin and control; agreements must reflect local law.
:::
