# Microeconomics for Business: Markets, Prices and Competition

:::obj In this chapter
Supply and demand, equilibrium, elasticity, production and productivity, costs, and market structures — applied to business decisions.
:::

## Supply, demand and equilibrium

**Demand** is the quantity buyers are willing and able to purchase at each price; it usually falls as price rises. **Supply** is the quantity producers are willing to sell at each price; it usually rises with price. The **equilibrium** price clears the market.

| Shift | Cause examples | Effect on price and quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Higher incomes, population growth, new uses, lower price of complements | Price ↑, quantity ↑ |
| Demand decreases | Recession, substitutes cheaper, changing tastes | Price ↓, quantity ↓ |
| Supply increases | New capacity, better technology, lower input costs | Price ↓, quantity ↑ |
| Supply decreases | Drought, war, plant outages, export bans, sanctions | Price ↑, quantity ↓ |

:::example Reading a commodity market (fictional numbers)
A drought cuts wheat output in a major exporting region by 8%. Short-run demand for wheat is inelastic (people still eat bread). Price elasticity of demand −0.2 implies price must rise about 8% ÷ 0.2 = **40%** to reduce consumption by 8% if no stocks are released. In practice, stocks, substitution (other grains for feed) and government stock releases moderate the rise — but the example explains why commodity prices spike dramatically on modest supply shocks.
:::

## Elasticity

:::formula Elasticities
Price elasticity of demand: Ed = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP (usually negative; |Ed| > 1 elastic, < 1 inelastic)
Income elasticity: %ΔQ ÷ %ΔIncome (> 1 luxury; 0–1 necessity; < 0 inferior good)
Cross-price elasticity: %ΔQ of A ÷ %ΔP of B (> 0 substitutes; < 0 complements)
Revenue effect of a price change ≈ %ΔP × (1 + Ed) for small changes
:::

Business implications:

- **Pricing:** if demand for your product is inelastic (few substitutes, small share of customer’s budget, high switching costs), price increases raise revenue.
- **Tax and tariff incidence:** when demand is inelastic, the buyer bears most of a tax or tariff; when elastic, the seller absorbs more.
- **Forecasting:** income elasticity tells you which products suffer most in recessions (luxury goods, construction materials) and which are defensive (basic food, utilities).

## Production, productivity and costs

**Productivity** = output ÷ input (labour productivity: output per hour; total factor productivity: output growth not explained by inputs). Productivity growth is the main source of long-run wage and profit growth.

Short run: some inputs are fixed; **diminishing marginal returns** set in. Long run: all inputs variable; **economies of scale** (falling average cost) up to a point, then possible diseconomies (coordination costs).

| Cost concept | Definition | Decision use |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal cost | Cost of one more unit | Pricing floor for incremental orders |
| Average total cost | Total cost ÷ units | Long-run pricing, capacity |
| Average variable cost | Variable cost ÷ units | Short-run shutdown decision: produce if price > AVC |
| Sunk cost | Unrecoverable past spending | Ignore |
| Opportunity cost | Best alternative forgone | Always include |

## Market structures

| Structure | Number of sellers | Product | Barriers | Pricing power | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | Many | Identical | None | None (price taker) | Some agricultural markets |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Differentiated | Low | Some | Restaurants, retail brands |
| Oligopoly | Few | Identical or differentiated | High | Significant, interdependent | Telecoms, cement, container shipping, aircraft manufacturing |
| Monopoly | One | Unique | Very high | High (often regulated) | Utilities, some infrastructure |
| Monopsony / oligopsony | One / few buyers | — | — | Buyer power | Government defence procurement, large retailers vs small suppliers |

**Concentration** is measured by market shares and the **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index** (HHI = sum of squared market shares in %). Competition authorities use HHI levels and changes to screen mergers (thresholds differ by jurisdiction).

:::example HHI
Four firms with shares 40%, 30%, 20%, 10%: HHI = 1,600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = **3,000** — highly concentrated by common thresholds. A merger of the 20% and 10% firms raises HHI by 2 × 20 × 10 = **400** points, which would attract scrutiny in many jurisdictions.
:::

:::ar هياكل السوق وأثرها في الربحية
كلما قلّ عدد المنافسين وارتفعت عوائق الدخول زادت قدرة الشركات على التسعير وتحقيق هوامش أعلى. في المنافسة الكاملة تكون الشركة «آخذة للسعر» ولا تملك قوة تسعيرية، أما في احتكار القلة فتراقب الشركات بعضها وتتأثر قراراتها بقرارات المنافسين. لذلك تهتم سلطات المنافسة بعمليات الاندماج التي ترفع درجة التركّز في السوق.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A 6% price increase reduces volume by 9%. Compute the elasticity and approximate change in revenue.
2. When a government imposes a 10% tariff on imported cement and domestic demand is inelastic, who bears most of the tariff? What happens to local producers?
3. Market shares: 35%, 25%, 15%, 15%, 10%. Compute HHI.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Ed = −9 ÷ 6 = **−1.5** (elastic). Revenue ≈ 1.06 × 0.91 − 1 = **−3.5%**.
2. Mostly domestic buyers (higher prices). Local producers gain pricing power and may raise prices toward the tariff-inclusive import price, increasing their margins.
3. 1,225 + 625 + 225 + 225 + 100 = **2,400**.
:::

# Macroeconomics: Growth, Inflation, Employment and the Business Cycle

:::obj In this chapter
The main macroeconomic indicators, what drives them, how they are published, and how they affect company demand, costs and risks.
:::

## Gross domestic product

:::formula GDP by expenditure
GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
C = household consumption; I = investment (business capex, housing, inventories); G = government consumption and investment; X − M = net exports.
:::

GDP can also be measured by production (value added by sector) and income (wages + profits + taxes less subsidies). **Nominal GDP** uses current prices; **real GDP** removes inflation. Growth is reported quarter-on-quarter (sometimes annualised, as in the US) or year-on-year — always check which.

For business, the **composition** matters more than the headline: oil-exporting economies distinguish **oil GDP** and **non-oil GDP**; a retailer cares about household consumption and wages; a cement producer about construction and government investment.

## Inflation

**Inflation** is the sustained rise in the general price level, measured by indices such as the CPI (consumer prices) and PPI (producer prices). **Core inflation** excludes volatile food and energy. Causes: demand exceeding supply capacity (demand-pull), rising input costs (cost-push), currency depreciation (imported inflation), expectations and wage-price dynamics, and excessive money creation (in extreme cases, hyperinflation).

:::example Inflation and a fixed-price service contract
A facilities-management company signs a 3-year contract at a fixed $1.0m per year. Costs in year 1 are $0.85m (margin 15%). Costs are 70% labour (inflation 6%/yr) and 30% materials (inflation 9%/yr).

| Year | Labour | Materials | Total cost | Revenue | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 595.0 | 255.0 | 850.0 | 1,000 | 15.0% |
| 2 | 630.7 | 278.0 | 908.7 | 1,000 | 9.1% |
| 3 | 668.5 | 303.0 | 971.5 | 1,000 | 2.8% |

An indexation clause (e.g., annual adjustment by a published CPI index, or a formula 70% wage index + 30% materials index) would have protected the margin. Inflation transfers value from those with fixed-price obligations to receive money to those with fixed-price obligations to pay.
:::

Inflation also: raises nominal interest rates; erodes the real value of cash and receivables; inflates nominal profits while understating the cost of replacing assets (historical-cost depreciation is too low); and complicates comparisons over time.

## Employment and wages

The **unemployment rate** = unemployed (seeking work) ÷ labour force. Low unemployment raises wage pressure and staffing difficulty; high unemployment depresses consumption. Many Gulf economies depend heavily on expatriate labour and run localisation (nationalisation) policies — such as quota systems for national employees — that affect hiring costs and planning; requirements must be checked locally.

## Business cycles and recessions

Economies alternate between **expansion**, **peak**, **contraction** and **trough**. Recessions are often triggered by tightening monetary policy, financial crises, commodity shocks, or demand shocks.

| Indicator type | Examples | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Leading | PMI new orders, building permits, yield-curve slope, credit growth, stock prices, consumer confidence | Anticipate turning points |
| Coincident | Industrial production, employment, retail sales, GDP | Confirm current state |
| Lagging | Unemployment duration, inflation, loan defaults | Confirm after the fact |

:::tip How companies prepare for cycles
Experienced CFOs keep liquidity headroom through the cycle, avoid funding long-term assets with short-term debt, prefer variable costs where possible, stress-test budgets for a 15–30% revenue fall, and track leading indicators relevant to their customers (e.g., a building-materials distributor monitors permits, cement sales and government project awards).
:::

## Economic growth in the long run

Long-run growth comes from more labour (population, participation), more capital (investment) and higher productivity (technology, skills, institutions). For investors and companies choosing markets, growth potential, demographics, institutional quality, rule of law, and openness to trade matter more than one year’s GDP figure.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Nominal GDP grew 9% and the GDP deflator rose 6%. What was real growth?
2. A distributor’s sales track non-oil GDP with an elasticity of 1.5. If non-oil GDP growth slows from 4% to 2%, what happens to expected volume growth?
3. In the fixed-price contract example, design a simple escalation clause and show year-3 revenue with it.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. 1.09 ÷ 1.06 − 1 ≈ **2.8%**.
2. From about 6% to about **3%** volume growth.
3. Clause: “Contract price adjusts annually by 70% of the change in the national wage index plus 30% of the change in the materials price index.” Year-3 adjustment factor ≈ 0.7 × 1.06² + 0.3 × 1.09² = 0.7 × 1.1236 + 0.3 × 1.1881 = 0.7865 + 0.3564 = **1.1429** → revenue ≈ **$1.143m**; margin ≈ (1,143 − 971.5) ÷ 1,143 ≈ **15%**.
:::

# Money, Interest Rates, Central Banks and Government Finance

:::obj In this chapter
How central banks influence interest rates and credit; how interest-rate changes reach companies and asset values; fiscal policy and government debt; and what all of this means for business decisions.
:::

## Central banks and their mandates

Central banks issue currency, act as bankers to banks and the government, supervise banks (in many countries), operate payment systems, manage reserves, and set monetary policy. Mandates vary: price stability (e.g., the European Central Bank), a dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices (the US Federal Reserve), or exchange-rate stability (countries with currency pegs).

## How monetary policy works

| Tool | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Policy rate | Rate at which the central bank lends to or remunerates banks; anchors short-term market rates |
| Open-market operations | Buying/selling securities or repos to manage liquidity |
| Reserve requirements | Share of deposits banks must hold at the central bank |
| Quantitative easing / tightening | Large-scale asset purchases (or run-off) affecting longer-term yields |
| Forward guidance | Communication about the expected path of policy |
| Macroprudential tools | Loan-to-value limits, capital buffers, lending restrictions |

**Transmission:** policy rate → interbank and reference rates (SOFR, EIBOR, SAIBOR) → bank lending and deposit rates → borrowing, investment and consumption; also → exchange rate → import prices; → asset prices (bonds, equities, property) → wealth and collateral values; → expectations.

:::framework Pegged currencies and monetary policy
Countries that peg their currency to the US dollar — for example Saudi Arabia (3.75 riyals per USD), the UAE (3.6725 dirhams), Qatar (3.64 riyals), Bahrain, Oman and Jordan — generally move their policy rates in line with the US Federal Reserve to maintain the peg, because free capital movement and a fixed exchange rate leave little room for independent monetary policy (the “impossible trinity”). Kuwait pegs to an undisclosed basket. For companies in these markets, US monetary policy largely determines local borrowing costs. Verify current regimes and rates with the relevant central bank.
:::

## Interest rates and company decisions

:::example A 200 bps rise in rates (fictional company)
A company has $50m of floating-rate debt at reference rate + 2.5%, EBITDA $20m, and is evaluating a project with IRR 11%.
- **Interest cost:** reference rate rises from 3% to 5% → all-in from 5.5% to 7.5% → interest +$1.0m per year; interest cover (EBITDA basis) falls from 7.3× to 5.3×.
- **Hurdle rate:** WACC rises perhaps from 9% to 10.5%; the 11% project’s NPV shrinks toward zero.
- **Customers:** consumers with variable-rate mortgages and businesses with floating debt spend less; demand slows.
- **Valuation:** a company valued at 12× EBITDA may be re-rated to 10× as discount rates rise — reducing the value of equity, and the collateral value of shares for lenders.
- **Working capital:** carrying inventory and receivables costs more; pressure to shorten terms rises across the supply chain.
- **Response:** hedge part of the floating exposure with an interest-rate swap or cap, prioritise high-return projects, accelerate working-capital release.
:::

## The yield curve

The **yield curve** plots government bond yields across maturities. Normally upward-sloping (investors demand more for longer maturities). An **inverted** curve (short rates above long rates) has historically often preceded recessions in the US, as markets expect future rate cuts. For corporates, the curve shape affects the choice between floating and fixed-rate debt and between short and long maturities.

## Fiscal policy and government debt

Governments spend (salaries, subsidies, infrastructure, social programmes), tax, and borrow to fund deficits. Fiscal policy affects businesses directly through government contracts, taxes and subsidies, and indirectly through demand and interest rates.

| Indicator | Why business cares |
|---|---|
| Budget balance (% of GDP) | Large deficits may lead to tax increases or spending cuts; financing pressure |
| Government debt (% of GDP) | Sustainability; crowding out of private credit; sovereign rating |
| Interest burden (% of revenue) | Fiscal space for projects |
| Fiscal breakeven oil price (oil exporters) | Oil price needed to balance the budget; below it, spending and project awards may be cut |
| Government arrears to contractors | Direct cash-flow risk for contractors and suppliers |

:::example Oil price and government spending (fictional country)
An oil exporter’s budget assumes oil at $75 per barrel; its fiscal breakeven is $80. If oil averages $60, revenue falls by roughly 20% of the oil share; the government delays new projects and payments to contractors stretch from 90 to 180 days. A contractor with $40m of receivables from government entities needs an extra $20m of working-capital funding — exactly when banks become more cautious about the sector.
:::

## Money, credit and banks

Most money is created by commercial banks when they lend (a loan creates a deposit). Credit growth fuels expansions; credit crunches deepen recessions. Business indicators: private-sector credit growth, loan-to-deposit ratios, interbank rates and spreads, and central-bank liquidity operations.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A company has $80m of debt: $30m fixed at 6% and $50m floating at reference + 2%. The reference rate falls from 4.5% to 3.5%. What is the change in annual interest?
2. Why do many Gulf central banks follow the US Federal Reserve’s rate decisions?
3. An importer finances inventory with an overdraft at reference + 3%. Rates rise 150 bps; average overdraft $6m; gross margin on goods 18%; inventory days 90. Estimate the margin impact as a % of sales.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Only the floating part changes: $50m × −1.0% = **−$500,000** per year.
2. Their currencies are pegged to the US dollar; with open capital accounts, maintaining the peg requires interest rates aligned with US rates, otherwise capital would flow out (if local rates were too low) or in (if too high), pressuring the peg.
3. Extra interest = $6m × 1.5% = $90,000 per year. Annual sales: inventory $6m at 90 days of COGS → COGS ≈ $24.3m → sales ≈ 24.3 ÷ 0.82 ≈ $29.7m. Impact ≈ 90 ÷ 29,700 ≈ **0.3% of sales** — about 1.7% of gross profit.
:::

# Exchange Rates, the Balance of Payments and International Trade Policy

:::obj In this chapter
What drives exchange rates, how currency regimes work, the balance of payments, trade deficits and surpluses, capital flows, tariffs and non-tariff barriers — with numerical effects on importers and exporters. FX market mechanics and hedging are in Part XVIII.
:::

## Exchange-rate regimes

| Regime | Description | Examples (illustrative) | Business implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free float | Market-determined | USD, EUR, GBP, JPY | Volatility; hedging markets deep |
| Managed float | Market with central-bank intervention | Many emerging markets | Periodic adjustments; intervention risk |
| Crawling peg / band | Gradual adjustments | Some emerging markets | Predictable depreciation |
| Hard peg | Fixed to anchor currency | GCC currencies to USD; Jordanian dinar | Low volatility while credible; risk of sudden devaluation if reserves fall |
| Currency board / dollarisation | Domestic money fully backed or replaced | Hong Kong dollar (currency board); Panama (uses USD) | Minimal FX risk vs anchor |
| Multiple rates / controls | Official and parallel rates; restricted access | Countries in crisis | Trapped cash; pricing complexity |

:::warn Peg risk is real
Pegs can last decades and then fail abruptly when reserves run out. Lebanon’s long-standing peg of about 1,507.5 pounds per dollar collapsed after the 2019 financial crisis; Egypt devalued sharply in 2016 and again in 2022–2024. Companies with local-currency revenue and USD costs or debt suffered severe losses. Assess the sustainability of a peg: FX reserves (months of imports), current-account balance, external debt, fiscal position, and existence of a parallel market rate.
:::

## What moves exchange rates

- **Interest-rate differentials** and expected monetary policy (capital seeks higher risk-adjusted returns).
- **Inflation differentials** (over the long run, **purchasing power parity**: high-inflation currencies tend to depreciate).
- **Current-account balance** and terms of trade (commodity exporters’ currencies often track commodity prices).
- **Capital flows** and risk sentiment (“risk-on/risk-off”; safe-haven currencies).
- **Political and policy uncertainty**, sanctions, capital controls.
- **Central-bank intervention.**

:::formula Parity relationships (approximations)
Relative PPP: expected % change in exchange rate ≈ inflation(domestic) − inflation(foreign)
Covered interest parity: Forward = Spot × (1 + i_quote) ÷ (1 + i_base) (for the period) — this one holds closely in practice because of arbitrage
:::

## The balance of payments

| Account | Components | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Current account | Trade in goods, trade in services, primary income (interest, dividends, wages), secondary income (remittances, aid) | Deficit = country spends more abroad than it earns; must be financed |
| Capital account | Capital transfers, non-produced assets | Usually small |
| Financial account | Foreign direct investment, portfolio flows, loans, changes in reserves | Financing of the current account |

A **trade deficit** is not automatically bad (a fast-growing economy importing capital goods may run one), but deficits financed by short-term, volatile inflows create vulnerability. **Remittances** are a major current-account inflow for countries such as Egypt, Lebanon and Jordan; **hydrocarbon exports** dominate Gulf current accounts.

## Currency moves and company results

:::example Importer and exporter hit by a 20% depreciation (fictional)
A local currency (LCY) depreciates from 10 to 12 per USD (the USD costs 20% more; the LCY has lost 16.7% of its USD value).

**Importer** sells in LCY, buys in USD. Annual imports $5m; sales LCY 70m; other costs LCY 10m.
- Before: COGS = 5m × 10 = LCY 50m → profit = 70 − 50 − 10 = **LCY 10m**.
- After (prices unchanged): COGS = LCY 60m → profit **LCY 0**. To restore profit it must raise prices by about 14% — possible only if competitors face the same cost and demand allows.

**Exporter** sells in USD, costs in LCY. Exports $8m; costs LCY 70m.
- Before: revenue LCY 80m → profit **LCY 10m**.
- After: revenue LCY 96m → profit **LCY 26m** (+160%), unless it imports inputs priced in USD.

**Borrower** with USD debt of $10m and LCY revenue: debt rises from LCY 100m to LCY 120m; leverage ratios jump overnight.
:::

## Tariffs and trade barriers

| Barrier | Description | Business effect |
|---|---|---|
| Ad valorem tariff | % of customs value | Raises landed cost |
| Specific duty | Fixed amount per unit/weight | Hits low-value goods proportionally more |
| Anti-dumping / countervailing duties | Additional duties on unfairly priced or subsidised imports | Sudden large cost increases on specific origins |
| Quotas | Quantity limits | Scarcity; licence value |
| Import licences / registrations | Permission required | Delays; market access barrier |
| Technical standards / conformity | Product certification, labelling | Testing costs; delays |
| Local content requirements | Minimum local value in government contracts | Need for local partners/production |
| Export restrictions | Bans or taxes on exports | Supply shocks for buyers abroad |
| Rules of origin | Determine eligibility for preferential tariffs | Sourcing and documentation |

Free trade agreements and customs unions (e.g., the GCC Customs Union, the Greater Arab Free Trade Area, EU agreements with Mediterranean partners) can reduce duties for goods meeting rules of origin — requiring correct certificates of origin.

:::example Tariff effect on landed cost and sourcing
A product costs $100 CIF from Country A (MFN duty 12%) or $106 CIF from Country B (FTA partner, 0% duty with valid origin certificate).
- From A: duty $12 → **$112**; from B: **$106**. Country B wins despite a higher price — provided the goods qualify under the rules of origin and the certificate is correctly issued.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Inflation is 15% locally and 3% in the US. Under relative PPP, what annual depreciation would you expect?
2. Spot EUR/USD 1.0800; 1-year USD rate 4.5%; EUR rate 2.5%. Compute the 1-year forward.
3. An exporter earns $20m, with USD-priced imported inputs of $6m and local costs of LCY 100m. LCY moves from 8 to 9 per USD. Compute profit before and after in LCY.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Approximately **12%** per year (1.15 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 11.65%).
2. Forward = 1.0800 × 1.045 ÷ 1.025 = **1.1011** (EUR at a forward premium because EUR rates are lower).
3. Before: revenue 160 − inputs 48 − local 100 = **LCY 12m**. After: 180 − 54 − 100 = **LCY 26m**. The natural hedge (USD inputs) reduces, but does not eliminate, the gain.
:::

# Geopolitical, Sanctions, Country and Sovereign Risk — and How Macro Shocks Reach Companies

:::obj In this chapter
How political and geopolitical events, sanctions and sovereign stress affect commercial transactions; how professionals assess country risk; and a framework that links macroeconomic shocks to company financial statements.
:::

## Country risk and sovereign risk

**Country risk** is the risk that conditions in a country — political, economic, legal, social — cause losses for businesses and investors there or trading with it. **Sovereign risk** is the risk that a government defaults on its own obligations, or uses its powers to stop others paying (transfer and convertibility restrictions, moratoria, expropriation).

| Component | Examples | Indicators |
|---|---|---|
| Political | Instability, regime change, conflict, corruption | Governance indicators, election calendars, conflict monitoring |
| Economic | Recession, inflation, devaluation | GDP, CPI, reserves, current account |
| Transfer & convertibility | Inability to convert local currency or transfer abroad | Parallel-market premium, central-bank FX allocations, arrears |
| Legal / regulatory | Weak contract enforcement, sudden rule changes | Rule-of-law indices, arbitration enforcement record |
| Expropriation | Nationalisation, forced contract renegotiation | History, investment treaties |
| Sovereign credit | Government default | Credit ratings, CDS spreads, debt/GDP, bond yields |

Sources commonly used: sovereign ratings from S&P Global, Moody’s and Fitch; OECD country risk classifications for officially supported export credits; export-credit agency country cover policies; IMF Article IV reports and World Bank data; trade credit insurers’ country ratings.

## Sanctions

Sanctions regimes (UN, US, EU, UK and others) restrict dealings with designated countries, sectors, entities, individuals and vessels. Key business consequences:

- **Prohibited transactions:** goods, services, financing or payments involving sanctioned parties.
- **Ownership rules:** under some regimes (e.g., US OFAC’s 50% rule), entities owned 50% or more by blocked persons are treated as blocked even if not listed.
- **USD nexus:** payments in USD clearing through US banks can bring transactions within US jurisdiction.
- **Secondary sanctions:** the US may penalise non-US persons for certain dealings with sanctioned targets.
- **Bank de-risking:** banks may refuse legal transactions involving high-risk jurisdictions, goods or routes.
- **Shipping:** vessel ownership, flag, AIS gaps, ship-to-ship transfers and port calls are screened.

:::warn Practical sanctions controls for a trading company
Screen all counterparties (including owners and banks), vessels and ports; obtain end-user declarations for sensitive goods; include sanctions clauses in contracts (right to suspend/terminate); check export-control classifications for dual-use items; keep records; escalate red flags (unusual routing, reluctance to identify end users, payments from third parties). Get specialised legal advice — sanctions rules change frequently.
:::

## Geopolitical risk in supply chains

Recent years have shown how conflicts and chokepoints disrupt trade: attacks on shipping in the Red Sea from late 2023 forced many carriers to re-route around the Cape of Good Hope, adding roughly 10–14 days to many Asia–Europe voyages and raising freight and insurance costs; the war in Ukraine disrupted grain, fertiliser and energy flows; pandemic-era port congestion caused record freight rates. Companies respond with diversified suppliers and routes, larger buffer inventories, regional sourcing (“nearshoring”), war-risk insurance, and contract clauses addressing re-routing costs.

## A framework: how macro shocks reach the financial statements

| Shock | Income statement | Balance sheet | Cash flow | Who is hurt / helped |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interest rates +200 bps | Higher interest; lower demand from rate-sensitive customers | Lower asset valuations; pension and lease discounting changes | Higher interest paid | Hurt: leveraged firms, real estate, consumers with debt. Helped: cash-rich firms, banks’ margins (initially) |
| Inflation +5 pts | Costs up; revenue up if pricing power; margin squeeze on fixed contracts | Inventory and receivables cost more to carry | Working capital rises in nominal terms | Hurt: fixed-price sellers, lenders at fixed rates. Helped: borrowers at fixed rates, firms with pricing power |
| Local currency −20% | Importers’ costs up; exporters’ revenue up | FX debt up in local terms | Hedging settlements | Hurt: importers, FX borrowers. Helped: exporters, tourism |
| Recession | Volumes down; bad debts up | Impairments | Lower CFO; tighter credit | Hurt: cyclicals. Helped: discounters |
| Oil price −30% | Lower fuel costs; in oil economies, lower government spending | Upstream asset impairments | Lower revenue for producers | Hurt: producers, oil-economy contractors. Helped: airlines, shipping, importers |
| Sanctions on a market | Lost sales; stranded receivables | Write-offs of receivables and assets | Trapped cash | Hurt: exposed exporters and banks |
| Tariff +15% | Landed cost up | Inventory cost up | More working capital | Hurt: importers; helped: domestic producers |

:::ar كيف تصل الصدمات الاقتصادية الكلية إلى الشركات؟
أي تغيّر في أسعار الفائدة أو التضخم أو سعر الصرف أو أسعار النفط ينتقل إلى قوائم الشركة عبر قنوات محددة: الإيرادات (الطلب والأسعار)، والتكاليف (المدخلات المستوردة والأجور)، وتكلفة التمويل، وقيمة الأصول والالتزامات بالعملات الأجنبية، ورأس المال العامل. المهني المتمرّس يحدد هذه القنوات مسبقاً لكل نشاط، ويقيس حساسية الأرباح والنقد لكل متغير، ثم يتخذ إجراءات للتحوط أو التنويع أو تعديل العقود.
:::

## Building a macro sensitivity for your business

1. List the macro variables that matter (e.g., USD/LCY, policy rate, oil price, construction output, consumer spending).
2. For each, estimate the transmission: revenue elasticity, cost share in foreign currency, floating-rate debt, customer sensitivity.
3. Compute the effect of a plausible shock on EBITDA, net income, cash and covenants.
4. Prioritise by impact and probability; define hedges, contract changes and early-warning indicators.

:::example Macro sensitivity table (fictional importer-distributor)
| Shock | Δ EBITDA ($m) | Δ Net income ($m) | Covenant headroom (net debt/EBITDA, limit 3.0×; base 2.2×) |
|---|---|---|---|
| USD/LCY +15%, 50% pass-through to prices after 3 months | −2.1 | −2.4 | 2.9× (tight) |
| Rates +200 bps on $25m floating debt | 0 | −0.4 | 2.2× (EBITDA unaffected; interest cover falls) |
| Demand −10% | −1.8 | −1.5 | 2.7× |
| Combined (all three) | −3.9 | −4.3 | 3.6× (breach) |

Conclusion: the combined downside breaches the covenant. Actions: hedge 60% of forecast USD purchases 6 months forward; fix half of the floating debt with a swap; negotiate a covenant at 3.5× in the next refinancing; introduce price-adjustment clauses with key customers.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. FX reserves in months of imports; parallel-market premium vs official rate; current-account balance and external debt service; central-bank FX allocation backlogs and payment delays; sovereign rating/CDS trend; IMF programme status.
2. Confirmed L/C (from a strong foreign bank), credit insurance including political risk (transfer/convertibility), export-credit agency cover, advance payments, escrow, or payment in an offshore account.
3. (a) Country (transfer and convertibility); (b) counterparty (commercial credit); (c) sovereign; (d) country (regulatory/legal).
:::

:::summary Part V summary
- Supply, demand and elasticity explain price moves and pricing power; market structure explains margins.
- GDP composition, inflation and the business cycle drive demand and costs; inflation punishes fixed-price commitments without indexation.
- Interest rates transmit through financing costs, demand, valuations and working capital; pegs import the anchor’s monetary policy.
- Exchange-rate changes redistribute profits between importers and exporters and can overwhelm borrowers with FX debt.
- Tariffs, sanctions and geopolitical shocks require structured screening, diversification and contract protection.
- Professionals translate macro scenarios into effects on EBITDA, cash and covenants — and act before the shock.
:::
