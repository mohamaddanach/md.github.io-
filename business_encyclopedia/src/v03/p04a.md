Corporate finance answers three questions every company faces: **What should we invest in?** (capital budgeting), **How should we pay for it?** (capital structure and financing), and **How do we manage day-to-day money and return value to owners?** (working capital, cash management and dividend policy). Valuation and M&A apply the same tools to whole companies.

A finance graduate has seen most formulas. This part emphasises application: realistic assumptions, the judgement involved in each input, how practitioners present results, and the mistakes that make models look precise while being wrong. Full step-by-step treatments of every formula, with Arabic explanations, are in Part XX.

# Time Value of Money

:::obj In this chapter
Present and future value, annuities, perpetuities, effective rates, loan payments and the cost of payment terms — the arithmetic beneath every financial decision.
:::

## Core relationships

:::formula Future and present value
FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ    PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ

r = periodic rate; n = number of periods. With m compounding periods per year at nominal annual rate i: FV = PV × (1 + i/m)^(m×t). Continuous compounding: FV = PV × e^(i×t).
:::

:::example Basic calculations
- $10,000 invested at 8% for 5 years: FV = 10,000 × 1.08⁵ = **$14,693.28**.
- $50,000 receivable in 3 years, discount rate 10%: PV = 50,000 ÷ 1.331 = **$37,565.74**.
- Which is better for a seller: $100,000 now, or $106,000 in 9 months, if its cost of funds is 9%? PV of 106,000 = 106,000 ÷ 1.09^0.75 = 106,000 ÷ 1.06677 = **$99,365** → take $100,000 now (ignoring credit risk, which would make the deferred option even worse).
:::

## Annuities and perpetuities

:::formula Annuities and perpetuities
PV of an ordinary annuity (payments at end of each period): PV = C × [1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ] ÷ r
FV of an ordinary annuity: FV = C × [(1 + r)ⁿ − 1] ÷ r
Annuity due (payments at start): multiply the ordinary result by (1 + r)
Perpetuity: PV = C ÷ r    Growing perpetuity (first payment C₁, growth g < r): PV = C₁ ÷ (r − g)
Loan payment (annuity): PMT = P × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ]
:::

:::example Annuities in business
- A lease of $1,000 per year for 5 years at 8%: PV = 1,000 × 3.99271 = **$3,992.71**.
- A business loan of $200,000 over 5 years, monthly payments, 9% nominal annual rate: r = 0.75% per month, n = 60. PMT = 200,000 × 0.0075 ÷ (1 − 1.0075⁻⁶⁰) = 1,500 ÷ 0.36130 = **$4,151.67 per month**. Total paid $249,100; interest $49,100.
- A property yielding $100,000 a year indefinitely, required return 8%: PV = **$1.25m**; if rents grow 3% a year: 100,000 ÷ (0.08 − 0.03) = **$2.0m**. Growth assumptions dominate value — the same logic drives terminal values in DCF.
:::

## Nominal, effective and real rates

:::formula Effective annual rate (EAR) and real rate
EAR = (1 + i/m)^m − 1
Fisher relation: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation)
:::

- A loan at “12% per annum, monthly” has EAR = 1.01¹² − 1 = **12.68%**.
- A deposit at 6% with inflation of 4%: real return = 1.06 ÷ 1.04 − 1 = **1.92%**.
- Banks may quote “flat” rates on consumer and some SME loans (interest on the original principal for the whole term), which roughly double the effective rate on an amortising loan; always compute the effective rate.

:::warn Day-count conventions
Interest for a period = principal × rate × (days ÷ day basis). USD money-market and SOFR loans normally use actual/360; GBP actual/365; bonds often 30/360. On $10m for 90 days at 6%: actual/360 gives $150,000; actual/365 gives $147,945. Know which convention your facility agreement specifies.
:::

## The cost of payment terms

Credit terms are a price. Offering a customer 90 days instead of 30 is a hidden discount; a supplier’s early-payment discount is an investment opportunity.

:::example Should we take the 2/10 net 30 discount?
Paying on day 10 instead of day 30 earns a 2% discount for paying 20 days early.
- Simple annualised return = (2 ÷ 98) × (365 ÷ 20) = **37.2%**.
- Effective annual return = (1 + 2/98)^(365/20) − 1 = **44.6%**.
If the company can borrow at 9% on its overdraft, taking the discount is extremely profitable. Many companies forgo it because of poor cash planning — an expensive habit.
:::

1. Compute the monthly payment on a $500,000, 7-year loan at 8% nominal annual, monthly payments.
2. A customer offers to pay $1,030,000 in 120 days instead of $1,000,000 in 30 days. Your cost of funds is 10%. Which is better (ignore credit risk)?

2. Compare at day 30: PV of 1,030,000 discounted 90 days = 1,030,000 ÷ (1 + 0.10 × 90/365) = 1,030,000 ÷ 1.02466 = **$1,005,210** > $1,000,000 → the deferred offer is slightly better before credit risk; with any material credit risk, the difference is too small to justify it.

4. Paying 45 days early earns 1/99: EAR = (1 + 1/99)^(365/45) − 1 = 1.0101^8.111 − 1 ≈ **8.5%**. If your borrowing cost is below 8.5%, take the discount; if above, pay on day 60.

# Capital Budgeting: NPV, IRR and Investment Decisions

:::obj In this chapter
How companies decide whether to invest: estimating incremental cash flows, applying NPV, IRR, payback and profitability index, and handling the practical complications — inflation, taxes, working capital, terminal values, capital rationing and strategic options.
:::

## The decision rule

A project creates value if the present value of its expected incremental cash flows exceeds the investment — **NPV > 0** — when discounted at a rate reflecting the project’s risk.

:::formula Net present value
NPV = Σ (CFₜ ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ) for t = 0…n
where CF₀ is normally negative (investment).

IRR is the rate r* at which NPV = 0. Profitability index PI = PV of future cash flows ÷ initial investment.
:::

## Estimating incremental cash flows

Include:

- **Incremental** revenue and costs (what changes because of the project).
- **Taxes** on incremental operating profit, including the tax shield of depreciation.
- **Capex**, including installation and commissioning.
- **Working capital** investment at start and as the project grows, and its **recovery** at the end.
- **Terminal / salvage value** after tax.
- **Opportunity costs** (e.g., land the company already owns that could be sold).
- **Side effects:** cannibalisation of existing sales, or synergies.

Exclude:

- **Sunk costs** (feasibility studies already paid).
- **Allocated overheads** that do not change.
- **Financing costs** (interest) — they are reflected in the discount rate, not in cash flows (when using WACC).

:::formula Project free cash flow
FCF = (Revenue − Operating costs − Depreciation) × (1 − t) + Depreciation − Capex − ΔWorking capital
= EBIT × (1 − t) + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
:::

## Worked example: a cold-storage warehouse (fictional)

A food distributor considers building a cold-storage warehouse.

| Assumption | Value |
|---|---|
| Construction and equipment (t = 0) | $2,000,000 |
| Working capital (t = 0, recovered in year 5) | $200,000 |
| Incremental EBITDA (rent savings + third-party storage income − operating costs) | $650,000 per year, years 1–5 |
| Depreciation | Straight-line over 5 years to zero: $400,000 per year |
| Tax rate | 20% |
| Sale value of assets at end of year 5 | $300,000 (fully taxable gain, as tax value is zero) |
| Discount rate (project WACC) | 11% |

**Annual cash flow, years 1–5:** EBIT = 650 − 400 = 250; tax 50; NOPAT 200; + depreciation 400 → **600** ($000).
**Year 5 extras:** after-tax sale proceeds 300 − 60 = 240; working-capital recovery 200 → year-5 total **1,040**.

| Year | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash flow ($000) | (2,200) | 600 | 600 | 600 | 600 | 1,040 |
| Discount factor 11% | 1.0000 | 0.9009 | 0.8116 | 0.7312 | 0.6587 | 0.5935 |
| Present value | (2,200.0) | 540.5 | 487.0 | 438.7 | 395.2 | 617.2 |
| Cumulative cash flow | (2,200) | (1,600) | (1,000) | (400) | 200 | 1,240 |

- **NPV = +$278.7k** → accept on financial grounds.
- **IRR ≈ 15.5%** (NPV at 15% = +30.1; at 16% = −25.9; interpolation).
- **Payback = 3.67 years** (3 + 400/600).
- **PI = 2,478.7 ÷ 2,200 = 1.13.**

:::tip How to present this to an investment committee
“The project requires $2.2m including working capital, generates an NPV of $0.28m at our 11% hurdle and an IRR of about 15.5%, with payback in under four years. The result is most sensitive to occupancy of the third-party storage space: if third-party income is 30% lower than planned, NPV falls to roughly zero. We recommend approval with a pre-letting target of 50% of third-party space before construction starts.”

Decision-makers want the answer, the key risk and the mitigating action — not the spreadsheet.
:::

## NPV vs IRR: when they disagree

IRR is intuitive but has flaws:

1. **Scale:** a small project with high IRR can create less value than a large project with lower IRR.
2. **Timing:** IRR implicitly assumes reinvestment at the IRR.
3. **Multiple IRRs:** cash flows that change sign more than once (e.g., large decommissioning costs at the end) can produce several IRRs or none.
4. **Financing-type flows:** for a cash inflow followed by outflows (a loan), a higher IRR is worse.

:::example Mutually exclusive projects
| | Year 0 | Years 1–4 | IRR | NPV at 10% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Project A | (1,000) | +1,300 in year 1 only | 30.0% | +181.8 |
| Project B | (5,000) | +1,800 per year | ≈ 16.4% | +705.8 |

If only one can be chosen, B creates more value despite its lower IRR. Use NPV for ranking mutually exclusive projects; use IRR as a supplementary communication tool. The **modified IRR (MIRR)** fixes the reinvestment assumption by compounding inflows at the cost of capital.
:::

## Other criteria

| Criterion | Strengths | Weaknesses | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| NPV | Theoretically correct; additive | Requires discount rate; less intuitive | Primary decision rule |
| IRR | Intuitive %; no need to state rate upfront | Scale, timing, multiple IRR problems | Private equity, project finance, communication |
| Payback | Simple; liquidity/risk proxy | Ignores time value and post-payback cash | Screening; uncertain environments |
| Discounted payback | Adds time value | Still ignores later cash flows | Risky markets |
| PI | Useful under capital rationing | Same as NPV for single decisions | Ranking when capital is limited |
| Accounting rate of return | Uses reported profits | Ignores cash and timing | Legacy practice; avoid |

## Practical complications

- **Inflation:** be consistent — nominal cash flows with nominal discount rates, or real with real.
- **Different lives:** compare projects using equivalent annual annuity (EAA = NPV ÷ annuity factor) or a common horizon.
- **Capital rationing:** rank by PI and choose the combination maximising total NPV within the budget.
- **Risk:** adjust the discount rate for project risk (not just company WACC); or use scenario-weighted cash flows; avoid double-counting risk in both.
- **Real options:** the option to expand, delay or abandon has value that static NPV ignores — e.g., building a warehouse with foundations for a second floor.
- **Strategic projects:** “strategic” is not a substitute for analysis; quantify the strategic benefit or at least the cost of not investing.

:::warn Common mistakes in capital budgeting
- Including interest in project cash flows and also discounting at WACC (double counting financing).
- Forgetting working capital or its recovery.
- Using company WACC for a project in a riskier country or business.
- Optimistic terminal values that account for most of the NPV.
- Ignoring cannibalisation of existing products.
- Treating sunk costs as relevant (“we’ve already spent $1m, we can’t stop now”).
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A machine costs $300,000, saves $90,000 a year before tax for 5 years, is depreciated straight-line to zero, tax 25%, discount rate 10%. Compute NPV and payback.
2. Two machines: A costs $50,000, lasts 3 years, NPV $12,000; B costs $80,000, lasts 6 years, NPV $18,000 (both at 10%). Which is better on an EAA basis?
3. Your division has a $10m budget. Projects (investment, NPV): P1 (4, 1.2), P2 (3, 1.0), P3 (5, 1.4), P4 (2, 0.5). Which combination maximises NPV?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Depreciation 60,000; EBIT 30,000; tax 7,500; NOPAT 22,500; cash flow = 22,500 + 60,000 = **$82,500** per year. PV = 82,500 × 3.79079 = 312,740 → **NPV = +$12,740**. Payback = 300,000 ÷ 82,500 = **3.64 years**.
2. Annuity factors at 10%: 3 years 2.48685; 6 years 4.35526. EAA A = 12,000 ÷ 2.48685 = **$4,825**; EAA B = 18,000 ÷ 4.35526 = **$4,133** → **A** is better (if it can be replaced on similar terms).
3. PIs: P1 1.30 (1.2/4 = 0.30 NPV per $), P2 1.33, P3 1.28, P4 1.25. Feasible combinations within 10: P1+P2+P4 (9) → NPV 2.7; P2+P3+P4 (10) → 2.9; P1+P3 (9) → 2.6; P1+P2 (7)… → best: **P2 + P3 + P4 = $2.9m**. (Ranking by PI alone picks P2, P1, then cannot fit P3; checking combinations matters when projects are indivisible.)
:::

# Cost of Capital, Capital Structure and Dividend Policy

:::obj In this chapter
How to estimate the cost of equity, the cost of debt and WACC; how companies choose between debt and equity; and how they decide how much cash to return to shareholders.
:::

## The cost of capital

The cost of capital is the return investors require for providing funds to an investment of a given risk — the opportunity cost of their money. It is a **market-based, forward-looking** concept, not the interest rate a company happens to pay.

### Cost of equity

:::formula CAPM
Ke = Rf + β × ERP (+ country risk premium + size or specific premiums where justified)

Rf = risk-free rate (government bond yield in the currency of the cash flows); β = sensitivity of the stock to the market; ERP = equity risk premium (expected market return above Rf).
:::

:::example Estimating Ke for a fictional Gulf-based logistics company (USD cash flows)
- Rf: 10-year US Treasury yield, assume 4.5%.
- Beta: relevered peer beta 1.2.
- ERP: assume 5.5% (practitioners commonly use 4–6% for mature markets; sources such as academic surveys and published estimates vary).
- Country risk premium: assume 1.5–3% depending on country, e.g., 2.0%.
- Ke = 4.5% + 1.2 × 5.5% + 2.0% = **13.1%**.

Alternatives and cross-checks: dividend discount model (Ke = D₁ ÷ P₀ + g); build-up methods for private companies; investors’ actual target returns (private equity often 20%+ for equity in emerging markets).
:::

### Beta: levering and unlevering

Peer betas reflect peers’ leverage. To use them, unlever to an asset beta and relever at the target’s capital structure:

:::formula Hamada (no debt beta, constant debt)
βL = βU × [1 + (1 − t) × D/E]    βU = βL ÷ [1 + (1 − t) × D/E]
:::

### Cost of debt

The **pre-tax cost of debt** is the yield the company would pay on new long-term borrowing today (market yield on its bonds, or reference rate + credit margin for loans). Interest is usually tax-deductible, so the after-tax cost is Kd × (1 − t). Use the marginal rate of new debt, not the historical coupon.

### WACC

:::formula Weighted average cost of capital
WACC = [E ÷ (D + E)] × Ke + [D ÷ (D + E)] × Kd × (1 − t)
Use target or market-value weights, not book values.
:::

Continuing the example: Kd 7% pre-tax, tax 15% → 5.95% after tax; target weights E 65%, D 35% → WACC = 0.65 × 13.1% + 0.35 × 5.95% = 8.52% + 2.08% = **10.6%**.

:::warn Common mistakes with WACC
- Using book weights, or the current (temporary) capital structure instead of the target.
- Mixing currencies: a USD risk-free rate with local-currency cash flows.
- Using the company WACC for every project regardless of risk.
- Spurious precision: WACC estimates have a range of ±1–2 percentage points; show sensitivity.
:::

## Capital structure: debt or equity?

| | Debt | Equity |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Lower (priority claim; tax-deductible interest) | Higher (residual claim) |
| Obligation | Fixed payments; default if missed | No fixed obligation |
| Control | Covenants, but no ownership | Dilution of ownership and control |
| Risk to company | Raises financial distress risk | Reduces it |
| Upside | Lenders get only interest | Shareholders get all residual upside |
| Availability | Requires cash flow, collateral, track record | Requires investors who believe in growth |
| Signalling | Confidence in cash flows | May signal shares are overvalued (for listed firms) |

### Leverage magnifies returns — both ways

:::example Effect of leverage on ROE (fictional)
Assets $10m; tax 20%; debt cost 8%.

| Scenario | EBIT | Unlevered ROE (equity 10m) | 50% debt ROE (equity 5m; interest 0.4m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Good year | 1.5m | 1.2 ÷ 10 = **12.0%** | (1.5 − 0.4) × 0.8 ÷ 5 = **17.6%** |
| Bad year | 0.5m | 0.4 ÷ 10 = **4.0%** | (0.5 − 0.4) × 0.8 ÷ 5 = **1.6%** |
| Very bad year | 0.2m | 0.16 ÷ 10 = **1.6%** | (0.2 − 0.4) × 0.8 ÷ 5 = **−3.2%** (and interest cover 0.5×) |

Leverage raises returns when return on assets exceeds the cost of debt and lowers them — sharply — when it does not.
:::

### Theories in one paragraph each

- **Modigliani–Miller (1958):** in perfect markets without taxes, capital structure does not affect firm value; the cost of equity rises with leverage to offset cheaper debt. With corporate taxes, debt adds value through the interest tax shield.
- **Trade-off theory:** firms balance the tax benefits of debt against the expected costs of financial distress (lost customers, fire sales, management distraction, legal costs). Stable, asset-rich, profitable firms can carry more debt.
- **Pecking order:** because managers know more than investors, firms prefer internal funds, then debt, then equity as a last resort.
- **Practice:** CFOs target a credit rating or leverage range (e.g., net debt/EBITDA 1.5–2.5×), keep liquidity headroom, match debt maturity and currency to assets, and preserve flexibility for opportunities.

### Hybrid and alternative instruments

Subordinated debt, mezzanine finance, convertible bonds, preference shares, shareholder loans, sukuk (Sharia-compliant certificates) and leases sit between pure debt and pure equity. Their classification (debt or equity) can differ for accountants, rating agencies, tax authorities and lenders.

## Dividend policy and returning cash

| Policy | Description | Typical company |
|---|---|---|
| Residual | Pay out what is left after funding all positive-NPV projects | Theoretically optimal; volatile dividends |
| Stable / progressive | Steady dividend per share growing gradually | Mature listed companies, utilities |
| Target payout ratio | Fixed % of earnings | Banks, many family companies |
| Special dividends | One-off distributions of excess cash | After asset sales |
| Share buybacks | Repurchase shares | Flexible; tax-efficient in some jurisdictions |
| No dividend | Reinvest everything | Growth companies, startups |

Constraints: company law (distributable reserves, solvency tests), loan covenants (dividend lock-ups when leverage is high), regulatory capital (banks, insurers), cash availability at the holding company, tax on dividends and withholding taxes for foreign shareholders.

:::ar سياسة توزيع الأرباح
لا توجد سياسة توزيع واحدة صحيحة لكل الشركات. الشركات الناضجة ذات التدفقات المستقرة توزّع نسبة كبيرة من أرباحها، بينما تحتفظ الشركات النامية بأرباحها لتمويل التوسع. ويجب مراعاة القيود القانونية (الاحتياطيات القابلة للتوزيع) والتعهدات البنكية التي قد تمنع التوزيع عند ارتفاع المديونية، إضافة إلى احتياجات النقد المستقبلية.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Peer equity beta 1.1 at D/E 0.5, tax 20%. Your target D/E is 1.0. Compute the unlevered and relevered beta.
2. Ke 14%, Kd 8% pre-tax, tax 25%, target D/(D+E) = 40%. Compute WACC.
3. A family company earns ROA of 7% pre-tax and can borrow at 9%. The owners want to increase leverage to “boost returns”. What do you tell them?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. βU = 1.1 ÷ [1 + 0.8 × 0.5] = 1.1 ÷ 1.4 = **0.786**. βL = 0.786 × [1 + 0.8 × 1.0] = **1.414**.
2. 0.6 × 14% + 0.4 × 8% × 0.75 = 8.4% + 2.4% = **10.8%**.
3. Borrowing at 9% to earn 7% (pre-tax) reduces ROE and increases risk — negative leverage. (After tax, the comparison is ROA × (1 − t) vs Kd × (1 − t); the conclusion is the same.) Improve operating returns first.
:::

# Working Capital and Cash Management

:::obj In this chapter
Managing receivables, inventory and payables; working-capital policies and financing; the 13-week cash flow forecast; and the economics of releasing cash.
:::

## Why working capital deserves senior attention

Working capital is usually the largest controllable source (or use) of cash in trading, distribution and manufacturing companies. Reducing the cash conversion cycle by 10 days on $200m revenue releases about $5.5m — often more than a year’s net profit for a distributor.

## Working-capital policies

| Policy | Investment in current assets | Financing | Profitability | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | High (large buffers of stock and cash) | Mostly long-term funds | Lower | Lower |
| Moderate (matching) | Moderate | Permanent current assets funded long-term; seasonal peaks short-term | Balanced | Balanced |
| Aggressive | Lean | Heavy short-term funding | Higher | Higher (refinancing and stock-out risk) |

The **permanent** part of working capital (the minimum level needed all year) should be financed with long-term or committed funding; **seasonal** peaks with revolving or short-term facilities.

## Receivables management

Levers: credit policy (who gets credit and how much), terms (30/60/90 days), invoicing speed and accuracy, collections process, dispute resolution, incentives (early-payment discounts), and risk transfer (credit insurance, factoring, L/Cs).

:::example Should we offer an early-payment discount?
Customers pay on average in 75 days. Offer 1.5% discount for payment within 15 days; expect 40% of $50m annual sales to take it.
- Cost: 1.5% × 20m = **$300k** per year.
- Benefit: receivables reduced by 20m × (75 − 15)/365 = **$3.29m**; at a 9% funding cost that saves **$296k**; plus lower bad-debt risk and collection effort.
- Roughly break-even financially; worthwhile only if credit risk or liquidity is a concern, or if funding is constrained. (The implied annualised cost of the discount is about 9.3% simple: 1.5/98.5 × 365/60.)
:::

## Inventory management

:::formula Economic order quantity (EOQ)
EOQ = √(2 × D × S ÷ H)
D = annual demand (units); S = cost per order; H = holding cost per unit per year (financing, storage, insurance, obsolescence — often 15–30% of unit cost per year).
:::

Example: D = 12,000 units, S = $150, H = $4 → EOQ = √(900,000) ≈ **949 units** (about 13 orders a year). EOQ is a starting point; minimum order quantities, container sizes, price breaks and lead-time variability adjust it in practice.

Other tools: ABC analysis (A items = ~80% of value, managed tightly), safety stock and reorder points (see Part IX), vendor-managed inventory, consignment stock, just-in-time, and periodic review of slow-moving stock.

## Payables management

Extending payables is the cheapest finance — until it damages supply. Good practice: negotiate terms rather than paying late; pay on the due date, not early (unless a discount justifies it); centralise payment runs; use supply chain finance so suppliers can get paid early at the buyer’s credit cost.

## Financing working capital

| Instrument | How it works | Suited to |
|---|---|---|
| Overdraft | On-demand limit on current account | Small timing gaps |
| Revolving credit facility | Committed draw/repay facility | Mid-size and large companies |
| Invoice discounting / factoring | Advance of 70–90% of eligible receivables; factoring may include collection and non-recourse risk transfer | Companies with good-quality receivables |
| Borrowing-base facility | Limit = eligible receivables × advance rate + eligible inventory × advance rate | Traders, distributors |
| Inventory / warehouse finance | Loans against stock, often with collateral management | Commodities, distributors |
| Import loans / trust receipts | Bank pays supplier (often under an L/C) and lends for 90–180 days until goods are sold | Importers |
| Supply chain finance (reverse factoring) | Bank pays the buyer’s suppliers early based on approved invoices | Large buyers with many suppliers |
| Pre-export finance | Loan to an exporter repaid from export proceeds | Commodity producers |

:::example Borrowing base (fictional distributor)
| Item | Amount | Eligibility / advance rate | Availability |

## Cash management and the 13-week cash flow forecast

The **13-week cash flow forecast (13WCF)** is a weekly, direct-method forecast of receipts and payments. It is the core tool of treasury in tight periods and is required by lenders in any restructuring.

:::example 13-week forecast excerpt (fictional, $000)
| Week | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | … | 13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening cash | 850 | 610 | 1,020 | 330 | … | |
| Customer receipts | 1,200 | 1,650 | 900 | 1,400 | … | |
| Supplier payments | (1,050) | (800) | (1,100) | (950) | … | |
| Payroll | — | — | (420) | — | … | |
| Rent, utilities, other | (90) | (60) | (70) | (60) | … | |
| Debt service | (300) | — | — | — | … | |
| Tax / VAT | — | (380) | — | — | … | |
| **Closing cash** | **610** | **1,020** | **330** | **720** | … | |
| Facility headroom | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 | … | |
| **Liquidity** | **2,110** | **2,520** | **1,830** | **2,220** | … | |

Practice: update weekly, compare forecast vs actual (variance analysis), and investigate large differences; the accuracy of the forecast becomes a measure of management credibility with lenders.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A company with revenue $120m and COGS $90m has DSO 70, DIO 80, DPO 40. Management targets DSO 55, DIO 65, DPO 50. How much cash is released?
2. Compute EOQ for D = 50,000 units, S = $200, unit cost $25, holding cost 20% per year.
3. Receivables $5m (of which $0.8m over 90 days, and one customer of $1.5m where the concentration limit is 20% of eligible receivables), inventory $3m (slow-moving $0.4m), advance rates 80% and 50%. Compute the borrowing base.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. DSO −15 days × 120/365 = $4.93m; DIO −15 × 90/365 = $3.70m; DPO +10 × 90/365 = $2.47m → total ≈ **$11.1m** released.
2. H = 0.2 × 25 = $5 → EOQ = √(2 × 50,000 × 200 ÷ 5) = √4,000,000 = **2,000 units**.
3. Eligible receivables before concentration: 5.0 − 0.8 = 4.2; 20% limit = 0.84 → exclude 1.5 − 0.84 = 0.66 → eligible 3.54 → × 80% = **2.83**. Inventory: (3.0 − 0.4) × 50% = **1.30**. Base ≈ **$4.13m** (iteration: concentration limits are sometimes applied to total eligible after the excess is removed; conventions vary by facility).
:::
