# Financial Planning, Forecasting and Modelling

:::obj In this chapter
Forecasting methods, the external funding requirement of growth, the architecture of a three-statement financial model, modelling best practice and how analysts use code alongside spreadsheets.
:::

## Planning horizons

| Horizon | Tool | Owner | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3–5 years | Strategic plan / long-range model | CEO, CFO, board | Strategy, capital allocation, financing |
| 12 months | Annual budget | All budget owners | Targets, resource allocation |
| Rolling 12–18 months | Rolling forecast | FP&A | Updated expectation |
| 13 weeks | Cash flow forecast | Treasury | Liquidity |
| Transaction-specific | Deal model / project model | Corporate development, bankers | Investment, lending, valuation |

## Forecasting methods

- **Top-down:** market size × market share.
- **Bottom-up / driver-based:** customers × orders × average order value; capacity × utilisation × price; headcount × productivity. Driver models expose assumptions and are easier to challenge.
- **Percentage-of-sales:** many costs and balance-sheet items scale with revenue — quick but crude.
- **Time-series and statistical models:** moving averages, exponential smoothing, regression, seasonality decomposition; machine-learning models for demand at SKU level (see Part XVI).
- **Judgemental / pipeline-based:** weighted sales pipeline for B2B.

:::tip The professional’s rule for forecasts
A forecast is a set of explicit, defensible assumptions — not a number. Show the drivers, compare them with history and with peers, and identify the two or three assumptions that matter most. “Revenue grows 25%” is weak; “we add 40 hotel customers at an average of $60k per year, consistent with last year’s 35, with churn of 8% as in the last three years” is strong.
:::

## How much external funding does growth need?

:::formula Additional funds needed (AFN), percentage-of-sales method
AFN = (A* ÷ S₀) × ΔS − (L* ÷ S₀) × ΔS − M × S₁ × (1 − payout)

A* = assets that grow with sales; L* = liabilities that grow spontaneously with sales (payables, accruals); M = net margin; S₁ = next year’s sales.
:::

:::example AFN for a fictional distributor
S₀ = $50m; growth 20% → ΔS = $10m; S₁ = $60m. A*/S₀ = 60%; L*/S₀ = 15%; net margin 5%; payout 40%.
AFN = 0.60 × 10 − 0.15 × 10 − 0.05 × 60 × 0.60 = 6.0 − 1.5 − 1.8 = **$2.7m**.
At 30% growth: ΔS = 15 → AFN = 9.0 − 2.25 − 0.05 × 65 × 0.6 = **$4.8m**. The faster the growth, the more external funding is required — banks must be engaged before growth, not after the overdraft is full.
:::

## The three-statement model

A three-statement model links forecast income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement so that every assumption flows consistently and the balance sheet balances.

| Module | Contents |
|---|---|
| Inputs / assumptions | Volumes, prices, cost ratios, working-capital days, capex plan, depreciation lives, debt terms, tax, dividends, scenarios |
| Revenue build | Drivers by segment/product/region |
| Operating costs | Fixed and variable costs, headcount |
| Working capital | DSO, DIO, DPO → balances |
| Fixed assets | Opening + capex − depreciation − disposals |
| Debt schedule | Opening, drawdowns, repayments, interest (often on average balance → circularity), revolver as plug |
| Tax | Taxable profit, tax losses, cash tax timing |
| Equity | Opening + net income − dividends + issues |
| Statements | Income statement, balance sheet, cash flow (indirect) |
| Outputs | KPIs, ratios, covenants, valuation, charts, scenario comparison |
| Checks | Balance check = 0; cash flow ending cash = balance sheet cash; covenant tests |

The usual **balancing mechanism**: forecast all items except cash and the revolving facility; the cash flow statement produces ending cash; if cash would fall below a minimum, the revolver draws (a “plug”); if surplus, it repays.

### Modelling best practice

- Separate inputs (clearly coloured, one location), calculations and outputs.
- One formula per row, consistent across columns; no hard-coded numbers inside formulas.
- Timeline flags (e.g., operating period, construction period) instead of manually edited ranges.
- Sign conventions documented; units in every row label.
- Error checks visible on every sheet; circularity controlled with a switch.
- Version control and change logs; independent model review for large decisions.
- Keep it as simple as the decision allows.

:::warn Common modelling errors
- Balance sheet does not balance and someone adds a “plug” to equity.
- Working capital forecast as a fixed amount while revenue doubles.
- Capex below depreciation for years while volumes grow (assets would wear out).
- Interest calculated on the opening debt only when large drawdowns occur in the year.
- Mixing nominal growth with real costs (inflation inconsistency).
- Terminal year not at a steady state (margins still expanding, capex below depreciation).
:::

## Code alongside spreadsheets

Spreadsheets dominate business communication; code is better for large data, repeated runs and simulation. A finance–CS professional uses both: Python or SQL to prepare data and run simulations, a clean spreadsheet or BI report to communicate.

```python
# Monte Carlo simulation of the cold-storage project NPV (illustrative)
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 100_000
ebitda = rng.normal(650, 90, n)            # $000 per year
capex  = rng.triangular(1900, 2000, 2300, n)
rate, tax, years = 0.11, 0.20, 5
dep = capex / years
annual = (ebitda - dep) * (1 - tax) + dep  # after-tax cash flow
annuity = (1 - (1 + rate) ** -years) / rate
terminal = (240 + 200) / (1 + rate) ** years  # after-tax salvage + WC recovery
npv = -(capex + 200) + annual * annuity + terminal
print(f"mean NPV {npv.mean():.0f}k, P(NPV<0) {np.mean(npv < 0):.1%}")
```

The output is a distribution rather than a single number. With these assumptions the mean NPV is about $0.22m — below the $0.28m base case because the capex risk is skewed upward — and roughly one simulation in five produces a negative NPV. That supports a far richer discussion than a single point estimate.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Using the AFN formula: S₀ $80m, growth 15%, A*/S₀ 55%, L*/S₀ 20%, net margin 4%, payout 50%. Compute AFN.
2. A model shows revenue growing from $20m to $60m in five years with working capital constant at $4m. What is wrong and how would you fix it?
3. Why do many models use the revolving facility as the balancing item?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. ΔS = 12; S₁ = 92. AFN = 0.55 × 12 − 0.20 × 12 − 0.04 × 92 × 0.5 = 6.6 − 2.4 − 1.84 = **$2.36m**.
2. Working capital should scale with activity: model receivables, inventory and payables from days (DSO/DIO/DPO) on forecast revenue and COGS. At constant days, WC would roughly triple to ~$12m, requiring ~$8m of extra funding the model ignores.
3. It mirrors reality: companies fund shortfalls by drawing on committed lines and repay with surpluses; it lets the model close (cash and debt both determined) and shows peak funding needs and covenant pressure.
:::

# Leverage, Break-even, Sensitivity and Scenario Analysis

:::obj In this chapter
Break-even analysis, operating and financial leverage, sensitivity and scenario analysis, simulation, and the main categories of financial risk.
:::

## Break-even analysis

:::formula Break-even
Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ (Price − Variable cost per unit)
Break-even revenue = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin ratio
Target-profit units = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ Contribution per unit
Margin of safety = (Actual sales − Break-even sales) ÷ Actual sales
:::

:::example Fictional furniture manufacturer
Fixed costs $1.2m per year; price $50; variable cost $30 → contribution $20 (CM ratio 40%).
- Break-even = 1.2m ÷ 20 = **60,000 units** = **$3.0m revenue**.
- Current sales 80,000 units → margin of safety = 20,000 ÷ 80,000 = **25%**.
- To earn $0.6m profit: (1.2 + 0.6) ÷ 20 = **90,000 units**.
:::

## Operating, financial and total leverage

:::formula Degrees of leverage
DOL = Contribution ÷ EBIT    DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − Interest)    DTL = DOL × DFL = Contribution ÷ (EBIT − Interest)
:::

Continuing the example at 80,000 units: contribution $1.6m; EBIT $0.4m; interest $0.1m.
DOL = 1.6 ÷ 0.4 = **4.0**; DFL = 0.4 ÷ 0.3 = **1.33**; DTL = **5.33**. A 10% fall in volume reduces EBIT by 40% (to $0.24m) and profit before tax by 53% (to $0.14m).

| Business | Operating leverage | Financial leverage | Combined risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset-light broker | Low | Low | Low |
| Airline | High | Often high | Very high |
| Utility | High | High, but stable regulated revenue | Moderate |
| Software (mature) | High | Low to moderate | Moderate |
| Leveraged buyout of a distributor | Low-moderate | High | High |

## Sensitivity analysis

Change one input at a time and observe the output. Present results as a table or **tornado chart** (inputs ranked by impact).

:::example Sensitivity of the cold-storage project NPV (base NPV $279k)
| Input changed | Low case | NPV ($000) | High case | NPV ($000) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Annual EBITDA ±20% | 520 | (106) | 780 | 663 |
| Capex ±10% | 2,200 | 108 | 1,800 | 449 |
| Discount rate ±1 pt | 12% | 213 | 10% | 348 |
| Salvage value ±$150k | 150 | 207 | 450 | 350 |

EBITDA is the dominant driver; the investment decision should focus on the reliability of third-party storage demand and pricing, not on fine-tuning the discount rate.
:::

## Scenario analysis

Scenarios change several related inputs together to describe coherent states of the world.

| Scenario | Narrative | Probability | NPV ($000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upside | Anchor tenant signs; tariffs rise with food inflation | 25% | 700 |
| Base | Plan as modelled | 50% | 300 |
| Downside | Recession; occupancy 60%; energy costs +30% | 25% | (400) |
| **Probability-weighted** | | | **225** |

Use scenarios to ask: Can the company survive the downside (covenants, liquidity)? What early indicators tell us which scenario is unfolding? What actions (pre-letting, fixed energy contracts, phased construction) improve the downside?

## Financial risk categories

| Risk | Description | Typical measures and tools |
|---|---|---|
| Market risk | Losses from FX, interest rates, commodity and equity price moves | Hedging, matching, limits, VaR |
| Credit risk | Counterparty fails to pay | Credit limits, insurance, collateral, L/Cs |
| Liquidity risk | Cannot meet obligations or refinance | Cash buffers, committed facilities, maturity spread |
| Funding / refinancing risk | Debt matures when markets are closed or rates are high | Early refinancing, diversified sources |
| Operational risk | Process, people, system failures | Controls, insurance, BCP |
| Covenant risk | Breach triggers default | Headroom monitoring, early negotiation |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Fixed costs $900k, price $120, variable cost $75. Compute break-even units and revenue, and units needed for $270k profit.
2. EBIT $2m, interest $0.8m, contribution $6m. Compute DOL, DFL and DTL. What happens to PBT if sales fall 5%?
3. Why might a project with a positive expected NPV still be rejected?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Contribution $45 → BE = 900,000 ÷ 45 = **20,000 units**; revenue **$2.4m**; for $270k profit: 1,170,000 ÷ 45 = **26,000 units**.
2. DOL = 3; DFL = 2 ÷ 1.2 = 1.67; DTL = **5**. Sales −5% → PBT ≈ −25% (from $1.2m to $0.9m).
3. The downside may threaten the company’s survival (liquidity, covenants), the risk may be outside appetite, the capital may be better used elsewhere (higher NPV per dollar), or strategic and execution risks are not captured in the model.
:::

# Business Valuation

:::obj In this chapter
Enterprise value vs equity value; trading comparables; precedent transactions; discounted cash flow valuation with a complete example; valuation of private companies; and how professionals triangulate.
:::

## What is being valued?

:::formula Enterprise value to equity value bridge
Equity value = Enterprise value − Debt − Debt-like items (leases if EBITDA is post-IFRS 16, pension deficits, certain provisions, deferred consideration) − Non-controlling interests − Preferred equity + Cash + Non-operating assets (investments, associates, surplus property)
Equity value per share = Equity value ÷ diluted shares outstanding
:::

**Consistency rule:** enterprise-value multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, EV/Sales) use metrics available to all capital providers (before interest); equity-value multiples (P/E, P/B) use metrics after interest (net income, book equity). Mixing them is a classic error.

## Approach 1: Trading comparables (“comps”)

1. Select peers: same industry, business model, size, geography, growth.
2. Collect market values (market cap, net debt) and financial metrics (LTM and forecast).
3. Compute multiples; examine the range; adjust for differences.
4. Apply the relevant multiple to the target’s metric.

:::example Comps for a fictional regional logistics company (EBITDA $20m)
| Peer | EV ($m) | EBITDA ($m) | EV/EBITDA | Revenue growth | EBITDA margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peer A | 450 | 60 | 7.5× | 6% | 14% |
| Peer B | 820 | 100 | 8.2× | 9% | 16% |
| Peer C | 540 | 60 | 9.0× | 12% | 18% |
| Peer D | 272 | 40 | 6.8× | 4% | 11% |
| Peer E | 1,010 | 100 | 10.1× | 15% | 20% |
| **Median** | | | **8.2×** | | |

Interquartile range 7.5–9.0× → EV **$150–180m**, median $164m. If the target grows faster than the median peer with higher margins, the upper half of the range is defensible; for a smaller, less liquid company, a discount may apply.
:::

## Approach 2: Precedent transactions

Multiples paid in past acquisitions of comparable companies. They include a **control premium** (buyers pay for control and synergies) and reflect market conditions at the time. Weaknesses: limited and dated data, different deal structures, undisclosed terms.

Example: median precedent EV/EBITDA 9.5× → EV ≈ **$190m** for the target above.

## Approach 3: Discounted cash flow (DCF)

:::formula DCF (enterprise value)
EV = Σ FCFFₜ ÷ (1 + WACC)ᵗ + TVₙ ÷ (1 + WACC)ⁿ
Gordon growth terminal value: TVₙ = FCFFₙ × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g)
Exit multiple terminal value: TVₙ = EBITDAₙ × exit multiple
:::

:::example DCF of the same fictional company
Forecast free cash flow to the firm ($m): Year 1: 10.0; Year 2: 11.0; Year 3: 12.0; Year 4: 12.8; Year 5: 13.5. WACC 10%; terminal growth 3%; net debt $40m; 10m shares.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FCFF | 10.0 | 11.0 | 12.0 | 12.8 | 13.5 |
| Discount factor (10%) | 0.9091 | 0.8264 | 0.7513 | 0.6830 | 0.6209 |
| PV | 9.09 | 9.09 | 9.02 | 8.74 | 8.38 |

- Sum of PV of forecast FCFF = **$44.3m**.
- TV₅ = 13.5 × 1.03 ÷ (0.10 − 0.03) = **$198.6m**; PV = 198.6 × 0.6209 = **$123.3m**.
- **EV = $167.7m**; equity = 167.7 − 40 = **$127.7m**; **$12.77 per share**.
- The terminal value is 74% of EV — typical, and a reason to scrutinise long-term assumptions.
- Cross-check with an exit multiple of 8.0× year-5 EBITDA of $20m: TV = $160m; PV $99.3m; EV $143.7m. The Gordon-growth TV implies an exit multiple of about 9.9× — on the high side relative to peers.
:::

**Sensitivity of EV ($m) to WACC and terminal growth:**

| WACC \ g | 2% | 3% | 4% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9% | 173.4 | 196.2 | 228.0 |
| 10% | 151.2 | 167.7 | 189.6 |
| 11% | 134.0 | 146.3 | 162.2 |

Small changes in WACC and g move value by ±20–35%. A DCF is a structured way to think about value drivers, not a precise answer.

## Triangulation: the “football field”

| Method | Low EV ($m) | High EV ($m) |
|---|---|---|
| Trading comps (7.5–9.0×) | 150 | 180 |
| Precedent transactions (8.5–10.5×) | 170 | 210 |
| DCF (WACC 9–11%, g 2–4%) | 134 | 228 |
| DCF central case | 146 | 190 |
| **Indicative range** | **155** | **185** |

The final judgement weighs which methods are most reliable for this company and purpose (a minority investment vs a control acquisition).

## Valuing private companies

Private companies are typically valued with the same methods plus adjustments:

- **Normalisation of earnings:** owner salaries above or below market, related-party rents, one-off items, personal expenses run through the company.
- **Discount for lack of marketability (DLOM)** for minority stakes that cannot be easily sold.
- **Size and specific-risk premiums** in the discount rate.
- **Quality of earnings** review: are profits real, recurring and converting to cash?
- **Working-capital normalisation:** in acquisitions, a target level of working capital (“peg”) is agreed; deviations at closing adjust the price.

:::warn Common valuation mistakes
- Applying EV multiples to net income or P/E to EBITDA.
- Using peers with very different growth, margins or risk without adjustment.
- Forgetting debt-like items (leases, deferred consideration, unpaid taxes, end-of-service obligations).
- DCF terminal growth above long-term nominal GDP growth, or terminal year capex below depreciation.
- WACC in one currency, cash flows in another.
- Treating a valuation as a price: price is what a buyer pays after negotiation.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Market cap $600m; debt $250m; cash $50m; lease liabilities $40m; NCI $30m; investment in associate $20m. Compute EV.
2. A company has net income $12m and EBITDA $30m; peers trade at 14× P/E and 8× EV/EBITDA; net debt $70m. Compute implied equity values from both methods and comment.
3. FCFF in year 5 is $25m; WACC 9%; g 2.5%. Compute TV and its PV (discount factor 0.6499).
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. EV = 600 + 250 + 40 + 30 − 50 − 20 = **$850m** (leases included if EBITDA is post-IFRS 16).
2. P/E: 14 × 12 = **$168m**. EV/EBITDA: 8 × 30 = 240 − 70 = **$170m**. Consistent; if they diverged, check for unusual interest, tax or leverage differences versus peers.
3. TV = 25 × 1.025 ÷ 0.065 = **$394.2m**; PV = 394.2 × 0.6499 = **$256.2m**.
:::

# Mergers, Acquisitions and Corporate Restructuring: The Financial View

:::obj In this chapter
How buyers decide what to pay, how synergies are valued, how deals are financed and structured, accretion/dilution, leveraged buyouts, and the finance of restructuring. Legal aspects are covered in Part XII and integration in Part XIV.
:::

## Value creation in an acquisition

:::formula Value created for the acquirer
Value to acquirer = Standalone value of target + PV of synergies − Price paid − Transaction and integration costs
The control premium paid = Price − standalone market value; the acquirer gains only if synergies exceed the premium plus costs.
:::

| Synergy type | Examples | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Cost synergies | Procurement savings, closing duplicate offices, shared IT | Higher — within management’s control |
| Revenue synergies | Cross-selling, new markets, pricing | Lower — depend on customers |
| Financial synergies | Lower funding cost, tax efficiencies | Varies; scrutinise |
| Dis-synergies | Customer losses, staff attrition, integration costs | Often underestimated |

Buyers typically value synergies net of integration costs, phased in over 2–3 years, discounted, and share only part of them with the seller in the price.

## Deal structures and consideration

| Element | Options | Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| What is bought | Shares (company with all its history) or assets (selected assets/liabilities) | Liabilities inherited; tax; licences and contracts transferability |
| Consideration | Cash, shares, mix, deferred consideration, earn-outs, vendor loans | Risk sharing; seller’s tax; acquirer’s balance sheet |
| Price mechanism | Completion accounts (price adjusted for actual net debt and working capital at closing) or locked box (price fixed on a historical balance sheet with leakage protection) | Certainty vs accuracy |
| Protection | Warranties, indemnities, escrow, W&I insurance | Allocation of unknown risks |

**Earn-outs** link part of the price to future performance (e.g., EBITDA in years 1–2), bridging valuation gaps but often causing disputes over accounting and management control.

## Accretion and dilution

Listed acquirers analyse the effect on earnings per share (EPS).

:::example Accretion/dilution (fictional)
Acquirer: net income $100m, 50m shares, EPS $2.00, share price $30 (P/E 15). Target: net income $20m; price $300m (P/E 15).

- **All-debt financing** at 6% pre-tax, tax 20%: after-tax interest = 300 × 6% × 0.8 = $14.4m. New EPS = (100 + 20 − 14.4) ÷ 50 = **$2.112 (+5.6% accretive)**.
- **All-stock financing** at $30: new shares 10m. New EPS = 120 ÷ 60 = **$2.00 (neutral)**.
- Add after-tax synergies of $4m: debt case EPS $2.192; stock case $2.067.

Rule of thumb: buying a company on a lower P/E than your own with stock is accretive; with debt, the deal is accretive if the target’s earnings yield (1/P/E paid) exceeds the after-tax cost of debt. **Accretion does not equal value creation** — paying too much financed with cheap debt can be EPS-accretive and still destroy value.
:::

## Leveraged buyouts (LBOs)

A financial sponsor (private equity) acquires a company using significant debt, repaid from the target’s cash flows, and sells it after 3–7 years.

:::example LBO returns (fictional)
- Entry: EBITDA $50m at 8.0× → EV $400m; debt 5.0× = $250m; sponsor equity $150m (fees ignored).
- Over 5 years: EBITDA grows to $65m; free cash flow repays debt to $100m.
- Exit at 8.0× → EV $520m; equity = 520 − 100 = **$420m**.
- MOIC = 420 ÷ 150 = **2.8×**; IRR = 2.8^(1/5) − 1 ≈ **22.9%**.

Value drivers: EBITDA growth (operational improvement), debt paydown (deleveraging), and multiple expansion (buying cheaper than selling — not something to rely on). High leverage also creates the risk of covenant breach if EBITDA falls.
:::

## Financing an acquisition

Cash on hand; acquisition term loans and bridge loans (later refinanced with bonds or equity); vendor financing; equity issue; seller rollover (seller keeps a minority stake). Lenders focus on pro forma leverage, interest cover, the credibility of synergies, and integration risk.

## Corporate restructuring: the financial mechanics

When a company cannot sustain its debt, stakeholders negotiate a restructuring:

1. **Stabilise liquidity:** standstill, 13-week cash flow, cost and capex cuts, working-capital release.
2. **Independent business review:** viability, sustainable debt capacity (e.g., the debt the business can service at 3–4× normalised EBITDA).
3. **Capital structure options:** maturity extensions, covenant resets, interest holidays or payment-in-kind, partial write-offs, debt-for-equity swaps, new money (often super-senior), asset disposals, new equity from shareholders.
4. **Allocation of losses:** by priority — equity first, then subordinated, then senior. The **absolute priority** principle is a guide; negotiated deals often give junior parties something to secure agreement.
5. **Implementation:** consensual agreement or court-supported procedure (jurisdiction-specific).

:::example Debt-for-equity swap (fictional)
Company with sustainable EBITDA $30m carries $300m of debt (10×). Sustainable debt estimated at 4× = $120m. Lenders exchange $180m of debt for 80% of the equity; existing shareholders keep 20% and inject $20m of new money. Lenders lose part of their claim but gain the upside of a viable company; shareholders avoid total loss.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Target standalone value $500m; synergies PV $120m; integration costs $30m; price $580m. Does the deal create value for the acquirer? How much of the synergy value goes to the seller?
2. An acquirer on P/E 12 buys a target on P/E 18 using all stock. Accretive or dilutive (ignore synergies)?
3. LBO: entry EV 10× EBITDA of $40m with 6× debt. Five years later EBITDA $48m, debt $180m, exit multiple 9×. Compute MOIC and approximate IRR.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. Value to acquirer = 500 + 120 − 580 − 30 = **+$10m** — marginally positive. Premium paid = 80; the seller captures 80 of the 120 synergies (67%), leaving little margin for error.
2. **Dilutive**: the acquirer issues shares valued at 12× its earnings to buy earnings at 18×; earnings yield acquired (5.6%) < acquirer’s earnings yield (8.3%).
3. Entry EV 400; debt 240; equity 160. Exit EV 9 × 48 = 432; equity 432 − 180 = 252. MOIC = 252 ÷ 160 = **1.58×**; IRR ≈ 1.575^(0.2) − 1 ≈ **9.5%** — weak: multiple contraction and modest growth.
:::

:::summary Part IV summary
- Value comes from cash flows, their timing and their risk; NPV is the core rule, IRR and payback are supporting tools.
- The cost of capital is an opportunity cost set by markets, estimated with judgement and presented as a range.
- Capital structure balances cheaper debt against distress risk and flexibility; leverage magnifies outcomes in both directions.
- Working capital is a major, controllable source of cash; growth needs funding before it happens.
- Valuation triangulates DCF, comparables and precedents; the assumptions matter more than the arithmetic.
- In M&A, value is created only if synergies exceed the premium and costs — EPS accretion is not proof of value.
:::
