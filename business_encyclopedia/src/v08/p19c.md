## Case 8 — A family office evaluates a minority stake in a private company

**Background.** Al-Bahar Family Office (fictional) considers buying 30% of SmileCare Clinics (fictional), a chain of 14 dental clinics in Bahrain founded by two dentists. The founders want capital to open 6 new clinics and partial liquidity.

**Participants.** Family office investment team; founders; financial due-diligence provider; legal counsel; healthcare regulatory advisor; bank (existing lender to SmileCare).

**Commercial objective.** Investor: a 15–20% IRR over 5 years with protections as a minority shareholder. Founders: growth capital, partial cash-out, continued control.

**Relevant terminology.** Quality of earnings (QoE), normalised EBITDA, related-party transactions, primary vs secondary shares, earn-out, shareholder agreement, reserved matters, tag-along, put option, MOIC, IRR.

### Quality of earnings

| Item | USD m |
|---|---|
| Reported EBITDA (pre-IFRS 16) | 5.00 |
| Founders’ salaries below market for their clinical and management roles | (0.60) |
| One-off gain on sale of old equipment | (0.20) |
| Rent paid to a founder-owned building below market | (0.30) |
| Pre-opening costs of two clinics opened late in the year (non-recurring) | 0.25 |
| **Normalised EBITDA** | **4.15** |

Other findings: patient-insurance receivables average 95 days (claims rejections 6%); two clinics operate under licences in a founder’s personal name (must be transferred); dentists’ contracts lack non-compete clauses; patient data stored without adequate access controls (data-protection compliance gap).

### Valuation

| Method | Range |
|---|---|
| Comparable private healthcare transactions (8–9× EBITDA) | EV USD 33.2–37.4m |
| DCF (WACC 12%, including 6 new clinics with execution risk) | EV USD 34–40m |
| Net debt (pre-IFRS 16) | USD 6.0m |
| **Equity value range** | **USD 27–34m** |

The founders initially asked USD 12m for 30% (implying USD 40m equity).

### Negotiated structure

- Purchase of 30% for **USD 9.6m** (implied equity USD 32m): USD 5.0m **primary** (new shares, cash to the company for expansion) and USD 4.6m **secondary** (cash to founders).
- **Earn-out:** an additional USD 1.2m to the founders if normalised EBITDA reaches USD 5.0m in year 2.
- **Conditions precedent:** licence transfers to the company; arm’s-length lease with the founder’s building; data-protection remediation plan; key dentists sign contracts with non-solicitation clauses (enforceability checked locally).
- **Shareholder agreement:** two of five board seats; reserved matters (budget, debt above USD 3m, new clinics outside Bahrain, related-party transactions, dividend policy, senior hires); information rights (monthly KPIs); tag-along; drag-along above a valuation threshold; **put option** at fair value after 6 years if no exit has occurred.

### Return calculation

Plan: 20 clinics; EBITDA USD 7.5m in year 5; exit at 9× → EV 67.5m; net debt 4.0m → equity 63.5m. Investor’s 30% = USD 19.05m, plus dividends USD 2.5m over the period.

- Without earn-out: MOIC = (19.05 + 2.5) ÷ 9.6 = **2.24×**; IRR ≈ **17.5%**.
- With earn-out paid (total cost USD 10.8m): MOIC ≈ **2.0×**; IRR ≈ **15%**.

### Failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Control |
|---|---|
| New clinics ramp slowly | Phased capital release; KPI review before each opening |
| Founders’ conflict or departure | Leaver provisions; key-person insurance; management depth plan |
| Regulatory sanction (licences, data) | CPs; compliance officer; annual audit |
| No exit route | Put option; drag-along; IPO readiness plan |

### Outcome and lessons

The deal closed after 4 months; the earn-out was triggered in year 2. **Lessons:** normalise earnings before valuing a private company; separate primary and secondary capital; minority investors protect themselves through governance rights and exit mechanisms, not just price.

## Case 9 — An entrepreneur raises startup capital

**Background.** Tasdeed (fictional), a Saudi B2B payments startup, helps SMEs collect invoices digitally. Metrics: annualised revenue USD 2.4m (growing ~150% year on year), gross margin 55%, net burn USD 350,000/month, cash runway 7 months. Earlier, the founders raised USD 0.5m on a post-money SAFE with a USD 5m cap. Pre-round ownership: founders 88.5%, SAFE holders 10% (on conversion), existing option pool 1.5%.

**Participants.** Two founders; existing SAFE investors; two venture capital funds; lawyers.

**Commercial objective.** Raise USD 4m to reach profitability-or-next-round milestones in 18–24 months, at acceptable dilution and terms.

### Competing term sheets

| Term | VC A | VC B |
|---|---|---|
| Amount | USD 4m | USD 4m |
| Pre-money valuation | USD 16m | USD 18m |
| Option pool | Top-up to 15% post-money, included in pre-money | Top-up to 10% post-money, in pre-money |
| Liquidation preference | 1× non-participating | 1.5× participating, capped at 3× |
| Board | 2 founders, 1 VC A, 1 independent (mutually agreed) | 2 founders, 2 VC B, 1 independent chosen by VC B |
| Anti-dilution | Broad-based weighted average | Full ratchet |

### Ownership after the round

| Holder | VC A deal | VC B deal |
|---|---|---|
| Founders | 58.4% | 64.5% |
| SAFE holders | 6.6% | 7.3% |
| Option pool | 15.0% | 10.0% |
| New investor | 20.0% | 18.2% |

(VC A: post-money 20m → investor 20%; pool 15%; remaining 65% split 88.5 : 10. VC B: post-money 22m → investor 18.2%; pool 10%; remaining 71.8%.)

### Founders’ proceeds in exits

| Exit value | VC A deal | VC B deal |
|---|---|---|
| USD 40m | Investor converts (20% × 40 = 8m > 4m) → founders 58.4% × 40 = **USD 23.4m** | Investor takes 1.5× preference (6m) + 18.2% of remaining 34m (6.2m) = 12.2m → capped at 3× = 12m; remaining 28m shared by others → founders 64.5/81.8 × 28 = **USD 22.1m** |
| USD 150m | Founders 58.4% × 150 = **USD 87.6m** | Investor converts (18.2% × 150 = 27.3m > 12m cap) → founders 64.5% × 150 = **USD 96.8m** |

VC B’s higher headline valuation is worth more to founders only in large exits; in modest outcomes its preference terms take more. Full-ratchet anti-dilution and board control are additional risks.

### Negotiation and outcome

The founders used the two offers to negotiate with VC A: **USD 17m pre-money, 12% post-money pool, 1× non-participating preference, broad-based weighted-average anti-dilution, 2–1–1 board composition with a mutually agreed independent director**. Resulting ownership: investor 19.0%, pool 12%, founders 62.0%, SAFE holders 7.0%.

### Use of funds and milestones

| Use | USD m |
|---|---|
| Product and engineering | 1.6 |
| Sales and partnerships (banks, ERPs) | 1.3 |
| Compliance and licensing | 0.4 |
| Working capital and buffer | 0.7 |

Milestones for the next round: annualised revenue USD 8m, gross margin 60%, burn multiple below 1.5, payment-institution licence obtained.

### Lessons

Compare term sheets on outcomes, not headlines; understand preference mechanics and anti-dilution; governance terms matter for control; raise with at least 6–9 months of runway left to negotiate from strength.

## Case 10 — A company acquires a competitor

**Background.** Levant Snacks Group (fictional, UAE-based; EBITDA USD 25m; net debt USD 35m) agrees to acquire Desert Snacks Co. (fictional, Saudi Arabia; revenue USD 60m; EBITDA USD 7.5m; net debt USD 10m) to gain Saudi distribution and production capacity.

**Participants.** Acquirer’s board and corporate development team; target’s founding family; M&A advisers; lenders; lawyers; financial, tax, commercial and environmental due-diligence providers; W&I insurer; competition authority.

**Commercial objective.** Create value through market access and synergies at a price that leaves a margin of safety.

### Valuation and price

- **Price:** enterprise value USD 60m (8.0× EBITDA) on a cash-free, debt-free basis → equity price USD 50m after deducting net debt.
- **Standalone DCF value of target:** EV ≈ USD 55m (the price includes a premium of ~USD 5m).
- **Synergies (run-rate by year 3):** procurement USD 0.72m (2% of target COGS of USD 36m); distribution consolidation USD 0.90m; overheads USD 0.60m → cost synergies **USD 2.22m**; revenue synergies (cross-selling) USD 1.0m gross profit, risk-weighted at 50%. One-off integration costs USD 3.0m.
- **PV of net synergies** (after tax 15%, discount rate 10%, growth 2%, phased in over 3 years) ≈ **USD 20m**.
- **Value created** ≈ 55 + 20 − 60 − 1.5 (transaction costs) ≈ **+USD 13.5m** — positive, but dependent on delivering most synergies.

### Financing and pro forma leverage

| Item | USD m |
|---|---|
| Uses: equity price 50 + refinancing target debt 10 + fees 1.5 | 61.5 |
| Sources: acquisition term loan 45 + cash 16.5 | 61.5 |
| Pro forma net debt (35 + 61.5) | 96.5 |
| Pro forma EBITDA (25 + 7.5) | 32.5 |
| **Pro forma leverage** | **2.97×** |
| With run-rate cost synergies (34.7) | 2.78× |

Lenders required leverage below 3.25× at closing, stepping down to 2.5× by year 3.

### SPA and protections

**Locked-box** mechanism (price fixed on the 31 December balance sheet; seller compensates any “leakage” such as dividends after that date); warranties on accounts, tax, licences, IP, employees and litigation; **W&I insurance** (limit 10% of EV, retention 0.5% of EV) replacing most of the seller’s warranty liability; **specific indemnity** for an open tax assessment; 3-year non-compete for the family; **conditions precedent:** competition clearance, lender funding, no material adverse change; long-stop date 6 months.

### Integration plan (first 100 days)

Retention bonuses for 12 key managers; keep the Desert brand; joint procurement team from month 2; distribution routes merged in Riyadh from month 4; ERP migration deferred to month 12; synergy tracker reported monthly to the board.

### Failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Control |
|---|---|
| A major Saudi distributor terminates its relationship | Pre-closing customer and distributor calls; transition incentives |
| Synergies delayed | Owner per synergy; monthly tracking; conservative bank case |
| Hidden liabilities (e.g., zakat/tax, labour) | Due diligence; specific indemnity; W&I insurance |
| Covenant pressure in a downturn | Equity cure rights; reduced dividends; working-capital programme |

### Outcome and lessons

By year 2, 80% of cost synergies were achieved; one regional distributor was lost (−USD 0.4m EBITDA). Leverage reached 2.4× by year 3. **Lessons:** pay for synergies only partly; protect against the unknown with DD, W&I and indemnities; leverage after an acquisition must survive a downside; integration execution decides value.

## Case 12 — A business evaluates a capital investment: rooftop solar

**Background.** Golden Loaf Bakeries (fictional) operates a large bakery with high daytime electricity consumption. It evaluates a 1 MWp rooftop and carport solar PV system to reduce grid purchases. (Assumptions are illustrative; net-metering rules, tariffs and permits vary by country and must be verified.)

**Participants.** CFO; operations director; EPC contractor; utility/regulator (connection approval); bank (possible financing); a solar developer offering a power purchase agreement (PPA) alternative.

**Commercial objective.** Reduce energy costs and exposure to tariff increases with an attractive return.

### Assumptions

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Capex (turnkey) | USD 750,000 |
| Year-1 generation | 1,650 MWh (all consumed on site) |
| Avoided grid tariff | USD 0.16/kWh, rising 2% a year |
| Panel degradation | 0.5% a year |
| O&M | USD 12,000 a year, rising 2% |
| Inverter replacement (year 12) | USD 60,000 |
| Life | 25 years |
| Tax | 20%; straight-line tax depreciation over 10 years |
| Discount rate | 10% |

### Calculation (rounded)

- Year-1 savings = 1,650,000 kWh × 0.16 = **USD 264,000**; net of O&M = 252,000; growth ≈ +1.5% a year (tariff +2%, degradation −0.5%).
- Years 1–10: tax = 20% × (252,000 − 75,000 depreciation) ≈ 35,400 → after-tax cash flow ≈ **USD 216,600** in year 1, growing ~1.5%. PV ≈ **USD 1.41m**.
- Years 11–25: no depreciation shield → after-tax cash flow ≈ USD 234,000 in year 11 growing ~1.5%. PV ≈ **USD 0.74m**.
- Inverter replacement (after tax) PV ≈ −USD 0.02m.
- **NPV ≈ USD 1.39m; IRR ≈ 30%; payback ≈ 3.4 years.**

Sensitivity: with an avoided tariff of USD 0.12/kWh, NPV ≈ USD 0.85m — still attractive.

### Alternative: power purchase agreement (PPA)

A developer offers to install and own the system and sell electricity to the bakery at USD 0.11/kWh for 20 years (no capex). Year-1 saving = 1,650 MWh × (0.16 − 0.11) = USD 82,500, growing with the tariff spread. PV over 20 years ≈ USD 0.8–0.9m with no capital outlay and no operating risk.

### Decision

| | Own (capex) | PPA |
|---|---|---|
| Capital required | USD 750k | None |
| NPV | ~USD 1.39m | ~USD 0.85m |
| Risks borne | Performance, O&M, technology | Mostly developer’s |
| Balance-sheet / covenant effect | Capex; possibly debt | PPA may be a lease-like commitment (check IFRS 16 analysis) |

**Recommendation:** own the system, financed 70% by a 7-year green term loan, because returns far exceed the cost of capital and the bakery has capacity to manage O&M contracts; if capital were constrained, the PPA would still create value.

### Lessons

Capital projects should be compared with alternative structures (ownership, leasing, PPA); tax shields and escalation assumptions matter; sensitivity analysis protects against over-optimistic energy-price assumptions.

## Case 14 — A software company evaluates a SaaS business model

**Background.** OrbitERP (fictional), a Gulf-based vendor of ERP software for SMEs, sells perpetual licences (USD 50k per new customer) plus 20% annual maintenance and implementation services. Revenue USD 12m: licences USD 5.0m (100 new customers), maintenance USD 4.0m (on an installed licence base of USD 20m), services USD 3.0m. The board considers selling all new contracts as SaaS at USD 1,500/month (USD 18k/year).

**Participants.** CEO, CFO, head of sales, product/cloud team, board, bank, prospective growth investor.

**Commercial objective.** Increase recurring revenue and enterprise value while surviving the transition dip.

### Customer-level comparison

| Cumulative revenue per customer (USD k) | Year 1 | Year 3 | Year 5 | Year 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perpetual licence + 20% maintenance | 50 | 70 | 90 | 110 |
| SaaS at 18k/year | 18 | 54 | 90 | 126 |

The SaaS customer becomes more valuable after year 5 (if retained), with steadier cash flows — but much less is received upfront.

### Four-year company projection (USD m; new customers 100, 110, 120, 130; churn ignored for simplicity)

| | Y1 | Y2 | Y3 | Y4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Licence model revenue** | 12.0 | 13.8 | 15.7 | 17.7 |
| **SaaS model revenue** | 7.3 | 9.4 | 11.7 | 14.1 |
| Revenue gap | (4.7) | (4.4) | (4.0) | (3.6) |
| SaaS model: ARR at year-end (SaaS only) | 1.8 | 3.8 | 5.9 | 8.3 |
| SaaS model: total recurring (SaaS + maintenance 4.0) | 5.8 | 7.8 | 9.9 | 12.3 |

(Licence model: licences 5.0/5.5/6.0/6.5 + maintenance 4.0/5.0/6.1/7.3 + services. SaaS model: subscription revenue on average customers in the year + maintenance on the existing base 4.0 + lower services.)

**Profit and cash:** with hosting costs (SaaS gross margin ~75% vs ~95% for licences), cumulative EBITDA over four years is roughly USD 14m lower under SaaS; the company would need about USD 8–10m of funding to cover losses during the transition.

**Valuation:** a licence-model business might be valued around 3× revenue (Y4: ~USD 53m); a SaaS business with strong retention might command ~6× recurring revenue (Y4: 12.3 × 6 ≈ USD 74m). Value creation depends on retention, growth and the investor market at the time.

### Decision and plan

Hybrid transition: SaaS for all new SME customers; licences remain available for large customers demanding on-premise deployment; migrate maintenance customers to SaaS with credits for licences already paid; raise USD 10m of growth equity; track ARR, net revenue retention, CAC payback and gross margin monthly.

### Failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Control |
|---|---|
| Churn higher than expected | Customer success team; annual prepayment discounts; integration features |
| Cash shortfall in the “SaaS valley” | Equity raised upfront; annual billing in advance |
| Cloud costs erode margin | Architecture optimisation; multi-tenant design; pricing tiers |
| Sales team resists (lower upfront commissions) | Compensation plan based on ACV/ARR |

### Lessons

Business-model transitions move revenue and cash in time; the dip must be funded; recurring revenue is valued more highly only if retention is strong; incentives and metrics must change with the model.

## Case 15 — An insurer underwrites a commercial property and business interruption risk

**Background.** Arabian Mutual Insurance (fictional) receives a broker submission for property damage (PD) and business interruption (BI) cover for a food-processing factory (fictional) in an industrial city.

**Participants.** Insured (factory owner); insurance broker; insurer’s underwriter; risk engineer (survey); reinsurers (treaty and facultative).

**Commercial objective.** Insurer: write a profitable risk within appetite and capacity. Insured: comprehensive cover at a fair premium with workable conditions.

### Submission

| Item | Sum insured (USD m) |
|---|---|
| Buildings | 18.0 |
| Machinery and equipment | 22.0 |
| Stock (peak value with declaration clause) | 9.0 |
| BI: gross profit, 18-month indemnity period | 21.0 |
| **Total** | **70.0** |

Loss history: no material claims in 5 years. Survey findings: sprinklers in production halls but **not** in the finished-goods warehouse; good hot-work permit system; thermographic inspections overdue; site 2 km from a seasonal flood channel (moderate exposure).

### Probable maximum loss (PML)

Worst credible single event: fire spreading from warehouse into the adjacent production hall, affecting 60% of physical values and stopping production for 12 months:
PML ≈ 0.6 × (18 + 22 + 9) + BI 14.0 (12 months of gross profit) ≈ **USD 43.4m**.

### Pricing (illustrative technical rates)

| Component | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| PD base premium | 49.0m × 0.10% | 49,000 |
| BI base premium | 21.0m × 0.12% | 25,200 |
| Loading: no warehouse sprinklers | +15% | 11,130 |
| Discount: USD 100k PD deductible | −10% | (8,533) |
| Discount: clean loss history | −5% | (3,840) |
| Flood loading | | 6,000 |
| **Technical premium** | | **≈ 78,957** |

### Capacity and reinsurance

The insurer’s net retention per risk is USD 5m (PML basis). It cedes the remainder through its surplus treaty and a facultative placement: ceded share ≈ (43.4 − 5.0) ÷ 43.4 ≈ **88%** of premium and losses; the insurer earns a ceding commission on the ceded premium (e.g., 20%).

### Terms and conditions offered

- Deductibles: PD USD 100,000 per occurrence; BI 7-day waiting period.
- **Warranty:** automatic sprinklers to be installed in the warehouse within 9 months (premium reduced by 10% on completion).
- Condition: annual thermographic inspection of electrical installations; hot-work permits maintained.
- Flood sub-limit: USD 10m.
- Exclusions: standard (war, nuclear, wear and tear, cyber); supplier-extension BI for the main packaging supplier added with a USD 2m sub-limit for additional premium of USD 3,500.

### Failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Control |
|---|---|
| Fire in the unsprinklered warehouse before installation | Interim conditions: storage height limits, fire watch, extinguishers; warranty timeline |
| Underinsurance of stock at peak | Declaration clause and seasonal increase |
| BI indemnity period too short | Broker advised 18 months (replacement machines lead time ~10 months + customer recovery) |
| Accumulation with neighbouring insured sites (same industrial city) | Insurer monitors accumulation; catastrophe reinsurance |

### Outcome and lessons

The insured accepted; sprinklers were installed in month 7, reducing the premium. **Lessons:** underwriting combines engineering, finance and portfolio thinking; risk improvements reduce both premiums and the probability of loss; PML and reinsurance determine how much risk an insurer can accept; the insured’s choice of sums insured and indemnity periods is as important as price.

:::answer Answers to the practice questions on investment, corporate, technology and insurance deals
1. MOIC = (19.05 + 3.5) ÷ 9.6 = 2.35× over 7 years → IRR ≈ 2.35^(1/7) − 1 ≈ **13.0%** (timing of dividends ignored).
2. Uses rise by USD 6m → pro forma net debt 102.5 ÷ 32.5 = **3.15×** — close to the 3.25× lender limit.
3. Savings 1,650,000 × 0.14 = 231,000 − O&M 12,000 = 219,000; tax 20% × (219,000 − 75,000) = 28,800 → after-tax ≈ **USD 190,200**.
4. Expected lifetime revenue ≈ 18k ÷ churn = 90k → churn ≈ **20% per year** (lifetime 5 years). With churn below 20%, SaaS customers are worth more than licence customers over their lifetime (undiscounted).
5. Base 74,200 − deductible discount 10% = 66,780 − loss-history 5% = 63,441 + flood 6,000 ≈ **USD 69,441** (before the additional 10% sprinkler reduction promised, which would bring it to about USD 62,500 depending on how it is applied).

:::summary Part XIX summary
- Real deals combine price, terms, documents, financing, risk transfer, legal protection and execution.
- Professionals structure deals around cash flows and risks, not only headline prices.
- Every case has failure scenarios; good deals include the controls before signing.
- Lessons repeat across industries: match financing to asset cycles, demand protection proportional to exposure, test the downside, and execute integration and operations carefully.
:::
