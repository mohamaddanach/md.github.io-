## Case 3 — A bank structures financing for an auto-parts importer

**Background.** Gulf Auto Parts Co. (fictional, Kuwait) imports spare parts from Japan, Korea and Germany and sells to workshops, dealers and fleet operators. Revenue USD 42m; EBITDA USD 4.6m; net debt USD 9.0m. It asks Training Bank (fictional) for USD 8m of facilities to replace a competitor bank.

**Participants.** Borrower and its owners; relationship manager; credit analyst; credit committee; trade-finance operations; external stock auditor; legal counsel.

**Commercial objective.** Bank: win a profitable relationship with acceptable risk and ancillary business (payments, payroll, FX). Borrower: larger and cheaper facilities, faster L/C processing, and 120-day import loans matching its stock cycle.

**Relevant terminology.** L/C line, trust receipt (TR) loan, borrowing base, cash margin, ageing, DIO, personal guarantee, covenant, RAROC, early-warning indicators.

### Analysis highlights

| Indicator | Value | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth (3-year CAGR) | 9% | Stable aftermarket demand |
| Gross margin | 31% | Typical for parts distribution |
| DIO | 150 days | Long: wide SKU range (18,000 SKUs) |
| Stock older than 12 months | 22% of inventory | Obsolescence risk |
| DSO | 55 days | Fleet customers on 60–90 days |
| Net debt / EBITDA | 1.96× | Moderate |
| EBITDA / interest | 7.2× | Comfortable |
| Owners | Family, second generation | Good conduct; key-person risk |

**Key insight:** the borrower asks for 120-day TR loans, but stock turns in about 150 days on average — and much slower for 22% of the stock. Funding long-tail inventory with short-term self-liquidating loans would create rollover pressure. The bank structures facilities around a **borrowing base** that excludes aged stock.

### Structure approved

| Facility | Limit (USD m) | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| Import L/C line (sight and usance up to 90 days) | 5.0 | 0.20% per quarter issuance; 10% cash margin |
| Trust-receipt loans (sub-limit of L/C line) | (4.0) | 120 days; ref. rate + 2.60%; drawn only within the borrowing base |
| Overdraft | 1.0 | Ref. + 3.0%; annual review |
| Letters of guarantee (customs, tenders) | 1.0 | 0.9% p.a.; 20% cash margin on tender bonds |
| Borrowing base | — | 80% of receivables < 90 days + 50% of inventory < 12 months, minus outstanding TR loans and overdraft |
| Security | — | Personal guarantees of two shareholders; assignment of insurance; pledge over stock in the main warehouse (where local law and practice allow) |
| Covenants | — | Net debt/EBITDA ≤ 3.0×; EBITDA/interest ≥ 3.5×; stock audit annually; monthly borrowing-base report |

**Borrowing base at approval:** receivables < 90 days USD 5.6m × 80% = 4.48; inventory < 12 months USD 10.5m × 50% = 5.25 → base **USD 9.73m**, comfortably above expected peak usage of USD 5.0m (TR + overdraft).

### Pricing and return to the bank

| Item | USD |
|---|---|
| Interest margin on average USD 3.5m drawn × 2.7% | 94,500 |
| L/C commissions (USD 18m issued per year × 0.20% per quarter × avg 1.5 quarters) | 54,000 |
| Guarantee fees | 7,200 |
| FX income on ~USD 20m conversions at 0.15% | 30,000 |
| Payment and payroll fees | 18,000 |
| **Total revenue** | **203,700** |
| Expected loss (PD 1.2% × LGD 45% × average exposure 6.5m) | (35,100) |
| Operating costs | (45,000) |
| **Pre-tax risk-adjusted income** | **123,600** |
| Allocated capital (risk-weighted exposure ~USD 5.5m × 12%) | 660,000 |
| **RAROC (pre-tax)** | **≈ 18.7%** |

### Failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Early warning | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Obsolete stock inflates inventory | Rising ageing; falling turnover | Borrowing base excludes stock > 12 months; annual independent stock audit |
| Stock pledged to two banks | Discrepancies in reports | Registered pledge; negative pledge; bank statements of other lenders |
| Major fleet customer defaults | DSO increase; concentration | Concentration limit (no customer > 15% of eligible receivables) |
| Funds diverted to owners’ real estate | Large transfers to related parties | Dividend restriction above 2.5× leverage; account monitoring |
| Supplier price increases (JPY, EUR) | Margin decline | Encourage FX hedging with the bank |

### Outcome and lessons

The bank won the relationship; the borrower obtained larger L/C capacity and lower pricing than its previous bank, but accepted the borrowing-base discipline. **Lessons:** match facility type to the asset cycle; exclude weak collateral rather than lending against it; a bank’s return depends heavily on ancillary business; monitoring requirements are part of the price of credit.

## Case 4 — A construction company obtains a letter of credit

**Background.** Muscat Builders LLC (fictional, Oman) buys 2,000 tonnes of steel rebar from an Indian mill: **USD 680 per tonne CFR Sohar, Incoterms® 2020 → USD 1,360,000**. The mill requires an irrevocable L/C at sight; the builder asks for 90-day usance to match its project billing cycle.

**Participants.** Applicant (builder); issuing bank (Omani bank, fictional); beneficiary (Indian mill); advising/negotiating bank in India; shipping line; inspection company; insurer (buyer’s cover).

**Commercial objective.** Builder: obtain the L/C quickly, protect itself against wrong goods and late shipment, and obtain 90 days’ credit. Mill: payment security with documents it can actually produce.

### Steps

1. **Credit line:** the builder had an L/C line of USD 3m with 10% cash margin; available headroom USD 1.9m — sufficient.
2. **Negotiation of usance:** the mill agreed to a **usance L/C, 90 days after B/L date, “usance payable at sight” (UPAS)** — the mill is paid at sight by the negotiating bank, while the builder pays at 90 days; the builder bears the discount interest.
3. **Drafting the L/C application** (key fields chosen by the builder’s procurement and finance teams with the bank’s trade advisor):

| Field | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Amount and tolerance | USD 1,360,000, ±5% | Matches quantity tolerance in contract |
| Goods description | “2,000 MT (±5%) deformed steel bars, grade B500B per BS 4449, sizes per contract no. MB-2026-07, CFR Sohar Incoterms® 2020” | Precise but not over-detailed (avoids trivial discrepancies) |
| Latest shipment date / expiry | 15 August / 5 September | Contract delivery plus presentation time |
| Partial shipments | Allowed | Mill may ship in two vessels |
| Documents | Commercial invoice; packing list (bundle list); full set clean on-board B/L to order of issuing bank, freight prepaid; mill test certificates; **inspection certificate issued by an independent inspection company** stating quantity and conformity to BS 4449 grade B500B; certificate of origin | Independent inspection protects the buyer against wrong grade |
| Insurance | Covered by applicant | CFR terms; buyer’s open cover |
| Presentation period | 21 days after shipment | Standard |
| Charges | Outside Oman for beneficiary | Common allocation |

**A trap avoided:** the mill’s draft proposed “certificate of quality issued by the beneficiary” — worthless as protection. The builder insisted on an independent inspection certificate; the mill agreed if the inspection company was named in advance and its cost shared 50/50.

### Costs to the applicant

| Item | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| Issuance commission | 0.30% per quarter × 2 quarters × 1.36m | 8,160 |
| SWIFT and courier | | 160 |
| Amendment (shipment date extension) | | 100 |
| Discount interest (UPAS) for 90 days | 1.36m × 6.8% × 90/360 | 23,120 |
| Opportunity cost of 10% cash margin for ~6 months at 4% | 136,000 × 4% × 0.5 | 2,720 |
| Inspection cost share | | 1,500 |
| **Total** | **≈ 2.6% of goods value** | **35,760** |

Without usance, the builder would have borrowed on its overdraft at 8.5% for 90 days (USD 28,900) — UPAS was cheaper and preserved overdraft headroom.

### Risks, failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Control |
|---|---|
| Mill ships wrong grade but documents comply | Independent inspection certificate as a required document |
| Shipment delayed beyond latest date | Mill requests amendment; builder can refuse if delay hurts the project (but then has no steel) — contract delay penalties as separate remedy |
| Steel price falls before shipment | Builder bound to pay compliant documents — price risk is the buyer’s once the contract is fixed |
| Fraudulent documents | Banks check apparent compliance only; choose reputable mill, verify B/L via carrier |
| Builder cannot pay at maturity | Bank’s risk; secured by cash margin, B/L to its order and the credit line |

### Outcome and lessons

The L/C was issued within three days; one amendment extended shipment by 10 days. Documents were compliant; the builder received the goods and paid at 90 days. **Lessons:** the applicant controls its protection through the documents it requires; usance structures align payment with the buyer’s cash cycle; L/C costs are significant and must be included in project pricing.

## Case 5 — A company negotiates a revolving credit facility

**Background.** Gulf Logistics Group (fictional, UAE) — revenue USD 310m, EBITDA USD 52m (post-IFRS 16; USD 41m pre-IFRS 16), net debt USD 95m excluding leases — wants a USD 75m, 3-year committed RCF from three relationship banks (a club deal) to replace bilateral overdrafts and fund acquisitions.

**Participants.** CFO and treasury; three banks (one as coordinator and agent); lawyers for borrower and lenders; material subsidiaries as guarantors.

**Commercial objective.** Committed liquidity at the lowest all-in cost with covenants that leave headroom for acquisitions and downturns.

### Term sheet negotiation

| Term | Banks’ initial proposal | Negotiated outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Amount / tenor | USD 75m, 3 years | USD 75m, 3 years + two 1-year extension options; USD 25m uncommitted accordion |
| Margin | Ref. + 2.10% flat | Margin grid by net leverage: < 1.5× 1.60%; 1.5–2.5× 1.85%; > 2.5× 2.20% |
| Commitment fee | 40% of margin | 35% of applicable margin |
| Utilisation fee | 0.20% above 50% usage | 0.10% above 33%; 0.20% above 66% |
| Upfront fee | 0.75% | 0.50% |
| Leverage covenant | Net debt/EBITDA ≤ 2.75× | ≤ 3.25× (pre-IFRS 16 basis, excluding lease liabilities), stepping up to 3.75× for 12 months after an acquisition above USD 30m |
| Interest cover | EBITDA/interest ≥ 5.0× | ≥ 4.0× |
| EBITDA definition | Strict, no add-backs | Pro forma for acquisitions; synergies add-backs capped at 10% of EBITDA |
| Equity cure | None | Allowed twice over the life, not in consecutive quarters |
| Guarantor coverage | Subsidiaries representing 90% of EBITDA and assets | 80% |
| Negative pledge | No exceptions | Baskets: existing asset finance; vehicle leases; USD 15m general basket |
| Dividends | Restricted above 2.5× | Permitted if pro forma leverage < 3.0× |
| Sustainability-linked margin | — | ±5 bps based on fleet emissions intensity KPI, externally verified |

### Cost calculation (expected average utilisation 40%, leverage 1.8×, reference rate 4.3%)

| Item | Calculation | USD per year |
|---|---|---|
| Interest on drawn | 30m × (4.3% + 1.85%) | 1,845,000 |
| Commitment fee on undrawn | 45m × (35% × 1.85%) | 291,375 |
| Utilisation fee (usage 40% > 33%) | 30m × 0.10% | 30,000 |
| Upfront fee amortised | 75m × 0.50% ÷ 3 | 125,000 |
| Agency fee | | 30,000 |
| **Total** | **Of which costs beyond the reference rate ≈ USD 1.03m (≈ 3.4% of the average drawn amount)** | **2,321,375** |

### Covenant headroom test

| Scenario | EBITDA pre-IFRS 16 | Net debt (excl. leases) | Leverage | Headroom vs 3.25× |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 41.0 | 95 | 2.32× | 29% |
| Downside (−20% EBITDA) | 32.8 | 100 | 3.05× | 6% |
| Acquisition of USD 40m target (EBITDA 5m) | 46.0 | 135 | 2.93× | 22% vs 3.75× step-up |
| Acquisition + downside (−20% on combined EBITDA) | 36.8 | 140 | 3.80× | **Breach** of the 3.75× step-up |

### Failure scenarios and controls

- Downturn combined with an acquisition → covenant breach: fund part of any acquisition with equity or delay it if the downside materialises; forecast covenants quarterly and negotiate a waiver early if needed.
- One bank exits at extension → extension only for consenting lenders; maintain relationships with other banks.
- Interest rates rise → hedge 50% of expected drawn amounts with swaps.

### Outcome and lessons

The facility was signed in 10 weeks. **Lessons:** covenant definitions (EBITDA basis, add-backs, IFRS 16) can matter more than margin; negotiate headroom against the downside plus planned acquisitions; compare costs at realistic utilisation; flexibility features (accordion, extensions, equity cure, baskets) have real value.

## Case 6 — A trader purchases and resells a cargo of raw sugar

**Background.** Meridian Softs (fictional), a trading house, buys 50,000 tonnes (±5%) of Brazilian raw sugar FOB Santos and sells it to a refinery in the UAE CFR Jebel Ali.

**Participants.** Brazilian seller (a sugar mill’s trading arm); trader; refinery (buyer); shipowner (voyage charter); ship broker; inspection companies (load and discharge); trade-finance bank; futures broker (ICE); cargo insurer.

**Commercial objective.** Earn a spread between the CFR sale premium and the FOB purchase premium plus freight and costs, without taking flat-price risk.

**Relevant terminology.** ICE Sugar No. 11, premium/differential, polarisation, pricing period, voyage charter, laytime and demurrage, L/C, hedge lots, variation margin.

### Pricing structure

| | Purchase | Sale |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | ICE No. 11 May futures, average of seller’s pricing days in April | ICE No. 11 May futures, average of buyer’s pricing days in April |
| Premium | + 0.40 ¢/lb FOB Santos | + 2.60 ¢/lb CFR Jebel Ali |
| Quality | Polarisation 96° basis with scale adjustments | Same |
| Payment | Trader’s L/C at sight | Refinery’s L/C at sight (unconfirmed, strong UAE bank) |

Unit conversion: 1 metric tonne = 2,204.62 lb → **1 ¢/lb = USD 22.0462 per tonne**. One ICE No. 11 contract = 112,000 lb; 50,000 t ≈ 984 lots.

### Hedging

The trader’s exposure arises because purchase and sale price on different days. As the seller prices (fixing the purchase), the trader **sells** futures; as the buyer prices (fixing the sale), the trader **buys back** futures. Outcome assumed: purchase pricing average 19.50 ¢/lb; sale pricing average 19.20 ¢/lb.

### P&L

| Item | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| Sale | (19.20 + 2.60) × 22.0462 × 50,000 | 24,030,358 |
| Purchase | (19.50 + 0.40) × 22.0462 × 50,000 | (21,935,969) |
| **Physical result** | | **2,094,389** |
| Futures hedge result | +0.30 ¢/lb × 22.0462 × 50,000 | 330,693 |
| **Hedged gross spread (2.20 ¢/lb = USD 48.50/t)** | | **2,425,082** |
| Ocean freight (Supramax voyage charter, USD 38/t) | | (1,900,000) |
| **Gross margin after freight** | **USD 10.50/t** | **525,082** |
| Cargo insurance | 0.06% × 110% × sales | (15,860) |
| Inspection and superintendence (load + discharge) | USD 0.25/t | (12,500) |
| Purchase L/C issuance | 0.10% | (21,936) |
| Negotiation charges on sale L/C | 0.05% | (12,015) |
| Financing: 85% of purchase for 45 days at 6.5% | | (151,496) |
| Futures commissions (984 lots × 2 × USD 3) | | (5,904) |
| Net demurrage at Santos (congestion) | USD 0.60/t | (30,000) |
| Quality claim reserve | 0.1% of sales | (24,030) |
| **Net profit** | **USD 5.03/t; 1.05% of sales** | **251,341** |

### Risks, failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Impact | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar rallies 3 ¢/lb during the hedge period | Variation margin calls ≈ 3 × 0.01 × 112,000 × 984 = **USD 3.31m** before the physical sale is paid | Margin-financing line with the bank; liquidity buffer |
| Congestion at Santos: 12 extra days at USD 25,000/day not recoverable | −USD 300,000 → **loss** | Laytime terms aligned in purchase contract; choose berths; build demurrage into pricing |
| Refinery rejects documents for discrepancy in a falling market | Delay, possible price renegotiation | Document checks; experienced operations team |
| Polarisation below basis | Price reduction per scale | Load-port inspection; supplier quality history |
| Seller fails to deliver | Buy replacement cargo at market | Seller KYC and track record; performance clauses; trade-association arbitration |

### Outcome and lessons

The cargo loaded with 4 days of demurrage (within estimate) and discharged on time. **Lessons:** the trader’s profit is the premium spread minus logistics and financing, not a bet on sugar prices; hedging transforms price risk into **liquidity** risk (margin calls) that must be financed; demurrage alone can erase a bulk margin.

## Case 13 — A company manages a currency exposure

**Background.** Delta Cotton Mills (fictional), an Egyptian yarn and fabric exporter, sells EUR 24m a year to European buyers. Costs: imported cotton USD 9.0m; EGP costs (labour, energy, local inputs) EGP 672m; USD-denominated loan of USD 15m at 8% interest. Baseline rates: EUR/USD 1.08; USD/EGP 48.

**Participants.** CFO and treasury; banks providing FX forwards; board (approves hedging policy); European buyers; cotton suppliers.

**Commercial objective.** Protect the margin and USD debt service from EUR/USD moves; manage EGP volatility; comply with local FX regulations and bank requirements.

### Exposure map (annual, USD m equivalent at baseline)

| Item | Currency | Amount | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | EUR | 25.92 (EUR 24m) | Long EUR |
| Cotton | USD | 9.00 | Short USD |
| Interest on loan | USD | 1.20 | Short USD |
| Local costs | EGP | 14.00 (EGP 672m) | Short EGP |
| **Profit** | | **1.72** | |

Net position: long EUR, short USD and EGP. A weaker EUR against USD hurts directly; a weaker EGP **helps** (local costs fall in USD terms), at least until local inflation catches up.

### Strategy chosen

1. **Hedge EUR/USD:** sell EUR forward against USD on a layered basis — 75% of the next 6 months’ receipts, 50% of months 7–12 — at an average forward rate of 1.09 (EUR at a forward premium to USD reflecting the interest differential).
2. **Natural hedge:** invoice 20% of new European contracts in USD where buyers accept.
3. **EGP:** convert EUR/USD proceeds to EGP monthly according to needs; no speculative holding of EGP or USD beyond policy limits.
4. **Debt:** consider refinancing part of the USD loan in EUR to match EUR revenue (a further natural hedge).

### Scenarios (annual profit, USD m)

| Scenario | Unhedged | With 75% EUR hedge at 1.09 |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline (1.08; 48) | 1.72 | 1.90 |
| EUR/USD falls to 0.97 (EGP unchanged) | −0.92 | 1.24 |
| USD/EGP rises to 58 (EUR unchanged) | 4.13 | 4.31 |
| Both (0.97 and 58) | 1.49 | 3.65 |

Calculations: hedged revenue in the EUR-fall case = EUR 18m × 1.09 + EUR 6m × 0.97 = USD 25.44m; EGP costs at 58 = 672 ÷ 58 = USD 11.59m.

### Failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Control |
|---|---|
| Over-hedging if European orders fall | Hedge only firm orders and a conservative share of forecasts |
| Banks unable to provide forwards (local market restrictions) | Use offshore hedging if permitted, or USD invoicing |
| EGP depreciation followed by high local inflation | Budget wages and energy at expected inflation; adjust prices to European buyers only where competitive |
| Treasury takes speculative positions | Board-approved policy, limits, segregation of duties, monthly reporting |

### Outcome and lessons

When EUR/USD fell 6% during the year, the hedged programme kept profit close to budget. **Lessons:** map exposures by currency before choosing instruments; natural hedges (invoice currency, debt currency) often beat derivatives; layered hedging smooths results; hedging policy must avoid speculation and over-hedging.

:::exercise Practice questions on banking, commodity and treasury deals
1. In Case 3, receivables < 90 days fall to USD 4.0m and young inventory to USD 9.0m while TR loans plus overdraft total USD 6.5m. Is the borrower within the borrowing base?
2. In Case 4, how much would the builder save if the bank reduced issuance commission to 0.20% per quarter?
3. In Case 5, recompute the annual cost at 80% utilisation (leverage still 1.8×).
4. In Case 6, what sale premium would make net profit zero (other costs unchanged)?
5. In Case 13, what profit results with no hedge if EUR/USD rises to 1.18 (EGP unchanged)? What with the 75% hedge?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions on banking, commodity and treasury deals
1. Base = 4.0 × 80% + 9.0 × 50% = 3.2 + 4.5 = **USD 7.7m** > 6.5m → within the base (headroom USD 1.2m), but trending down; the RM should investigate.
2. Saving = 0.10% × 2 × 1.36m = **USD 2,720**.
3. Drawn 60m × 6.15% = 3,690,000; undrawn 15m × 0.6475% = 97,125; utilisation fee 60m × 0.20% = 120,000; upfront 125,000; agency 30,000 → **≈ USD 4.06m**.
4. Net profit is USD 251,341; each 0.01 ¢/lb of premium = 0.01 × 22.0462 × 50,000 = USD 11,023 (slightly less after percentage-based costs). Break-even premium ≈ 2.60 − 251,341 ÷ 11,023 × 0.01 ≈ **2.37 ¢/lb**.
5. Unhedged: revenue 24 × 1.18 = 28.32 → profit = 28.32 − 9.0 − 1.2 − 14.0 = **USD 4.12m**. Hedged: 18 × 1.09 + 6 × 1.18 = 19.62 + 7.08 = 26.70 → profit **USD 2.50m**. The hedge gives up upside — the price of certainty.
:::
