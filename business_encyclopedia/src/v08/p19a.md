## Case 1 — A distributor purchases medical gloves from an overseas supplier

**Background.** Amman Medical Supplies (fictional), a Jordanian distributor to hospitals and pharmacies, wants to import nitrile examination gloves from a Malaysian manufacturer to replace a local supplier whose prices rose 15%. Annual requirement: 12 × 40ft containers; first order: 2 containers (2,400 cartons; 2.4 million gloves).

**Participants.** Buyer; Malaysian manufacturer; buyer’s bank (Amman) and supplier’s bank; Jordanian food and drug authority (product registration); freight forwarder; Aqaba port and customs; independent inspection company; cargo insurer.

**Commercial objective.** Secure reliable, compliant supply at a landed cost at least 10% below the local supplier, without prepaying large amounts to a new supplier.

**Relevant terminology.** FCA/CPT, documentary collection (D/P), AQL inspection, product registration, landed cost, HS code, certificate of analysis, shelf life, working capital.

### Transaction structure

| Element | Agreed terms |
|---|---|
| Price | USD 25.50 per carton of 1,000 gloves, **FCA Port Klang container terminal, Incoterms® 2020** → USD 61,200 per order |
| Payment | 20% advance by T/T on order; 80% **documents against payment (D/P)** through banks under URC 522 |
| Quality | Specifications per EN 455 / ASTM standards (as registered); pre-shipment inspection by an independent company at AQL 1.5; certificate of analysis per lot |
| Delivery | Within 35 days of advance; shelf life ≥ 30 months at shipment |
| Remedies | Rejection if inspection fails; replacement at supplier’s cost; 0.5% per week delay penalty capped at 5% |
| Law and disputes | English law; arbitration (Asian International Arbitration Centre, Kuala Lumpur seat) |

### Negotiation process

The supplier initially asked for 50% advance and 50% before shipment. The buyer argued that as a first-time customer it could not prepay 100% to a new supplier; it proposed an L/C. The supplier preferred to avoid L/C costs and complexity. Compromise: **20% advance + 80% D/P**, with the buyer paying for pre-shipment inspection. The buyer accepted a 2% higher price in exchange for 12-month price stability.

### Documents

Proforma invoice; purchase contract; product registration certificate; pre-shipment inspection certificate; commercial invoice; packing list; certificate of origin (for possible preferential treatment — check applicability); certificate of analysis; bill of lading (consigned to order, endorsed through the collection); insurance certificate (buyer’s open cover); collection instruction (supplier’s bank); import declaration.

### Financial calculations (per order)

| Item | USD |
|---|---|
| Goods FCA (2,400 cartons × 25.50) | 61,200 |
| Freight Port Klang → Aqaba (2 × 40ft) | 4,400 |
| Insurance (buyer’s open cover, 0.12% × 110% × CIF) | 87 |
| **CIF value** | **65,687** |
| Customs duty (assume 0% for medical gloves — verify) | 0 |
| Port, clearance, inspection fees at Aqaba | 1,150 |
| Inland transport Aqaba → Amman (2 × USD 600) | 1,200 |
| Pre-shipment inspection | 450 |
| Bank charges (T/T + collection, both sides) | 380 |
| Financing (advance 45 days; balance ~20 days until sales; 8%) | ~280 |
| **Landed cost** | **69,147** |
| **Per carton** | **28.81** |
| Local supplier price | 33.00 |
| **Saving per carton** | **4.19 (12.7%)** |

Annual saving at 12 containers (14,400 cartons) ≈ **USD 60,300**. Working capital: average inventory rises from about 30 to 60 days of usage because of transit and order cycles → extra ~USD 68,000 of working capital (financing cost ~USD 5,500 a year), still far below the annual saving.

### Risks and decisions

| Risk | Decision / control |
|---|---|
| Supplier fails to ship after advance | Limit advance to 20% (USD 12,240); verify supplier (licences, audits, references) |
| Quality failure | Independent pre-shipment inspection with authority to reject; registration-compliant specs |
| Buyer refuses documents under D/P (from the supplier’s view) | Supplier accepts this risk for a new customer because of the 20% advance |
| Regulatory delay (registration) | No order before registration is approved |
| FX | USD pricing; JOD pegged — low risk |
| Supply concentration | Keep the local supplier for 25% of volume during the first year |

### Failure scenarios and recommended controls

| Scenario | Consequence | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Inspection fails (pinholes above AQL) | Shipment delayed; stock-out risk | Safety stock 4 weeks; contractual replacement within 14 days |
| Documents arrive after goods | Port storage and demurrage | Courier documents promptly; supplier’s bank instructed to send by express |
| Shelf-life below hospital tender requirement | Rejection by customers | Contract minimum shelf life at shipment |
| Container damaged | Loss | Insurance; survey at arrival |

### Outcome and lessons

The first two orders passed inspection; landed cost was within 2% of plan. After three orders, the supplier accepted 100% D/P, reducing the buyer’s prepayment risk to zero. **Lessons:** a balanced payment structure (small advance + D/P) can replace an L/C between new partners; independent inspection is the buyer’s main protection in D/P because banks do not check goods; always compare suppliers on landed cost and include the working-capital effect of longer transit.

## Case 2 — An exporter negotiates a three-year supply contract

**Background.** Nile Ceramics (fictional), an Egyptian tile manufacturer, negotiates a 3-year contract to supply a Saudi building-materials retailer with 1.5 million m² per year — USD 6.0m per year (USD 18m total). The retailer currently buys from Spain and China.

**Participants.** Exporter; Saudi retailer (buyer); exporter’s bank; credit insurer; forwarder and shipping line; Saudi product-conformity bodies; lawyers.

**Commercial objective.** Exporter: secure base-load volume for a new production line with acceptable margin and payment security. Buyer: lower cost than Spanish tiles, quality comparable, reliable supply, and 90-day credit.

**Relevant terminology.** Take-or-pay, price-adjustment formula, open account, credit insurance, receivables financing, exclusivity, performance guarantee, FOB vs CFR, conformity certification, natural FX hedge.

### Negotiation issues and outcomes

| Issue | Buyer’s position | Exporter’s position | Agreed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | USD 3.80/m² CFR Dammam | USD 4.20 | **USD 4.00 CFR**, fixed for 12 months |
| Price adjustment | None | Index to gas and freight | Annual review; adjustment if Egyptian industrial gas price or freight index moves > 8% (shared 50/50) |
| Volume | Flexible | Take-or-pay 1.5m m² | **Minimum 1.2m m²/year** (80%) with shortfall fee USD 0.40/m² |
| Payment | 120 days open account | L/C at sight | **90 days open account**, credit-insured; 10% security via SBLC from buyer’s bank |
| Quality | Spanish standard | Factory standard | Defined grade/tolerances, pre-shipment inspection by buyer’s agent |
| Performance security | 10% performance bond | None | **5% performance guarantee** (URDG 758) |
| Exclusivity | Exporter exclusive to buyer in Saudi Arabia | No restriction | Exclusive for retail channel only; projects open |
| Disputes | Saudi courts | London arbitration | Arbitration in a neutral regional centre; English-language proceedings |

### Financial calculations (year 1)

| Item | USD m |
|---|---|
| Revenue (1.5m m² × 4.00) | 6.00 |
| Production cost (variable USD 2.55/m²; energy 30% of it) | (3.83) |
| Freight Damietta → Dammam (USD 0.32/m²) | (0.48) |
| Export documentation, inspection, conformity | (0.05) |
| Credit insurance (0.35% of sales) | (0.02) |
| Receivables financing: 90 days on 90% of sales via insured factoring at 7% | (0.09) |
| Guarantee fees (5% performance guarantee at 1.2% p.a.) | (0.004) |
| **Contribution** | **1.53 (25.4%)** |
| Fixed costs of new line (incl. depreciation) | (0.90) |
| **Operating profit from the contract** | **0.63** |

**FX:** revenue in USD, about 65% of costs in EGP → a natural long-USD position; EGP depreciation increases margins, while Egyptian inflation raises costs with a lag. The exporter converts USD only as needed for EGP costs and keeps USD for USD-denominated inputs and debt service.

**Energy sensitivity:** a 25% rise in gas cost raises variable cost by 0.30 × 0.25 × 2.55 = USD 0.19/m² → USD 0.29m a year; under the adjustment clause (beyond 8%, shared 50/50), the exporter recovers part from year 2.

### Risks, failure scenarios and controls

| Scenario | Control |
|---|---|
| Buyer insolvency or protracted default | Credit insurance (90% indemnity) + SBLC for 10% |
| Buyer orders below minimum | Shortfall fee; credit insurance covers only invoiced sales |
| Production problems → late deliveries → call on performance guarantee | Buffer stock in Dammam bonded warehouse; realistic delivery windows |
| Gas supply interruptions in summer | Inventory build-up before peak; force majeure clause covering government energy rationing |
| Conformity certificate delays | Start certification before contract signing |

### Outcome and lessons

Contract signed with insured open-account terms; the exporter’s bank financed receivables at 90% because they were credit-insured. **Lessons:** trade multiple variables (price, volume floor, terms, security, adjustment clause); credit insurance can turn open-account terms into bankable assets; long contracts need price-adjustment mechanisms for volatile inputs.

## Case 7 — A manufacturer negotiates with a distributor

**Background.** PureFlow Technologies (fictional), a Saudi manufacturer of water-filtration systems, appoints a distributor in Egypt. Annual target: 8,000 units in year 1, rising to 15,000 in year 3.

**Participants.** Manufacturer; candidate distributor (an Egyptian company with 60 dealers and service technicians); manufacturer’s legal counsel; banks.

**Commercial objective.** Manufacturer: market entry with a committed partner, brand control, payment security. Distributor: exclusivity, competitive price, marketing support, protection of its investment.

**Relevant terminology.** Exclusivity, minimum purchase targets, territory, market development funds (MDF), resale price (competition law), SBLC, stock rotation, termination, after-sales service, registration of the agency/distributor (local law).

### Key terms negotiated

| Term | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Territory and exclusivity | Exclusive for Egypt for 3 years, conditional on minimum purchases (8,000 / 11,000 / 15,000 units) |
| Price | Distributor price USD 140 FCA Jeddah; recommended retail price equivalent ~USD 230 (distributor free to set resale prices) |
| Payment | 60 days open account backed by an SBLC of USD 400,000 from an Egyptian bank (renewed annually) |
| Marketing | MDF of 3% of purchases, matched by distributor; joint annual marketing plan |
| Service | Distributor maintains 20 certified technicians and spare-parts stock for 2 months; manufacturer provides training |
| Stock rotation | Distributor may return up to 5% of annual purchases of slow models |
| Brand | Trademark registered by manufacturer in Egypt; licence to distributor during agreement |
| Termination | For missed targets (after 90-day cure), insolvency, breach; on expiry or termination, manufacturer buys back saleable stock at net cost minus 10% |
| Law | Saudi or English law with arbitration — subject to mandatory Egyptian rules on commercial agency/distribution, which local counsel must check |

### Calculations

**Distributor’s economics (year 1):**

| Item | USD |
|---|---|
| Purchases (8,000 × 140) | 1,120,000 |
| Freight, duties and clearance (assume 18% of FCA value) | 201,600 |
| Landed cost per unit | 165.20 |
| Average net selling price to dealers (dealers earn their own margin) | 205.00 |
| Gross margin per unit | 39.80 (19.4%) |
| Total gross margin | 318,400 |
| Marketing (own share 3%) | (33,600) |
| Service team and spare parts | (120,000) |
| Warehouse, sales staff, admin | (95,000) |
| Financing (SBLC fee 1.5% + working capital) | (24,000) |
| **Distributor operating profit** | **45,800 (≈2.8% of sales)** |

Thin in year 1; the distributor’s case relies on growth to 15,000 units (fixed costs spread) and on service revenue (installations, filter replacements).

**Manufacturer’s economics:** contribution per unit USD 52 (price 140 − variable cost 88) → USD 416,000 in year 1, minus MDF USD 33,600 and support costs USD 60,000 → **USD 322,400**.

### Risks and controls

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Distributor fails to perform but has exclusivity | Targets with termination rights; non-exclusive conversion option |
| Payment default | SBLC covering ~4 months of purchases |
| EGP devaluation hurting distributor’s competitiveness | Joint price review; manufacturer offers temporary price support in exchange for higher volumes |
| Termination dispute (local protective laws) | Local legal advice before signing; clear contractual compensation formula |
| Brand misuse | Trademark owned by manufacturer; quality standards; audit rights |

### Outcome and lessons

Year 1 reached 7,600 units (95% of target); the manufacturer agreed a 90-day cure plan instead of termination. **Lessons:** distribution agreements must make the distributor’s economics viable or it will under-invest; tie exclusivity to performance; payment security and trademark ownership are non-negotiable; local mandatory law on distribution/agency can override contract choices.

## Case 11 — A logistics company calculates the profitability of a shipment

**Background.** Delta Clear Logistics (fictional, Dammam) is asked to quote a door-to-door shipment for an industrial customer: 2 × 40ft high-cube containers of machinery parts from a supplier near Stuttgart, Germany, to the customer’s plant in Riyadh. Terms of sale between supplier and customer: FCA supplier’s premises; Delta Clear acts as the customer’s forwarder.

**Participants.** Forwarder (Delta Clear) and its German agent; trucking company in Germany; shipping line (Hamburg → Dammam); Saudi customs; Delta Clear’s own trucks; customer.

**Commercial objective.** Win the shipment at a competitive price with at least 15% gross margin and no unpriced risks (demurrage, storage).

### Cost build-up (buy rates)

| Leg / item | USD |
|---|---|
| German agent: pickup Stuttgart → Hamburg (2 × 1,150) | 2,300 |
| Export customs documentation (agent) | 180 |
| Origin terminal handling (2 × 260) | 520 |
| Ocean freight Hamburg → Dammam incl. surcharges (2 × 2,450) | 4,900 |
| Destination THC and delivery order | 760 |
| Customs clearance (own brokerage, internal cost) | 250 |
| Trucking Dammam → Riyadh (own trucks, internal cost 2 × 780) | 1,560 |
| Insurance arranged for customer (0.15% on USD 420,000 × 110%) | 693 |
| Agent fee | 150 |
| **Total cost** | **11,313** |

### Pricing and profitability

| | USD |
|---|---|
| Quoted price (all-in, excluding duties and VAT advanced at cost) | 13,300 |
| Gross profit | 1,987 (14.9%) |
| Duty and VAT advanced for customer (5% duty on CIF ~USD 428,000 ≈ 21,400; VAT 15% ≈ 67,400) | Advanced at cost plus 1% disbursement fee = 888 |
| Financing of advances: USD 88,800 for 30 days at 7% | (511) |
| **Gross profit after disbursement economics** | **2,364** |
| Allocated overheads (sales, operations, IT) at USD 450 per container | (900) |
| **Net contribution to profit** | **1,464 (≈ 11%)** |

### Risks and decisions

| Risk | Decision |
|---|---|
| Customer pays at 60 days while Delta pays shipping line at 14 days and customs immediately | Price disbursement fee; credit limit; require duty pre-funding for large amounts |
| Demurrage if customer’s documents (invoice, conformity certificates) are late | Quote states free time (7 days) and that D&D beyond free time caused by customer is re-invoiced at cost |
| Rate increases before booking | Quote valid 14 days; surcharges subject to carrier changes |
| Liability for cargo damage | Delta’s standard trading conditions limit liability; customer advised to accept insurance (included) |
| Heavy machinery overweight for road limits | Verify weights in advance; use specialised trailers if needed |

### Failure scenarios and controls

- Customs reclassifies machinery parts → higher duty and inspection delay: confirm HS code with customer before shipment.
- Carrier rolls the containers to the next vessel: book early; monitor; notify customer immediately.
- Customer disputes invoice due to unclear scope: quotation lists inclusions and exclusions explicitly.

### Outcome and lessons

Delta won the shipment at USD 13,300. A 3-day customs inspection was absorbed within free time. **Lessons:** forwarders must look beyond the freight margin to working capital (duty advances), credit risk and D&D exposure; clear scope and validity in quotations prevent margin leakage; disbursements are a financing service and should be priced.
