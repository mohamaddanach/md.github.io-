Experienced professionals do not know more formulas than good graduates — they see more of the situation. They notice incentives behind statements, alternatives that were not mentioned, information one side has and the other lacks, effects that appear only later, and the difference between a good outcome and a good decision. This part collects the mental models that separate professional judgement from technical competence, each illustrated with business situations and a contrast between how a graduate and an experienced professional typically think.

:::

# Economic Thinking: Opportunity Cost, Incentives, Agency, Information and Transaction Costs

:::obj In this chapter
Seven economic concepts that explain much of business behaviour, each with a business example and the professional’s angle.
:::

## Opportunity cost

The value of the best alternative given up. Every use of cash, capacity, management time or credit lines has an opportunity cost.

:::case Warehouse space
A distributor uses 2,000 m² of its own warehouse to store slow-moving stock worth $400,000 that sells a few units a month. The graduate sees “no cost — we own the warehouse”. The professional sees that the space could store fast-moving products generating $150,000 a year of gross profit, or be leased to a third party for $120,000 a year, and that $400,000 of cash is tied up. Decision: liquidate most of the slow stock at a discount.
:::

## Incentives

People respond to how they are measured and paid. Before trusting a recommendation, ask who benefits.

| Situation | Incentive problem | Professional response |
|---|---|---|
| Salespeople paid commission on revenue | Discounting to close deals; selling to risky customers | Commission on gross margin, paid on collection |
| Buyers rewarded for “savings” vs last price | Choosing cheap but unreliable suppliers | Measure total cost of ownership and OTIF |
| Brokers paid on transaction value | Pushing deals regardless of quality | Independent verification; fee structures aligned with outcomes |
| Managers rewarded on annual EBITDA | Cutting maintenance and training | Balanced scorecard; multi-year metrics |
| Lenders’ relationship managers rewarded on volume | Lending growth over quality | Risk-adjusted metrics; independent credit function |

## Principal–agent problems

An **agent** acts on behalf of a **principal** but may pursue its own interests: managers vs shareholders, employees vs employers, distributors vs manufacturers, fund managers vs investors, contractors vs clients. Tools: alignment of pay (equity, deferred bonuses), monitoring (audits, reporting), governance (independent boards), contracts (performance clauses), and reputation.

## Information asymmetry

One party knows more than the other. Sellers of companies know more than buyers; borrowers know more than lenders; employees know more about their effort than managers.

**Responses:** due diligence, warranties and indemnities, screening (credit analysis), signalling (audited accounts, certifications, guarantees, owner co-investment), and contract design (earn-outs that pay only if performance materialises).

## Moral hazard

After a contract is signed, a protected party may take more risk because someone else bears the consequences: an insured company neglecting fire safety; a borrower with limited liability gambling with the bank’s money when close to insolvency; a distributor with guaranteed margins not pushing sales. **Responses:** deductibles and co-insurance, covenants and security, monitoring, performance-linked terms.

## Adverse selection

Before a contract, the riskiest parties are the most eager to accept standard terms: borrowers who accept any rate may be the weakest; customers most eager for long credit terms may be those who cannot pay; insurance buyers with the worst risks buy the most cover. **Responses:** screening, risk-based pricing, collateral, references, probationary limits.

:::case The “too eager” customer
A new buyer immediately accepts 120-day terms and a large initial order without negotiating price. The graduate is pleased. The professional asks why a buyer would accept without negotiation — and orders a credit check, which reveals two recent payment defaults and a court case. The order proceeds only against a confirmed L/C.
:::

## Transaction costs

The costs of finding partners, negotiating, contracting, monitoring and enforcing. They explain why firms make some things in-house and buy others (Coase, Williamson): when assets are specific, transactions frequent and uncertainty high, firms tend to integrate or use long-term contracts; when goods are standard, markets work well.

:::example Make or buy logistics
A food manufacturer with specialised cold-chain needs, daily deliveries to supermarkets and penalties for late delivery may build its own fleet (high asset specificity and coordination needs) or sign a long-term dedicated contract with a 3PL; a manufacturer of standard packaged goods with flexible schedules may simply buy spot trucking.
:::

المهني المتمرّس يسأل دائماً: ما تكلفة الفرصة البديلة؟ ومن المستفيد من هذه التوصية؟ هل لدى الطرف الآخر معلومات لا نملكها؟ هل سيغيّر الطرف المحمي سلوكه بعد التوقيع (الخطر الأخلاقي)؟ ولماذا يقبل هذا العميل شروطنا بسرعة (الاختيار العكسي)؟ وما تكاليف البحث والتفاوض والمتابعة والتنفيذ؟ هذه الأسئلة تكشف المخاطر التي لا تظهر في الجداول المالية.
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Identify the economic concept: (a) a CEO pursues acquisitions that increase company size and his pay but destroy value; (b) a company with credit insurance relaxes its credit checks; (c) only customers with poor credit apply for your no-deposit financing offer; (d) a manager keeps an unprofitable project because “we’ve already invested $3m”.
2. Design an incentive plan for a distributor that encourages both sales growth and payment discipline.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (a) Principal–agent problem (incentives misaligned with shareholders); (b) moral hazard; (c) adverse selection; (d) sunk-cost fallacy (ignoring opportunity cost; past costs are irrelevant).
2. Rebates based on annual purchases above targets, paid only on invoices settled within terms; additional rebate for on-time payment; reduced rebate or loss of exclusivity for overdue balances; marketing funds tied to sell-out data.
:::

# Strategic Thinking: Positioning, Bargaining Power, Moats, Network Effects, Scale, Scope, Switching Costs and Industry Structure

:::obj In this chapter
How professionals assess where profits are made in an industry and whether a business can defend them.
:::

## Competitive positioning

A business must be clear about **where it plays** (customers, products, geographies, channels) and **how it wins** (lowest cost, distinct value, specialist focus, ecosystem). Being “stuck in the middle” — neither the cheapest nor clearly better — leads to price competition without advantage.

## Bargaining power

Profit is divided between a firm, its customers and its suppliers according to bargaining power.

| Power increases when… | Example |
|---|---|
| Few alternatives exist | A sole-source component supplier |
| Switching is costly | An ERP vendor with years of customer data and customisations |
| The buyer is large relative to the seller | A hypermarket chain buying from small food producers |
| The product is a large share of the buyer’s costs | Steel for a pipe manufacturer (buyer negotiates hard) |
| The seller can integrate forward / buyer backward | A manufacturer threatening to sell directly |
| Information is asymmetric | An expert advisor vs an inexperienced client |

## Economic moats

Durable advantages that protect returns: **intangible assets** (brands, patents, licences — e.g., a scarce port operating concession), **cost advantages** (scale, location, proprietary processes), **switching costs**, **network effects**, and **efficient scale** (markets that support only a few players profitably, such as some regional infrastructure). Ask: how long would it take and how much would it cost a well-funded competitor to replicate this business?

## Network effects

A product becomes more valuable as more people use it. **Direct** (messaging apps), **indirect/two-sided** (marketplaces: more buyers attract more sellers), **data network effects** (more usage improves the product). They create winner-take-most dynamics but are weaker than often claimed when multi-homing is easy (drivers and riders use several apps) or when local networks are separate.

## Economies of scale and scope

**Scale:** unit costs fall with volume (fixed costs spread; purchasing power; specialisation). **Scope:** costs fall when related products share resources (same distribution network carrying several product lines; same kitchen producing multiple delivery brands; a bank cross-selling FX and trade finance to lending clients).

## Switching costs

Financial (exit fees, new investments), procedural (learning, data migration, re-certification), relational (relationships, risk of disruption). High switching costs support pricing power and retention; companies build them through integration, data, training and long-term contracts — and buyers should anticipate them when choosing suppliers (negotiate exit terms upfront).

## Industry structure

Profitability differs by industry structure: concentration, entry barriers, capital intensity, cyclicality, regulation, substitute threats and value-chain position. A strong company in a structurally bad industry often earns less than an average company in a good industry. Porter’s Five Forces and value-chain profit pools help identify where profits concentrate (e.g., in airlines, profits historically concentrated in aircraft manufacturers, lessors and reservation systems more than in airlines).

## Competitive intelligence

Legal and ethical gathering of information about competitors and markets: public filings, websites and job postings, trade data, customer and supplier interviews, trade shows, industry reports, patents, regulatory filings, price monitoring. Never obtain confidential information improperly or exchange competitively sensitive information with competitors (competition-law risk).

:::case Graduate vs professional: entering a market
A logistics company considers launching a same-day delivery service in a Gulf city. **Graduate:** “The market is growing 25% a year; we can capture 5%.” **Professional:** “Who captures the profits? Platforms own the customer relationship and squeeze couriers; riders multi-home; switching costs for merchants are low; scale economies favour the top two players who already have density. Our advantage would be B2B scheduled deliveries for industrial clients we already serve — less glamorous, but defensible.”
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Identify the main moat (if any): (a) a port terminal concession for 30 years; (b) a mobile app for restaurant discounts; (c) a cement plant 300 km from the nearest competitor; (d) an accounting software used by 70% of local accountants.
2. A supplier tells you its product is unique, but you discover two alternatives with 3 months’ qualification time. How does this change your negotiation?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. (a) Licence/concession (intangible) + efficient scale; (b) weak — low switching costs, easy imitation (possibly local network effects); (c) cost/location advantage (transport costs protect local market); (d) network effects (accountants share know-how and formats) and switching costs.
2. Your BATNA improves: you can credibly start qualifying alternatives, negotiate price and terms more firmly, and include supply-security clauses; switching costs (3 months) mean you must start early.
:::

# Decision-Making Under Uncertainty and the Professional Mindset

:::obj In this chapter
Expected value, scenarios, risk-adjusted returns, capital allocation, opportunity evaluation, strategic trade-offs, negotiation leverage and second-order effects — and a consolidated contrast between graduate and professional thinking.
:::

## Expected value — and its limits

Expected value (EV) weighs outcomes by probability. Good decisions maximise EV **subject to survival constraints**: a bet with positive EV that could bankrupt the company is a bad bet. Professionals look at the distribution, the worst case, reversibility, and correlation with other risks.

:::example Two contracts
Contract A: 90% chance of +$1.0m, 10% chance of −$0.5m → EV +$0.85m. Contract B: 70% chance of +$3.0m, 30% chance of −$4.0m → EV +$0.9m. B has higher EV, but a 30% chance of a $4m loss — more than the company’s equity of $3m. A professional chooses A, or restructures B (insurance, risk-sharing, staged commitments).
:::

## Scenario analysis and decision trees

Use scenarios for big decisions: define drivers, construct coherent worlds, assess outcomes, identify robust options and signposts. **Decision trees** structure sequential decisions with uncertainty (e.g., pilot first, then expand if results meet thresholds); the **value of information** is the improvement in expected value from learning before committing — often a reason to run pilots.

:::example Staging reduces risk
Launching nationally costs $5m with a 50% chance of success (value $14m) and 50% of failure (value $0) → EV = 7.0 − 5.0 = **+$2.0m**. A $0.5m pilot reveals success or failure with 90% reliability. If the pilot is positive, launch (expected success probability rises to 90%); if negative, stop.
- Pilot positive (probability 0.5 × 0.9 + 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.5): launch EV = 0.9 × 14 − 5 = 7.6.
- Pilot negative (0.5): stop, value 0.
- EV with pilot = 0.5 × 7.6 − 0.5 = **+$3.3m** > $2.0m. The information from the pilot is worth about $1.8m (3.3 + 0.5 − 2.0) — far more than its $0.5m cost.
:::

## Risk-adjusted returns and capital allocation

Compare opportunities by return **relative to risk and capital**, not by headline profit: RAROC in banking, risk-adjusted NPV with appropriate discount rates, return on capital employed against WACC, payback under stress. Capital allocation choices — reinvest in the core, expand, acquire, pay down debt, pay dividends, buy back shares — should be made against a common hurdle and with discipline (post-investment reviews; stopping projects that fail milestones).

## Evaluating opportunities: a professional checklist

1. What problem or demand does it serve, and how do we know?
2. Why us — what advantage do we bring?
3. What are the unit economics and the capital required?
4. What must be true for this to succeed? Which assumptions are riskiest?
5. What is the downside, and can we survive it?
6. How can we stage commitments and learn cheaply?
7. Who are the counterparties, and what are their incentives?
8. What contracts, guarantees, insurance or hedges protect us?
9. What second-order effects could follow?
10. What is the opportunity cost?

## Strategic trade-offs

Strategy requires choosing what **not** to do: growth vs margin, control vs speed (own vs partner), flexibility vs commitment (spot vs long-term contracts), standardisation vs customisation, liquidity vs return. Making trade-offs explicit avoids incoherent strategies.

## Negotiation leverage

Leverage comes from alternatives (BATNA), time pressure (who needs the deal sooner), information, the other side’s constraints, and the ability to create value (variables they value more than you). Professionals build leverage before negotiating — e.g., qualifying a second supplier before renegotiating with the first.

## Second-order effects

Ask “and then what?”

| First-order effect | Second-order effects |
|---|---|
| Cut prices 10% to win share | Competitors match; margins fall industry-wide; customers expect lower prices permanently |
| Extend customer payment terms to boost sales | Working capital rises; borrowing increases; covenant pressure; riskier customers attracted |
| Cut maintenance to meet EBITDA target | Breakdowns later; lost production; higher repair costs |
| Push suppliers to 120-day terms | Weak suppliers fail or raise prices; supply risk increases |
| Aggressive bonus on revenue | Bad-quality sales; returns; disputes |
| Hedge 100% of forecast FX | Over-hedging if sales fall; losses on hedges without matching exposure |

## Graduate vs professional: a consolidated contrast

| Situation | Typical graduate thinking | Experienced professional thinking |
|---|---|---|
| Big new order | Revenue and margin | Cash cycle, credit risk, capacity, concentration, financing, contract terms |
| Supplier with lowest price | Choose it | Landed cost, reliability, quality, payment security, dual sourcing |
| Profitable company in difficulty | Contradiction | Working capital, debt maturities, covenants, liquidity |
| Investment with high IRR | Approve | Size, risk, assumptions, downside, alternatives, staging |
| Customer accepts terms immediately | Good news | Why? Adverse selection — check credit |
| Market forecast of 25% growth | Use it | Who wrote it, what assumptions, who captures profits |
| A contract clause “standard” | Accept | Who bears what risk, enforceability, interaction with other clauses |
| Successful outcome | Proof of a good decision | Was it skill or luck? Would we make the same decision again? |
| Bad news | Wait for certainty | Communicate early with options |
| Disagreement with a senior colleague | Stay silent or argue emotionally | Present data and options respectfully |

الخريج يركز غالباً على الإيرادات والهامش والعائد المعلن، أما المهني المتمرّس فيسأل عن النقد ومواعيده، والمخاطر وأسوأ الاحتمالات، والحوافز وراء كل اقتراح، والمعلومات الناقصة، والآثار اللاحقة لكل قرار، والبدائل المتاحة. كما يفرّق بين القرار الجيد والنتيجة الجيدة، فقد تكون النتيجة جيدة بسبب الحظ رغم أن القرار كان سيئاً، والعكس صحيح. والهدف من هذه الموسوعة كلها هو بناء هذه الطريقة في التفكير.

:::exercise Practice questions
1. A project has 60% chance of +$2m and 40% chance of −$1.5m. Compute EV. Under what conditions would you still reject it?
2. Name three second-order effects of a bank sharply raising its lending rates to SMEs.
3. Your company can invest $10m in either (a) expanding a proven product line with IRR 16% and low risk, or (b) a new market with IRR 28% in the base case but high uncertainty. How would you decide?
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. EV = 1.2 − 0.6 = **+$0.6m**. Reject if a $1.5m loss threatens liquidity or covenants, if it is highly correlated with other risks the company already holds, if the capital has better risk-adjusted uses, or if the probabilities are unreliable and the downside is irreversible.
2. Weaker SMEs default more (adverse selection and higher burden), better SMEs move to other banks or capital sources, the bank’s loan book shrinks or deteriorates, and suppliers to SMEs face payment delays.
3. Compare risk-adjusted returns (higher discount rate or scenario-weighted NPV for b), downside and survival, strategic value (option to grow), the ability to stage (b) with a pilot, capability fit, and portfolio balance. A common answer: fund (a) fully and stage (b) with a pilot budget and clear milestones.
:::

:::summary Part XXII summary
- Look for opportunity costs, incentives and hidden information in every situation.
- Agency, moral hazard and adverse selection explain many contract terms — design protections deliberately.
- Profitability depends on industry structure and bargaining power; durable moats protect it.
- Decide with expected values, scenarios and staging, subject to survival constraints.
- Always ask “and then what?” — second-order effects separate professionals from beginners.
:::
