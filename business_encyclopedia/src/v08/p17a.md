Knowledge creates value only when it is communicated clearly to the right people at the right time. In business, how you write an email, open a meeting, report a delay or disagree with a senior colleague shapes how others judge your competence. This part teaches the language and behaviour expected in professional environments — especially in international business, banking, trade, consulting, sales and management — with templates, model emails, a large phrasebook with Arabic equivalents, and bilingual dialogues.

:::obj Learning objectives for Part XVII
- Write clear professional emails, commercial and banking correspondence, executive summaries, proposals and reports.
- Run and participate in meetings, calls and presentations effectively; network and introduce yourself professionally.
- Communicate delays, problems, risks and disagreement constructively.
- Use precise professional expressions in English and understand their Arabic equivalents and context.
- Explain complex ideas simply and ask intelligent questions.
:::

# Professional Writing: Emails, Commercial Correspondence, Executive Summaries, Proposals and Reports

:::obj In this chapter
Principles of business writing, email structure, templates for common situations (client, supplier, bank, management), executive summaries, proposals and reports.
:::

## Principles

1. **Lead with the point.** State the purpose, conclusion or request in the first two sentences (the “bottom line up front”).
2. **Write for the reader.** What do they need to know, decide or do? What do they already know?
3. **Be specific.** Numbers, dates, names, documents, deadlines.
4. **Be concise.** Short paragraphs, bullet points for lists, no filler.
5. **Make actions explicit.** Who does what by when.
6. **Be courteous and neutral.** Firm on substance, polite in tone; avoid emotional language and blame.
7. **Check before sending:** recipients, attachments, numbers, names, tone; never send in anger.

## Email structure

| Part | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Subject line | Topic + action/status | “Approval needed: revised L/C terms for PO 3317 by 14 May” |
| Greeting | Appropriate formality | “Dear Mr. Haddad,” / “Hi Sara,” |
| Opening | Purpose in one sentence | “I am writing to request an extension of the latest shipment date under L/C 0451.” |
| Body | Context, key facts, options | Short paragraphs or bullets |
| Request / next steps | What you need, by when | “Could you confirm by Thursday 16 May?” |
| Closing | Courteous sign-off | “Kind regards,” |
| Signature | Name, title, company, contacts | — |

:::warn Common email mistakes
- Vague subject lines (“Question”, “Urgent!!!”).
- Burying the request in the fifth paragraph.
- Long threads where the decision is lost — summarise the current position.
- Copying too many people, or using reply-all unnecessarily.
- Informal language with external senior contacts or banks.
- Commitments made by email without authority (emails can form or modify contracts).
:::

Kind regards,
[Name] · Sales Manager · [Company] · [Phone]

## Executive summaries

An executive summary should allow a senior reader to make a decision without reading further. Structure (often called SCQA or pyramid): **Situation** (context), **Complication** (problem/opportunity), **Question** (what must be decided), **Answer** (recommendation), followed by key supporting points and the decision requested.

:::example Executive summary (fictional)
“**Recommendation:** approve a $2.2m cold-storage warehouse in Dammam. **Why:** we rent third-party cold storage at rising rates ($0.65m/year, +18% in two years) and turn away third-party clients for lack of capacity. The project has an NPV of $0.28m at our 11% hurdle (IRR 15.5%) and payback in 3.7 years. **Key risk:** occupancy of third-party space; mitigated by pre-letting 50% before construction. **Decision requested:** board approval on 20 May to start construction in July.”
:::

## Proposals and reports

Proposals are covered in {{ch:Pricing, Discounting and Commercial Proposals}}. Reports (analysis, due diligence, project close) follow a similar logic: conclusion first, then findings with evidence, then appendices. Charts must have message titles (“Freight costs drove 70% of the margin decline”) and sources.

## Communicating costs and margins

Be precise about bases and definitions: “gross margin 18% on selling price” vs “markup 22% on cost”; “price per tonne CFR”; “all-in cost including fees”. In negotiations with customers, avoid disclosing your margins; discuss value, market references and cost drivers instead (“steel prices rose 12% in the last quarter”).

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Rewrite this subject line: “Re: Re: FW: shipment”.
2. Write a three-sentence email to your bank asking for an increase in your import-loan limit from $2m to $3m, mentioning one reason and attaching documents.
3. Rewrite in a neutral tone: “Your team AGAIN sent wrong documents. This is unacceptable and is costing us money.”
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. “Shipment 4417 (Jebel Ali): customs hold — documents needed by 10:00 tomorrow.”
2. “Dear Mr. Karim, we would like to request an increase in our import-loan limit from USD 2.0m to USD 3.0m, in view of a new 12-month supply contract with a hotel group that increases our purchases by about 40%. Please find attached the signed contract, our latest management accounts and a monthly cash-flow forecast. We would welcome a meeting next week to discuss terms.”
3. “For the third shipment this quarter, the documents we received contained errors (details attached), which led to demurrage charges of USD 1,800. Could we agree a document checklist and a pre-shipment review with your team to prevent this from happening again?”
:::

# Meetings, Calls, Presentations and Networking

:::obj In this chapter
Practical behaviour and language for meetings, telephone and video calls, presentations, introductions and networking; asking good questions; explaining complex ideas.
:::

## Meeting etiquette

| Before | During | After |
|---|---|---|
| Clear purpose and agenda; pre-reads; invite the right people; prepare your points and data | Arrive on time; introductions; agree objective; listen; take notes; contribute concisely; respect hierarchy and cultural norms; avoid side conversations and phones | Send minutes or summary within 24 hours: decisions, actions, owners, deadlines; follow up |

In many Middle Eastern business settings, relationship-building conversation before business, hospitality (coffee, tea), and respect for seniority are customary; meetings may start later or include interruptions; decisions may be confirmed later by the senior decision-maker. Adapt without stereotyping: observe and follow the host’s lead.

## Telephone and video calls

- Identify yourself and the purpose at the start (“This is Rami from ClearPath; I’m calling about the delivery schedule for order 5521.”).
- Check that the timing is convenient.
- Summarise agreements at the end and confirm by email.
- For video calls: test technology, mute when not speaking, look at the camera, share only relevant screens.

## Presentations

1. Start with the message and what you want from the audience.
2. Structure: problem → analysis → options → recommendation → next steps.
3. One message per slide; titles that state conclusions; readable charts; minimal text.
4. Know your numbers and the assumptions behind them.
5. Anticipate questions; prepare backup slides.
6. Respect time; finish early rather than late.

## Introductions and networking

:::dialog Self-introduction at an industry event
“Good evening, I’m Omar Haddad. I work in treasury at Levant Pharma, mainly on FX hedging and bank relationships for our exports to Europe. I saw your presentation on supply-chain finance — I’d be interested in how your platform handles suppliers in countries with currency restrictions. Would you have ten minutes now, or could we arrange a call next week?”
:::

Networking principles: be curious about others; offer value (introductions, information); follow up within 48 hours; maintain relationships over time, not only when you need something; keep a professional online profile.

## Asking intelligent questions

| Purpose | Example question |
|---|---|
| Clarify | “When you say ‘margin’, do you mean gross margin on selling price?” |
| Quantify | “Roughly how many containers were delayed last quarter, and what did that cost?” |
| Understand drivers | “What explains the increase in DSO — specific customers or general payment behaviour?” |
| Test assumptions | “What happens to the NPV if occupancy is 60% instead of 80%?” |
| Explore risk | “What is the worst realistic case, and could we survive it?” |
| Decision process | “Who else needs to approve this, and what will they focus on?” |
| Next steps | “What would you need from us to move forward?” |

## Explaining complex ideas clearly

Use the **headline–explanation–example** pattern; avoid jargon or define it; use analogies; quantify; show a simple picture or table; check understanding (“Does that make sense, or shall I show the numbers?”).

:::example Explaining working capital to a non-finance manager
“Every time we grow sales by $1 million, we need about $250,000 more cash tied up in stock and unpaid invoices before customers pay us. That’s why, even though we’re profitable, the bank overdraft keeps rising. If we collect 15 days faster, we free about $400,000.”
:::

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Write minutes (decisions and actions) for a 30-minute meeting where the team agreed to (a) switch freight forwarder for the Asia lane from July, (b) ask the bank for a facility increase, and (c) review pricing on steel products monthly.
2. Prepare a 30-second introduction of yourself for a meeting with a bank relationship manager.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. “**Decisions:** (1) Asia–Gulf freight will move to Forwarder B from 1 July; (2) the CFO will request a USD 3m increase in the RCF; (3) steel-linked prices will be reviewed monthly from June. **Actions:** Logistics Manager — notify Forwarder A and sign B’s rate agreement by 15 June; CFO — send facility request with forecast to the bank by 7 June; Commercial Director — implement monthly price-review procedure and inform key customers by 31 May. Next review: 20 June.”
2. “Good morning, I’m [name], the new financial analyst at [company], responsible for cash forecasting and working-capital reporting. I’ll be your day-to-day contact for our monthly borrowing-base reports and covenant certificates. I’d like to understand your reporting requirements so we can make them accurate and timely.”
:::

# Difficult Conversations: Delays, Problems, Risks, Disagreement and Negotiation Language

:::obj In this chapter
How to communicate bad news, problems, uncertainty and disagreement professionally — and the language of negotiation and escalation.
:::

## Communicating delays and problems

Principles: **early** (before the other side discovers it), **factual** (what happened, impact), **ownership** (no blame-shifting), **solution-oriented** (what you are doing, options), **specific** (new dates, amounts), and **follow-up** (regular updates).

Structure: (1) the news, (2) the cause briefly, (3) the impact, (4) actions and options, (5) what you need, (6) next update.

## Discussing risks and uncertainty

- Separate facts from estimates: “We know X; we estimate Y; we don’t yet know Z.”
- Use ranges and probabilities: “Delivery is likely between 18 and 22 June; the most likely date is 19 June.”
- Describe triggers and mitigations: “If the vessel misses the window on the 9th, we will switch to air freight for the critical 20%.”
- Avoid false certainty and excessive hedging.

## Handling disagreement professionally

| Situation | Approach | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| You disagree with a senior colleague | Acknowledge, then add data | “I see the logic. One point worries me: the customer’s payment history shows an average of 110 days, not 60. Could we price that in?” |
| A client rejects your recommendation | Understand their concern | “Help me understand what concerns you most — the cost or the implementation timeline?” |
| Conflict between departments | Focus on shared objectives | “We both want to keep this customer; let’s find terms that also protect cash flow.” |
| Someone is wrong in a meeting | Correct facts, not the person | “I think the figure may be from last year; this year’s number is 4.2 million — I can share the source.” |
| You were wrong | Admit quickly and fix | “You’re right — I used the wrong exchange rate. Corrected figures attached.” |

## Escalation

Escalate when an issue exceeds your authority, a deadline is at risk, or agreed processes are not working. Escalate with facts, impact, options and a recommendation — not complaints. “I need to escalate the customs hold on shipment 4417: demurrage is USD 300 per day from tomorrow; the broker needs a revised certificate from the supplier, who has not responded for two days. I recommend that you call their sales director; alternatively we can request a temporary release against a guarantee.”

## Negotiation language

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Opening | “Let’s start by clarifying what each side needs from this agreement.” |
| Anchoring | “Based on current market rates, we were expecting a price in the range of…” |
| Probing | “What is driving the request for 120-day terms?” |
| Conditional offers | “If you can commit to a two-year term, we could look at the price.” |
| Signalling flexibility | “There may be some flexibility on delivery dates.” |
| Holding firm | “I’m afraid that is our best position on price; we may be able to help on payment terms.” |
| Rejecting politely | “I understand the request, but we wouldn’t be able to accept those liability terms.” |
| Summarising | “So, to confirm where we are: price X, terms Y, subject to Z.” |
| Closing | “If we can agree on this, we are ready to sign this week.” |
| Walking away | “It seems we can’t bridge the gap at this stage. Let’s keep in touch if circumstances change.” |

:::exercise Practice questions
1. Your project will exceed budget by 12%. Draft the first four sentences of your message to the sponsor.
2. Rephrase constructively: “That will never work.”
3. Write a conditional concession in response to a customer requesting a 5% discount.
:::

:::answer Answers to the practice questions
1. “I want to flag early that the project is forecast to exceed its budget by 12% (USD 84,000). The main driver is data migration, which required 40% more effort than planned due to duplicate records. We have identified savings of USD 30,000 in later phases and can phase the reporting module to next year to cover the rest. I’d like your approval on that option by Friday.”
2. “I have concerns about how this would work in practice — particularly [issue]. Could we test it on a smaller scale first?”
3. “If you can increase the annual volume to 1,500 tonnes and agree to 30-day payment, we could offer a 3% volume rebate at year-end.”
:::
